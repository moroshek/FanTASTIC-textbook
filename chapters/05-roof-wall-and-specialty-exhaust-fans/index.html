    Roof, Wall and Specialty Exhaust Fans¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter surveys the exhaust and ventilation products that sit on roofs and walls or serve a specific application: kitchen exhaust fans, smoke and heat exhaust fans, fiberglass fans for corrosive service, mancoolers, housed centrifugal and plenum fans, square inline and tubeaxial fans, wall exhausters, gravity relief ventilators, and hooded, louvered, and low-profile roof units. It explains upblast versus downblast discharge and how entrained flow works in lab exhaust. After completing it, readers can match a common building ventilation need to the product line built for it.

    From Fan Families to Product Lines¶

    Chapter 4 sorted fans into three families by the path air takes through the impeller. A Twin City Fan catalog, however, is not organized by family alone. It is organized by the job: a restaurant hood, an atrium that must be cleared of smoke, a wastewater plant full of corrosive vapor, a hot foundry floor. Each job produced a product line whose housing, discharge, materials and listings were shaped around that job's air and that job's mounting location.

    Every Building Has an Exhaust Story

    Walk across almost any commercial roof and you will see a dozen fans, each one chosen for a reason. By the end of this chapter you will be able to read that roof like a parts list and name the product line a customer's job calls for. Let's follow the airflow.

    The chapter moves in four steps. It starts with the four general-purpose fan styles that appear inside buildings and ductwork, moves up to the roof and the wall to compare discharge directions, then covers the application specialists, and finishes with entrained flow, the trick that lets a laboratory exhaust fan dilute and launch its plume.

    General-Purpose Building Fans¶

    Four fan styles do most of the air moving inside a building's duct system and air-handling equipment. Two are centrifugal and differ in whether they have a housing; two sit in line with the duct.

    Housed Centrifugal Fan¶

    A housed centrifugal fan is a centrifugal fan whose impeller turns inside a scroll housing. FanPedia (p. 4) describes the type this way: air is drawn in through one side of the housing (single inlet) or both sides (double inlet) and is discharged at a right angle to the fan shaft. The scroll collects the air thrown off the impeller tips, converts part of its velocity into static pressure, and delivers it through a single rectangular outlet that bolts to a duct.

    Two width-and-inlet combinations cover most catalog listings:

    • Single width, single inlet (SWSI): one inlet and an impeller of standard width, as in the BC-SW.
    • Double width, double inlet (DWDI): two inlets and a double-width impeller, which roughly doubles the volume a given impeller diameter can move, as in the BC-DW.

    The same housed construction accepts backward inclined, airfoil, backward curved, radial tip, forward curved and radial bladed wheels, and it is the base for packaged configurations on FanPedia p. 4: roof-mounted filtered supply units, enclosed duct fans, and the lab exhaust fans covered at the end of this chapter.

    Worked example: what leaves the outlet. A customer's drawing shows an SWSI housed fan delivering 6,000 CFM through an outlet that measures 24 in. by 18 in. How fast does the air leave the scroll (values illustrative, standard air)?

    1. Outlet area: \( A = 2.0 \text{ ft} \times 1.5 \text{ ft} = 3.0 \text{ ft}^2 \).
    2. Outlet velocity: \( V = Q / A = 6{,}000 / 3.0 = 2{,}000 \) fpm.
    3. Velocity pressure at standard air, using the relation from Chapter 2:
    \[ VP = \left( \frac{V}{4005} \right)^2 = \left( \frac{2000}{4005} \right)^2 = 0.249 \text{ in. wg} \]

    where \( VP \) is velocity pressure in inches of water gauge and \( V \) is air velocity in feet per minute.

    A DWDI fan handling the same 6,000 CFM would split it, about 3,000 CFM per inlet. Either way, a housed fan concentrates all of its air into one outlet at a known velocity, so the duct connection there matters. Chapter 12 shows how that one outlet can be pointed in many directions.

    Plenum Fan¶

    A plenum fan is an unhoused centrifugal fan that runs inside an air plenum. FanPedia (p. 5) defines a plenum as an air-filled space within a structure, and the plenum fan's job is to pressurize that space. With no scroll, the impeller discharges air radially in all directions, 360 degrees around the wheel, and the surrounding cabinet does the job a housing would do: it collects the air and sends it to whatever outlets the cabinet has. The fan has an inlet funnel on one side and a frame that carries the impeller, shaft, bearings and drive.

    The current Twin City Fan plenum fan line (E-Series EPF, EPFN, EPQ and EPQN, plus commercial, modular and EC versions) uses 9- or 12-blade airfoil impellers for general HVAC duty: large volumes of clean air at low to moderate pressure. A close relative, the plug fan, mounts its panel on the outside wall of the plenum with the impeller inside; Chapter 6 covers it.

    The Cabinet Is the Housing

    Notice what disappeared when the scroll went away: the fan no longer decides where the air goes, the plenum does. That is why a plenum fan can feed a top opening, two side openings and a bottom opening at once, while a housed fan feeds exactly one outlet.

    Worked example: housed or plenum in an air handler. An air-handling unit manufacturer is laying out a cabinet that must deliver supply air through three duct connections: one on top and one on each side. The unit serves a pharmaceutical cleanroom, and the owner wants as little maintenance in the airstream as possible.

    1. Count the outlets. A housed fan would need a discharge plenum and transitions after its one outlet to feed three connections. A plenum fan pressurizes the cabinet section, so all three openings draw from the same space.
    2. Check the air. Clean air at low to moderate pressure is the duty the airfoil plenum line is built for.
    3. Choose the drive. FanPedia (p. 170) states that direct drive Arrangement 4 plenum fans mount the impeller on the motor shaft, eliminate belt residue and need less maintenance, and are therefore widely used in cleanroom and pharmaceutical applications.
    4. Conclusion. A direct drive plenum fan fits. A housed fan stays the better choice for a single ducted outlet or a free-standing fan. Chapter 11 explains arrangement numbers in full.

    Square Inline Centrifugal Fan¶

    A square inline centrifugal fan puts a centrifugal impeller inside a square housing with removable side panels. FanPedia (p. 6) notes that it can move air straight through the fan or turn it 90 degrees, depending on how the ductwork connects. The square shape lets the fan drop into rectangular duct runs and above-ceiling spaces, and the removable panels give service access without taking the fan out of the duct. Twin City Fan's square inline line includes the belt-driven BSI and direct-drive DSI with backward inclined wheels, plus a DSI mixed flow version. Choose it when the duct runs through a tight space, the air is relatively clean, and the installer wants one compact box that can absorb a duct turn.

    Tubeaxial Fan¶

    A tubeaxial fan is an axial fan in a plain cylindrical housing with no guide vanes. Air enters one end of the tube, passes through the impeller, and leaves the other end moving in the same straight line, carrying some rotational swirl because nothing straightens it. A vaneaxial fan adds a vane section that helps straighten the airflow (FanPedia p. 7). The current catalog positions tubeaxial fans for general ventilation through process air supply, in direct and belt driven models with fixed or adjustable pitch impellers; vaneaxials are aimed at moderate to high pressures.

    The tubeaxial is also the base model for roof-mounted axial fans, which add a hood or a stack cap (FanPedia p. 7), such as the TUB upblast and THB hooded roof ventilators. In a belt-driven tubeaxial (FanPedia p. 15), the motor sits on a motor base saddle outside the tube, and a belt tube, also called a belt fairing, shields the belt where it crosses the airstream.

    Worked example: duct velocity and duty. A contractor needs 8,000 CFM of exhaust from a warehouse process area through a straight, round duct, and asks whether a 24-in. tubeaxial is a sensible starting point (values illustrative).

    1. Tube area: \( A = \pi r^2 = \pi (1.0 \text{ ft})^2 = 3.14 \text{ ft}^2 \).
    2. Air velocity through the tube: \( V = 8{,}000 / 3.14 = 2{,}546 \) fpm, an ordinary duct velocity for general exhaust.
    3. Duty: a short, straight duct at low pressure points to a tubeaxial. A long duct with filters and higher pressure would point to a vaneaxial.
    4. Drive: if the exhaust carries heat or fumes the motor should not breathe, the belt-driven version keeps the motor outside the tube.

    The answer is yes, confirmed against the catalog performance curve (Chapter 10).

    The four general-purpose styles compare as follows.

    Fan style Housing Air path Typical place in a building
    Housed centrifugal Scroll In through the side, out at 90 degrees through one outlet Ducted supply and exhaust, utility duty
    Plenum None; the cabinet acts as the housing In through the funnel, out radially into the plenum Inside air-handling units
    Square inline centrifugal Square box, removable panels Straight through or a 90-degree turn Rectangular duct, above ceilings
    Tubeaxial Plain tube, no vanes Straight through, with swirl Short ducted runs, roof ventilators

    On the Roof and Through the Wall¶

    Most exhaust air ends up leaving through the roof or an exterior wall, and the defining choice there is the direction the air leaves the unit. FanPedia (p. 5) describes the centrifugal power roof ventilator family by that direction. Upblast and side wall configurations exhaust air in a straight line through the fan. Downblast, louvered and hooded configurations move air up through the fan and then deflect it down and outward.

    Upblast Discharge¶

    Upblast discharge sends exhaust air straight up, away from the roof surface. The impeller turns on a vertical shaft, the air rises through the wheel and leaves through the top of the unit, and the plume carries contaminants, heat and grease clear of the roof and of nearby air intakes. Because the outlet faces the sky, upblast units carry a weather defense: FanPedia's technical descriptions (p. 181) note that butterfly-type gravity dampers, built into roof-mounted upblast fans, prevent outside air from back-drafting in and provide weather protection when the fan is off. Twin City Fan's VCU, VCUB and BCRU upblast roof exhausters, and the kitchen and smoke versions covered below, all use this discharge.

    Downblast Discharge¶

    Downblast discharge turns the air leaving the impeller back down toward the roof, out and around the unit. The classic downblast centrifugal roof exhauster has a mushroom-shaped motor cover over a spun aluminum shroud; air rises through the wheel, strikes the inside of the cover, and spills out the bottom edge in all directions. The low silhouette and built-in weather protection suit clean air: restroom, general office and storage exhaust. The current catalog lists the VC and DCRD direct-drive and BCRD belt-driven downblast models. FanPedia's exploded view (p. 19) names the parts common to both roof exhauster types: motor cover, motor housing, motor, impeller, shroud, skirt, bird screen, curb cap and roof curb.

    Downblast Means Clean Air Only

    A common trap is quoting a downblast exhauster for kitchen, fume or hot exhaust because it is cheaper and lower. A downblast unit lays its discharge back onto the roof, where grease collects and fumes find the nearest outdoor-air intake, so for anything but relatively clean air, switch the quote to an upblast unit.

    Hooded, Louvered and Low-Profile Roof Units¶

    Three more roof configurations differ mainly in how they shelter the discharge. The hooded and louvered exhausters share the "up, then down and out" path of the downblast; the low profile ventilator comes in both upblast and hooded forms.

    A hooded roof exhauster is a centrifugal roof exhauster topped by a weatherhood. Air passes up through the impeller and the hood turns it down and outward around the base, giving full rain and snow protection over the opening. The current catalog lists the DCLH (direct drive) and BCLH (belt driven) models. The same hood idea appears on axial roof units, where the hood deflects exhaust downward or, on a supply unit, admits air from underneath.

    A louvered roof exhauster replaces the hood with a louvered penthouse: a box with fixed weather louvers on its sides. Air rises through the impeller into the penthouse and is deflected down and outward through the louvers, which shed rain while keeping the unit's profile boxy and compact. The current catalog lists the DCLP (direct drive) and BCLP (belt driven) models.

    A low profile roof ventilator is a propeller-type roof ventilator built to sit close to the roof deck, for roofs where a tall unit would be visible from the street or would catch the wind. The roof ventilator line offers low-profile versions in both discharge styles: the LUD and LUB upblast units (plus the LUBMO, with its motor out of the airstream) and the LHD and LHB hooded units, some of which add filters for supply duty. Like the rest of the roof ventilator line, these use cast aluminum adjustable-pitch or fabricated steel fixed-pitch axial impellers rather than centrifugal wheels.

    Centrifugal Wall Exhauster¶

    A centrifugal wall exhauster is the side-wall version of the centrifugal roof exhauster: the same backward inclined, non-overloading wheel and motor package, mounted on an exterior wall so the shaft is horizontal and air leaves straight out, away from the building face. It serves buildings without convenient roof access and duct runs that end at a wall. Several Twin City Fan models do double duty on a roof or a wall (VCU and VCUB); the BCRW is a dedicated belt-driven wall unit, the BCRWR is its kitchen version, and the fiberglass line includes the HA and HAB wall exhausters.

    Gravity Relief Ventilator¶

    A gravity relief ventilator has no fan at all. It is a weather-protected roof opening that lets air leave (relief) or enter (intake) a building under natural pressure differences: warm air rising inside the building, wind across the roof, or the slight positive pressure created when supply fans run. Twin City Fan offers them as spun aluminum (GRV), modular hooded (MGR relief, MGI intake), louvered (TEL relief, TIL intake) and fiberglass (MA) units. They are often specified beside powered fans: the supply fan pushes air in and the gravity relief ventilator lets it out without a second motor.

    Cabinet Ventilator¶

    A cabinet ventilator is a small centrifugal blower, usually with a forward curved wheel, packaged in a sheet-metal cabinet that mounts in or above a ceiling, on a wall, or inline in a duct. It is the quiet, continuous-duty fan for bathrooms, small offices, generator rooms and general HVAC exhaust. In Twin City Fan's current catalog the group is called "Ceiling & Cabinet Ventilators" and lists duct blowers (DBS forward curved inline, DBT twin forward curved inline) beside ceiling and inline ventilators (T ceiling ventilator, TL inline/ceiling ventilator). It is a close relative of the ceiling ventilator introduced in Chapter 4: the same quiet, indoor, clean-air duty, but packaged in a cabinet that also mounts on a wall or inline in a duct.

    Application Specialists¶

    The next four product lines are defined less by airflow path than by what is in the air, or by where the people are.

    Kitchen Exhaust Fan¶

    A kitchen exhaust fan removes the hot, moist, smoky and grease-laden air that ovens, stoves, grills and dishwashers produce, and discharges it away from building surfaces. Grease is the deciding factor: it coats any surface it lands on and is a fire hazard, so kitchen fans use upblast or wall discharge that throws the plume clear of the roof, and they carry a safety listing for grease-laden air. Twin City Fan's current catalog lists its kitchen exhaust fans to UL 705 Supplement SC (formerly UL 762) and pairs them with grease collection systems. The line includes the BCRUR upblast roof and BCRWR wall exhausters, the VCU and VCUB roof/wall units, a BCVR kitchen utility set, and QCLBR and QSLR mixed flow inline fans.

    Smoke and Heat Exhaust Fan¶

    A smoke and heat exhaust fan is built to keep running while it moves smoke and hot gas out of a burning building, so occupants can escape and firefighters can enter. Its motor, bearings and materials are selected to survive a temperature that would stop an ordinary fan. Twin City Fan's current catalog rates its smoke and heat fans to withstand a minimum of 500°F for four hours or 1,000°F for 15 minutes. The line spans spun aluminum roof exhausters (BCRUSH), utility sets (BCVSH), mixed flow fans (QCLBSH, QSLSH), roof ventilators (LUBSH, TUBSH) and axial fans (TBSH, TCTSSH, TCVSSH); inline versions can be roof-mounted with a stack cap whose butterfly dampers are held by a fusible link designed to stay open in a fire. High-temperature construction in general is covered in Chapter 21.

    Fiberglass Fan¶

    A fiberglass fan has its housing, and often its impeller, molded from fiberglass-reinforced plastic (FRP) so it can exhaust moisture-laden, corrosive or chemically contaminated air that would eat through carbon steel. The current catalog describes FRP as a more economical answer than stainless steel or other exotic alloys, with molded housings that resist denting and cracking. Options let the resin and surface match the chemistry: vinyl ester resin, fire retardant resin, a synthetic surface veil, static grounding, and a stainless steel shaft. Twin City Fan builds fiberglass versions of nearly every fan style in this chapter, from housed centrifugal (BAFF, BCSF, RBOF) and inline centrifugal (ILCF) to tubeaxial (TAHF, TAMF), roof exhausters (WA, WAB), wall exhausters (HA, HAB) and panel fans (TCWPF). Wastewater treatment, plating and chemical process exhaust are typical jobs.

    Mancooler¶

    A mancooler is an axial fan packaged to blow air directly on workers in a hot industrial space. Twin City Fan's current catalog describes two forms: mounted units on a movable base whose swivel allows a full 360-degree rotation, and stationary units with a predrilled base plate for wall, column, ceiling or floor mounting (TCSM). Both have inlet and outlet screens as standard, fabricated to meet OSHA safety requirements, because the fan runs within reach of people. A mancooler does not exhaust anything; it moves air across the body to speed evaporative and convective cooling at a foundry pour station or a loading dock.

    Ask What Is in the Air First

    Here is a shortcut for any exhaust inquiry: ask "what is in the air?" before "how much air?" Grease points to a listed kitchen fan, fire duty to a smoke and heat fan, corrosive vapor to fiberglass, and clean air leaves every option open.

    Entrained Flow in Laboratory Exhaust¶

    Fume hood exhaust can be toxic, corrosive or explosive, and it must not re-enter the building through a nearby intake. Chapter 4 introduced the lab exhaust fan and its bypass air plenum; this section adds the second dilution mechanism FanPedia names (pp. 4 and 6), entrained flow.

    Four flows need names before the arithmetic:

    • Contaminated air is the exhaust drawn from the fume hoods.
    • Bypass flow is fresh outside air drawn in through the bypass air plenum and mixed with the contaminated air before it reaches the fan.
    • Fan flow is everything that passes through the impeller: contaminated air plus bypass air.
    • Entrained flow is outside air pulled into the discharge plume by the high-velocity jet leaving the fan's nozzle or windband. It never passes through the impeller; the jet drags it along.

    The plume leaving the stack carries the total flow, the fan flow plus the entrained flow:

    \[ Q_{\text{fan}} = Q_{\text{contaminated}} + Q_{\text{bypass}} \]
    \[ Q_{\text{total}} = Q_{\text{fan}} + Q_{\text{entrained}} \]

    where each \( Q \) is a volume flow rate in CFM. Because the contaminant amount stays the same while the air volume grows, the concentration in the plume falls in proportion:

    \[ C_{\text{plume}} = C_{\text{hood}} \times \frac{Q_{\text{contaminated}}}{Q_{\text{total}}} \]

    where \( C_{\text{hood}} \) is the concentration in the hood exhaust and \( C_{\text{plume}} \) is the concentration leaving the stack, in the same units (ppm here).

    Free Dilution Air

    Think of the nozzle as a pump with no moving parts. Bypass air costs fan capacity because it goes through the impeller; entrained air rides along for free because the jet's own momentum pulls it in, adding mass to the plume and helping it climb.

    Worked example: diluting a fume hood plume. A lab exhaust fan serves fume hoods that exhaust 8,000 CFM of contaminated air at an illustrative 100 ppm. The bypass damper admits 2,000 CFM of outside air, and the nozzle entrains 7,000 CFM more (values illustrative; real entrainment is a catalog-rated value for a specific model and nozzle).

    1. Fan flow: \( Q_{\text{fan}} = 8{,}000 + 2{,}000 = 10{,}000 \) CFM. The fan must be selected for this volume.
    2. Total flow: \( Q_{\text{total}} = 10{,}000 + 7{,}000 = 17{,}000 \) CFM leaves the stack.
    3. Plume concentration: \( C_{\text{plume}} = 100 \times 8{,}000 / 17{,}000 = 47.1 \) ppm.
    4. Interpretation: the fan moves 10,000 CFM but the plume carries 17,000 CFM, so entrainment more than halves the hood concentration at no extra fan volume. The larger, faster plume also rises higher, which is why the current catalog stresses discharge velocity and plume height for lab exhaust.

    Twin City Fan's current lab exhaust line includes induced flow mixed flow fans (TVIFE, QIFE), induced flow centrifugal fans (BAIFE, BCIFE), and venturi nozzle fans (QFE, TFE). Chapter 6 returns to bypass flow, plume dilution and lab exhaust total flow.

    You Can Read a Roof Now

    You can now tell an upblast from a downblast at a glance, explain why a plenum fan needs no scroll, and work out how much air a lab exhaust plume really carries. That is the vocabulary behind most of the ventilation inquiries you will ever quote.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • A housed centrifugal fan delivers all its air through one scroll outlet at a right angle to the shaft; a plenum fan has no scroll and pressurizes the cabinet around it, so the cabinet decides where the air goes.
    • A square inline centrifugal fan fits rectangular duct and can turn the air 90 degrees; a tubeaxial fan is a plain-tube axial fan and the base for roof-mounted axial units.
    • Upblast discharge throws air up and away from the roof, for grease, fumes and heat; downblast discharge lays it back over the roof and is for clean air only. Hooded and louvered roof exhausters shelter a down-and-out discharge, and low profile roof ventilators keep axial units close to the deck.
    • A centrifugal wall exhauster discharges straight out from a wall, a gravity relief ventilator moves air with no fan, and a cabinet ventilator is a quiet blower for ceiling or inline duty.
    • Kitchen, smoke and heat, and fiberglass fans are defined by what is in the air: grease, fire, or corrosive vapor. A mancooler blows air on people.
    • Entrained flow joins the lab exhaust plume without passing through the fan, so total flow exceeds fan flow and plume concentration falls in proportion.
