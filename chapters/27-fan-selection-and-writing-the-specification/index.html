    Fan Selection and Writing the Specification¶

    Summary¶

    This final chapter brings the book together. It starts from application requirements (airstream conditions, space constraints, noise limits, and efficiency requirements) and walks through selecting the impeller, arrangement, construction, drive, and accessories, sizing the motor for cold start, and weighing trade-offs between candidates. It then covers the complete fan description, writing a CSI-format specification with a performance clause, submittal data and certified drawings, and specification review. It ends with the capstone: a complete written fan specification for a realistic application.

    Welcome to Fan Selection and the Specification

    Every chapter so far handed you one part of a fan: a family, an impeller, an arrangement, a rotation code, a construction package, an accessory. This chapter is where you put the parts together into one fan that suits one job, and then write it down so precisely that a factory, a contractor and an owner all build, install and accept the same machine. By the end you will have written a complete fan specification of your own. Let's follow the airflow.

    From the Job Site to the Order¶

    Fan selection is the process of turning a described application into one fan: a family, a model and size, an impeller, an arrangement, a drive, a construction package, a motor and a list of accessories, each chosen for a reason that can be stated. It is not the same as reading a performance table. The table answers one question (what speed and power does this size need for this airflow and pressure?), while selection answers all of them, and many of the answers constrain each other. A hot airstream rules out some arrangements; the arrangement decides where the motor sits; the motor position has to agree with the discharge; the discharge comes from the ductwork.

    Because the decisions interact, experienced application engineers work through them in a fixed order and loop back when a later step breaks an earlier one. The order used in this chapter runs:

    1. Gather the application requirements and correct the duty point to standard air.
    2. Choose the fan family and impeller.
    3. Pick a size and speed from the current catalog table.
    4. Choose the arrangement, then the drive.
    5. Choose the construction, then size the motor for the coldest start.
    6. Choose the accessories.
    7. Check the sound data and the dimensions against the limits.
    8. Compare candidates and settle the trade-offs.
    9. Write the complete fan description and the specification, then review it.

    The diagram below lets you check that you know what information each step consumes, which is the skill that tells you where to loop back when something changes.

    Application Requirements¶

    Application requirements are the facts about the job that any correct fan must satisfy: the duty point (airflow and pressure), the air conditions, the airstream contents, the space available, the noise and efficiency limits, the power supply, and the owner's preferences for maintenance and access. They come from the specifying engineer's schedule, the process engineer's data sheet, the plant walk-down, or a phone call with a customer. Most bad selections are not calculation errors; they are a missing requirement that nobody asked about. The four subsections below cover the requirements that most often go missing.

    Airstream Conditions¶

    Airstream conditions are everything about the gas the fan moves except its quantity: temperature (normal, maximum and minimum), elevation or barometric pressure, moisture, and what the gas carries, such as dust, fibers, corrosive vapors, or flammable gases and dusts. Temperature and elevation set the air density, which changes the pressure and power the fan needs (Chapter 2). Contents decide the impeller, the materials, the coatings, the liners and whether spark-resistant construction is required. Ask for the range, not one number: the hottest temperature sets the construction package, while the coldest temperature sets the motor.

    Space Constraints¶

    Space constraints are the physical limits on where the fan can go: the footprint and height available, the location and direction of the connecting ducts, the room needed to remove the impeller or motor, door and aisle widths for rigging the fan in, and the structure that has to carry it. A fixed footprint often decides the arrangement before performance does, since arrangements differ in where the motor sits and how long the assembly runs. Space constraints also drive the discharge position and rotation, because the outlet has to point at the duct (Chapter 12).

    Noise Limits¶

    Noise limits are the maximum sound a fan installation may produce, written by an acoustical consultant, an owner's standard or a workplace regulation. A limit can be stated as sound power at the fan inlet or outlet, as sound pressure at a stated distance or location, as an A-weighted single number, or as a value in each octave band. The form matters: sound power describes the fan as a source and is what a catalog publishes, while sound pressure depends on the room, the duct and the distance (Chapter 25). If a limit is written in sound pressure, someone has to convert the fan's sound power to the listener's location before the two can be compared.

    Efficiency Requirements¶

    Efficiency requirements are the project's rules about how much energy the fan may use: a minimum fan efficiency rating, a rule that the operating point sit near the fan's peak efficiency, a motor efficiency standard, or a cap on brake horsepower. They come from energy codes, utility incentive programs, owner standards and the specifying engineer. Twin City Fan's Model RTF guide specification, for example, requires motors that comply with the Energy Independence and Security Act of 2007 and premium efficiency, inverter-duty motors when a variable frequency drive will control them.

    One widely used fan rating is the fan efficiency grade (FEG), defined by AMCA Standard 205. An FEG is a label that classifies a fan's peak total efficiency, adjusted for impeller diameter, into bands such as FEG 67, 71, 75, 80 and 85, with higher numbers meaning a more efficient aerodynamic design. It rates the fan alone, not the motor or drive, and it says nothing about where on the curve the fan will actually run. For that reason codes that used FEG paired it with a selection rule: ASHRAE Standard 90.1 editions from 2010 through 2016 required FEG 67 or higher and an operating point within 15 percentage points of the fan's peak total efficiency. Newer codes have moved to the fan energy index (FEI, AMCA Standard 208), which rates efficiency at the actual duty point, so check which metric the project's code edition names.

    A High Grade Does Not Mean an Efficient Selection

    A common trap is to accept any size of an FEG 85 fan because the grade is high. The grade describes the fan's best point; a size chosen far to the left or right of that point can still waste power. Check where the duty point falls on the curve, not only the grade on the label.

    The Fan Selection Checklist¶

    Requirements that are not written down get lost between the first phone call and the order. A fan selection checklist is a standing list of every value needed to select, describe and order a fan, filled in for each job before selection starts. The categories it needs follow from the requirements above and from the decisions later in this chapter:

    Category Values to collect
    Duty Airflow (ACFM), static pressure, whether pressure is at actual or standard conditions, any alternate duty points
    Air conditions Normal, maximum and minimum temperature; elevation or barometric pressure; moisture
    Airstream contents Dust type, loading, stickiness and abrasiveness; corrosive vapors; flammable gases or dusts
    Space Footprint, height, inlet and outlet duct directions, service clearances, rigging path
    Noise Limit, its form (sound power or pressure, octave bands or A-weighted) and where it applies
    Efficiency Code or owner requirement (FEG, FEI, motor efficiency), operating hours
    Electrical Voltage, phase, frequency, enclosure, hazardous location classification, VFD or across-the-line start
    Flow control Turndown range, how often reduced flow is needed, damper, inlet vanes or speed control
    Installation Floor, mezzanine, roof or ceiling; isolation; seismic or wind requirements
    Documents Submittal content, certified drawings, tests, schedule

    A checklist is a living document: when a job reveals a requirement you did not ask for, add it to the list for the next job.

    Selecting the Fan¶

    Fan Selection: From Duty Point to Catalog Size¶

    The core of fan selection is finding a size and speed that delivers the required airflow and pressure, and that step depends on a correction you learned in Chapter 2. Catalog tables are published for standard air (0.075 lb/ft³), but the fan sees actual air. At a fixed speed, a fan moves the same volume regardless of density, while the pressure it develops and the power it draws are both proportional to density. To use a standard-air table for hot or high-altitude air, convert the required static pressure to the pressure the fan would develop in standard air:

    \[ SP_{std} = \frac{SP_{act}}{d} \qquad d = \frac{P_b}{29.92} \times \frac{530}{T + 460} \]

    where:

    • \( SP_{std} \) is the static pressure to look up in the standard-air table, in. wg
    • \( SP_{act} \) is the static pressure required at actual conditions, in. wg
    • \( d \) is the density ratio, actual density divided by 0.075 lb/ft³
    • \( P_b \) is the barometric pressure at the site, in. Hg (29.92 at sea level)
    • \( T \) is the airstream temperature, °F

    The airflow is looked up unchanged, in actual cubic feet per minute. The table then gives the RPM, which is correct as read, and the brake horsepower at standard air, which is multiplied by \( d \) to get the power at the hot operating condition.

    Worked example. A dryer exhaust fan must move 20,000 ACFM against 12.0 in. wg static pressure. The gas is 500°F, the plant is at sea level, and the gas carries a moderate load of sticky, abrasive, combustible wood dust. (The duty and the catalog readings below are illustrative values for teaching; a real selection reads the current catalog or Twin City Fan's selection software.)

    1. Density ratio: \( d = 1.00 \times 530 / (500 + 460) = 0.552 \).
    2. Standard-air static pressure: \( SP_{std} = 12.0 / 0.552 = 21.7 \) in. wg.
    3. Read the table at 20,000 CFM and 21.7 in. wg. Suppose it shows a size that needs 1,450 RPM and 96.0 BHP at standard air.
    4. Hot operating power: \( 96.0 \times 0.552 = 53.0 \) BHP.
    5. Check the operating point: it should sit on the stable, right-hand side of the fan curve, clear of the peak pressure region, and near enough to peak efficiency to meet any efficiency requirement.

    The 21.7 in. wg figure matters later: it is the pressure the fan structure must be built for whenever it runs in cold air, and it is the number the construction class is checked against.

    Same Fan, Two Weights of Air

    Notice that this fan has two personalities: hot, it works against 12.0 inches of water and draws 53 BHP, while a cold start at the same 1,450 RPM raises that to 21.7 inches and 96 BHP. Every later decision, from construction class to motor size, has to respect whichever personality is harder on that part.

    Impeller Selection¶

    Impeller selection is choosing the impeller type, and with it the fan family, that the airstream and the duty allow. The trade runs along one line (Chapter 9): airfoil and backward curved impellers give the best efficiency but need clean air or light dust, radial tip impellers handle large volumes of clean or dirty gas with a self-cleaning tip, and radial bladed impellers survive sticky, abrasive or stringy material at the cost of more power. For the dryer exhaust, sticky and abrasive dust rules out the airfoil and backward curved wheels, and the large volume at moderate to high pressure points to a radial tip fan. Twin City Fan's guide specification describes its Model RTF radial tip fan for exactly this kind of service: exhaust from collectors, scrubbers and cyclones, and induced draft on boilers, incinerators and kilns.

    Arrangement Selection¶

    Arrangement selection is choosing the standard arrangement that places the impeller, bearings and motor where the application needs them (Chapter 11). The deciding questions are, in order: Can the bearings and motor be in the airstream? Is the drive belt or direct? Does the assembly fit the space? Does it need to sit on isolators?

    FanPedia supplies the facts that answer those questions for a single width, single inlet centrifugal fan:

    • Arrangement 1 overhangs the impeller with both bearings on a pedestal out of the airstream, "ideal for high temperature or contaminated air," with the motor mounted separately in position W, X, Y or Z (FanPedia p. 162).
    • Arrangement 4 mounts the impeller on the motor shaft, so the motor sits right against the housing; FanPedia's plug fan description notes that high airstream temperatures may limit this arrangement (p. 171).
    • Arrangement 8 extends the Arrangement 1 pedestal to carry the motor, coupled directly to the fan shaft (p. 165).
    • Arrangement 9 is belt drive only, with the motor on a slide base on the side of the bearing pedestal; the unit ships as a complete assembly, and the motor typically goes on the left side for clockwise fans and on the right for counterclockwise fans (p. 165).
    • Arrangement 9F extends the frame for a motor too large for the pedestal, and is not suitable for mounting isolators directly under the fan (p. 166).
    • FanPedia's high temperature construction page lists Arrangements 1, 8, 9, 9F and 10 for high temperature centrifugal fans (p. 110).

    Worked example. Continue with the dryer exhaust. The fan must fit a 10 ft by 8 ft pad on a mezzanine and sit on spring isolators.

    1. Bearings and motor out of the 500°F gas: Arrangement 4 is out. Arrangements 1, 8, 9 and 9F remain.
    2. Isolators required: Arrangement 9F is out, since FanPedia says it cannot take isolators directly and recommends Arrangement 1 on an isolation base instead.
    3. Space: Arrangement 1 needs a separate motor base beside or behind the fan, and Arrangement 8 adds the motor and coupling to the length of the pedestal. Arrangement 9 hangs the motor on the side of the pedestal, which keeps the assembly short and lets it ship complete.
    4. Result: Arrangement 9, provided the catalog confirms the motor frame fits the pedestal (the motor is sized below). If it does not, the fallback is Arrangement 1 on a common isolation base, and the dimension check has to prove that still fits the pad.

    Drive Selection¶

    Drive selection is choosing how the motor turns the impeller so the fan runs at the speed the table requires: through V-belts and sheaves, through a flexible coupling, or with the impeller on the motor shaft, with or without a variable frequency drive (VFD). A belt drive can produce any fan speed and can be changed later with a new sheave, which is why FanPedia says of belt-driven Arrangement 9 axial and inline fans that "the exact point of rating can be easily achieved" (p. 173). A direct drive removes belts, belt dust and belt maintenance, but without a VFD the fan turns at motor speed.

    Worked example. The selection needs 1,450 RPM. Four-pole and six-pole motors on 60 Hz power run near 1,780 and 1,180 RPM at full load. The fan laws from Chapter 25 show what happens if the fan is simply coupled to one of them on the same system:

    Choice Speed ratio Airflow (ACFM) Hot BHP Verdict
    Belt drive at 1,450 RPM 1.00 20,000 53.0 Meets the duty
    Direct, 4-pole, 1,780 RPM 1.23 about 24,550 about 98 (×1.85) 23% too much air, almost twice the power
    Direct, 6-pole, 1,180 RPM 0.81 about 16,280 about 28.6 (×0.54) 19% short of the duty
    Direct, 4-pole with VFD set to 1,450 RPM 1.00 20,000 53.0 plus VFD losses Meets the duty

    Airflow scales with the speed ratio, and power with its cube: \( (1{,}780/1{,}450)^3 = 1.85 \). Either the belt drive or the VFD meets the duty. The belt drive matches the Arrangement 9 choice and keeps the speed change cheap; the VFD option would require a direct-drive arrangement such as Arrangement 8, a longer assembly, but it removes belts from a dusty, hot area and gives speed turndown. For this job, Arrangement 9 with a belt drive is chosen, and the dust and heat around the belts become an accessory question (a belt guard and a maintenance plan).

    Run the Fan Laws Before You Say Direct Drive

    Here's a quick sanity check: divide the nearest motor speed by the required fan speed and cube it. If the result is far from 1.00 and there is no VFD, a direct-drive selection is either starving the system or paying for power it does not need.

    Construction Selection¶

    Construction selection is choosing how the fan is built to survive its duty: the pressure and speed class, the materials and thicknesses, the temperature package, any spark-resistant construction, abrasion protection and coatings (Chapter 20 and Chapter 21). Each choice is checked against the harshest condition the fan will meet, not the normal one.

    Worked example. The dryer exhaust fan, checked item by item against Twin City Fan's Model RTF guide specification (Section 23 34 16) and FanPedia:

    1. Pressure class. The guide specification lists RTF Class 18 at a maximum tip speed of 18,000 ft/min and static pressure up to 24 in. wg, and Class 23 at 23,000 ft/min and up to 36 in. wg. The cold-start pressure of 21.7 in. wg is under 24, so Class 18 is the first candidate. Tip speed confirms it: for an illustrative 40.25 in. wheel at 1,450 RPM, \( \pi \times 40.25 \times 1{,}450 / 12 = 15{,}280 \) ft/min, under 18,000. Because metal loses strength when hot, also confirm in the catalog that the class's maximum speed at 500°F still covers 1,450 RPM.
    2. Temperature package. The guide specification's 500°F tier calls for high temperature grease and bearings, a shaft seal and a shaft cooler. FanPedia's high temperature packages are grouped by range, with 301°F to 500°F as the first (p. 110), and it recommends shaft coolers above 300°F (p. 192).
    3. Spark resistance. The dust is combustible, so spark-resistant construction is needed. The guide specification limits AMCA Types A and B to 250°F, which leaves Type C, offered for 500°F or 800°F. FanPedia describes Type C as reducing the chance of contact between rotating and stationary parts, typically with an aluminum inlet cone and rub plate, and requiring a steel funnel with a rubbing band and bars at high temperature (p. 193).
    4. Abrasion. The guide specification offers a 7-gauge steel scroll liner and side liner, welded in place, and states that bolt-on liners are not acceptable.

    Many Rules at Once Is the Normal Feeling Here

    If juggling temperature, pressure, dust and spark resistance at once feels heavy, that is normal: this is where every earlier chapter lands on one fan. Take the harshest condition for each part, one part at a time, exactly as the worked example does, and write down the source for each answer as you go.

    Cold Start Motor Sizing¶

    Cold start motor sizing is choosing a motor large enough for the brake horsepower the fan draws when it starts in air colder, and therefore denser, than its design air. A hot-gas fan selected for 53.0 BHP at 500°F will draw the standard-air 96.0 BHP if it starts at 70°F at the same 1,450 RPM, because power rises with density. A 60 HP motor sized from the hot rating would overload on every cold start. The two usual answers are to size the motor for the cold condition (here, a 100 HP motor, which also covers belt losses of a few percent) or to limit the cold-start load with a closed damper or reduced VFD speed until the gas is hot, which the specification must state as a required startup sequence. The guide specification adds a general rule: the driven load should not require the motor to run in its service factor range above 1.0.

    Accessory Selection¶

    Accessory selection is adding the optional parts the application needs, each tied to a stated requirement. The most reliable method is to walk the checklist one row at a time and ask what each requirement demands, then confirm each accessory against the catalog option list.

    Isolators Without Flex Connectors

    Watch out for a specification that puts the fan on springs but leaves the duct connections rigid. FanPedia states that flex connectors are required on all isolated fans; without them the duct either restrains the isolators or cracks. Add both, and say who furnishes the connectors.

    Proving the Selection: Sound and Dimension Data¶

    Octave Band Sound Data¶

    Octave band sound data is a fan's sound power level reported separately in each of eight frequency bands, centered at 63, 125, 250, 500, 1,000, 2,000, 4,000 and 8,000 Hz. Twin City Fan's Model RTF guide specification asks for exactly this in the submittal: "fan sound power levels in eight octave bands and A-weighted overall sound power level or sone values," tested to AMCA Standard 300. Band data matters because noise control works band by band: a silencer, a wall or a duct lining attenuates some frequencies far better than others, and the human ear weighs them differently.

    To compare band data with an A-weighted limit, add the standard A-weighting correction to each band and then add the bands logarithmically:

    \[ L_{WA} = 10 \log_{10} \sum_{i=1}^{8} 10^{(L_{W,i} + A_i)/10} \]

    where:

    • \( L_{WA} \) is the overall A-weighted sound power level, dB
    • \( L_{W,i} \) is the sound power level in octave band \( i \), dB
    • \( A_i \) is the A-weighting correction for band \( i \), dB

    Worked example. The owner limits outlet sound power to 95 dB A-weighted. The selected fan's outlet sound power (illustrative values) is shown with the A-weighting corrections:

    Band center (Hz) 63 125 250 500 1,000 2,000 4,000 8,000
    \( L_W \), dB 98 96 94 90 87 83 78 72
    A-weighting, dB −26.2 −16.1 −8.6 −3.2 0 +1.2 +1.0 −1.1
    A-weighted, dB 71.8 79.9 85.4 86.8 87.0 84.2 79.0 70.9

    Adding the eight A-weighted bands logarithmically gives 92.5 dB, which meets the 95 dB limit. Adding the unweighted bands gives 101.6 dB, a number that would wrongly appear to fail. Notice also which bands control the answer: the 63 Hz band is the loudest unweighted, but after weighting the 500 and 1,000 Hz bands dominate, so those are the bands a silencer would need to attack if the limit were lower.

    Dimensional Data Table¶

    A dimensional data table is the catalog table that gives the outside dimensions, mounting dimensions, inlet and outlet sizes and approximate weights of each fan size, keyed by letters to a dimension drawing. Each letter (A, B, C and so on) labels one distance on the drawing, and each row gives the values for one size and often one arrangement. The table is the tool for checking space constraints during selection; it is approximate, and the catalog normally says so, which is why certified drawings exist (below).

    Worked example. Will the Arrangement 9 dryer exhaust fan fit its 10 ft by 8 ft (120 in. by 96 in.) pad? The dimension letters and values below are illustrative; real ones come from the current catalog page for the selected size and arrangement.

    Letter What it measures Value (illustrative)
    A Overall height, floor to top of housing 94 in.
    C Overall length, inlet face to back of pedestal 86 in.
    D Overall width, including side-mounted motor and belt guard 90 in.
    E Floor to centerline of outlet 70 in.
    1. Length: 86 in. against 120 in. leaves 34 in., enough for the inlet duct connection and its flex connector.
    2. Width: 90 in. against 96 in. leaves 6 in. in total, and the isolation base usually extends beyond the fan feet. That is too tight to pull the motor or change belts.
    3. Height and outlet: the outlet centerline at 70 in. has to line up with the existing duct; a mismatch means a transition piece.
    4. Action: ask whether the motor can move to the other side (which for Arrangement 9 means checking the rotation), or move the fan so the motor side faces open floor, and get a certified drawing before releasing the order.

    Draw the Clearance, Not Just the Fan

    Want to save yourself a field change? Sketch the pad at scale and draw the fan's dimension box plus the space needed to pull the motor, open the access door and change the belts. If the clearance boxes overlap a wall, the fan does not fit, even when its own dimensions do.

    Comparing Candidates¶

    Selection Trade-Offs¶

    Selection trade-offs are the conflicts between good properties that no single fan can have at once, and that the selection has to settle on purpose. The common ones are efficiency against tolerance for dirty air (the impeller line from airfoil to radial bladed), first cost against operating cost (a larger, slower fan usually costs more to buy and less to run), belt drive flexibility against direct drive maintenance, compact arrangements against service access and isolation, and margin against oversizing (a fan selected with too much margin runs away from its best efficiency). A trade-off is settled well when the reason is written down and traceable to a requirement.

    Fan Comparison¶

    A fan comparison is a side-by-side table of two or three candidate fans for the same duty point, built so a customer or a reviewer can see why one was chosen. It lists the same attributes for each candidate: performance (RPM, brake horsepower, efficiency, sound), construction (class, materials, temperature package), arrangement and drive, accessories, and the risks each candidate carries. For the dryer exhaust, the impeller types from Chapter 9 give three candidates:

    Attribute RTF radial tip RBO open radial paddle HIB backward curved
    Airstream tolerance Clean or dirty gas; self-cleaning radial tip Coarse, sticky, heavy or abrasive material Clean air or light dust loading
    Relative efficiency at this duty Higher than radial bladed at high airflow Lowest of the three Highest of the three
    Risk on this job Erosion of blades by abrasive dust; liners help Highest power and motor cost Dust buildup and erosion on curved blades
    Verdict Selected Fallback if the dust proves stickier than expected Rejected for the airstream

    The table does not replace the numbers: a real comparison adds the RPM, brake horsepower, sound and dimensions for each candidate from the current catalog.

    Describing and Specifying the Fan¶

    Complete Fan Description¶

    A complete fan description is the compact, ordered statement of every attribute the factory needs to build one fan: quantity, manufacturer and model, size, class, arrangement, rotation and discharge, motor position, drive, motor, construction and accessories, together with the duty point and air conditions. It is what goes on a quote line, a fan schedule or an order, and it is complete when two people reading it would build the same fan.

    Worked example. The dryer exhaust fan, as one description:

    One (1) Twin City Fan Model RTF radial tip fan, Class 18, size per selection, Arrangement 9, belt driven, CW THD (CW 90), motor on left side of pedestal. Duty: 20,000 ACFM at 12.0 in. wg static pressure, 500°F, sea level (0.0414 lb/ft³). Motor: 100 HP, TEFC, 460 V, 3 phase, 60 Hz, sized for cold start at 70°F. Construction: high temperature package to 500°F with shaft seal and shaft cooler; AMCA Type C spark-resistant construction; welded 7-gauge scroll and side liners. Accessories: raised bolted access door; scroll drain with 3/4 in. NPT plug; parallel blade outlet damper with electric actuator, rated 301°F to 600°F; OSHA belt guard; isolation base with spring isolators; inlet and outlet flex connectors rated for 500°F.

    To check a description, break it into its parts and check each pair that interacts: the rotation against the motor side (FanPedia puts the Arrangement 9 motor on the left for clockwise fans, p. 165), the discharge against the duct direction, the spark-resistance type against the temperature, the motor against the cold-start load, and the isolators against the flex connectors.

    Annotated Fan Drawing¶

    An annotated fan drawing is a drawing of the fan, usually a simple side and drive-side view, marked with the attributes that set its orientation and layout: the arrangement, the rotation arrow seen from the drive side, the discharge position, the motor position, the inlet and outlet connections, and callouts for accessories such as access doors, drains and the shaft cooler. It is the picture that goes with the complete fan description, and it catches errors words miss, such as a motor drawn on the side where the outlet duct runs. When you produce one, view it from the drive side, label the rotation and discharge in both letter and angle form, and dimension the clearances the space check depends on.

    Fan Specification¶

    A fan specification is the written document that tells bidders what the fan must do and how it must be built, tested, documented and installed, so that any compliant product is acceptable and any non-compliant one can be rejected. Unlike the complete fan description, which describes one fan from one manufacturer, a specification describes requirements, names a basis of design and may allow comparable products. It must be specific enough to exclude a fan that would fail and open enough to allow fair bidding.

    Worked example. One requirement written three ways shows what a specification sentence has to do:

    1. "Fan shall be suitable for high temperature." This cannot be enforced: no temperature, no construction.
    2. "Fan shall be suitable for 500°F." Better, but a bidder can still omit the shaft cooler and argue the fan is "suitable."
    3. "Maximum operating temperature: 500°F (260°C). Provide high temperature grease and bearings, shaft seal and shaft cooler." This follows the Twin City Fan guide specification's wording, states the condition and the required features, and can be checked against a submittal line by line.

    The test for every sentence is the same: could a reviewer prove from the submittal that the requirement is met or not met?

    CSI Format Specification¶

    A CSI format specification is a specification organized by the Construction Specifications Institute's MasterFormat, the numbering system used for construction project manuals in North America. Fans fall in Division 23 (heating, ventilating and air conditioning); Twin City Fan's published guide specifications use sections such as 23 34 13 for axial HVAC fans, 23 34 16 for centrifugal HVAC fans and industrial fans, and 23 34 23 for HVAC power ventilators. Every section follows the same three-part structure:

    • Part 1, General: summary, reference standards (AMCA 204, 205, 210, 211 and 300; ABMA 9 and 11; NEMA MG 1; NFPA 70), submittals, quality assurance, coordination, delivery and warranty.
    • Part 2, Products: manufacturer and basis of design, performance requirements, then the fan itself: impeller, shaft, bearings, housing, drive, motor, construction options and accessories, and source quality control such as factory balance and run tests.
    • Part 3, Execution: examination, installation, field quality control, adjusting and cleaning.

    Twin City Fan publishes these guide specifications in CSI format on tcf.com, with bracketed options and "Specifier" notes that tell the writer which paragraphs to keep for a given drive, arrangement or temperature.

    Specification Performance Clause¶

    The specification performance clause is the paragraph in Part 2 that states what the fan must deliver and how its ratings must be proven. A complete clause states the airflow and pressure, the air temperature and elevation (or density) at which they apply, whether the ratings are for site elevation or sea level, the rating standards, and any limit on power, sound or efficiency. For the dryer exhaust fan:

    Fan shall deliver 20,000 ACFM at 12.0 in. wg static pressure with gas at 500°F at sea level (0.0414 lb/ft³). Performance shall be rated in accordance with AMCA Standard 210 and certified under AMCA Standard 211, and fan efficiency shall be classified under AMCA Standard 205. Motor shall be sized for the brake horsepower at a 70°F start without operating in the service factor. Outlet sound power shall not exceed 95 dB A-weighted, tested in accordance with AMCA Standard 300 and reported in eight octave bands.

    Before keeping the AMCA certification sentences, check that the selected size is eligible: the RTF guide specification itself notes that some, but not all, fans in that series are eligible for the AMCA seal.

    Submittal Data¶

    Submittal data is the package of documents the manufacturer or contractor sends for the engineer's review before the fan is released for manufacture, so the engineer can confirm it meets the specification. Twin City Fan's Model RTF guide specification lists the action submittals: rated capacities and operating characteristics, fan performance curves with flow, static pressure and horsepower, sound power in eight octave bands with an A-weighted or sone value, motor ratings and electrical characteristics, specialty components and accessories, and dimensioned drawings with weights and attachments. Informational submittals (quality-control reports, an ISO 9001 certificate) and closeout submittals (operation and maintenance data) follow later.

    Certified Drawings¶

    Certified drawings are drawings the manufacturer produces for a specific order and certifies as accurate for the fan being built, unlike catalog dimension drawings, which are typical and approximate. They show the overall and mounting dimensions, anchor bolt locations, inlet and outlet connections, weights, the rotation, discharge and motor position as ordered, and the accessories in their actual locations. Contractors set anchor bolts, cut openings and fabricate duct transitions from them, so the dimension check in this chapter's worked example ends with "get a certified drawing before releasing the order."

    Specification Review¶

    Specification review is the check of a specification, or of a submittal against one, for completeness and internal consistency before it is issued or approved. A reviewer reads every clause with the selection checklist beside it and asks two questions: is every requirement stated so it can be proven, and does any clause contradict another or contradict a source? The common findings are a duty point with no air condition, a construction option that cannot meet the temperature, a motor sized for the hot condition, missing accessories that another clause requires, and sound data in the wrong form.

    Review the Pairs, Not Only the Lines

    Most specification errors are not wrong lines; they are two right-looking lines that cannot both be true, such as Type B spark resistance and a 500°F airstream. Think of the review as checking the edges between clauses, the same way you checked rotation against motor side.

    Capstone: Writing Your Fan Specification¶

    The capstone fan specification is the final project of this course: a complete written specification, with the reasoning for each choice, for a realistic application. You choose the fan family, impeller, arrangement, drive, construction, motor and accessories from the current catalog, apply the density and cold-start corrections, check sound and space, and present the result as a complete fan description, a CSI-format specification section built from the matching Twin City Fan guide specification, a performance clause, an annotated drawing and a short list of submittals you will require. Use the dryer exhaust fan in this chapter as your model, then write a specification for a different application of your own or one your instructor assigns.

    A capstone submission is complete when it contains:

    1. The filled-in selection checklist for the application.
    2. The duty point corrected to standard air, with the density ratio shown.
    3. Each selection decision (impeller, arrangement, drive, construction, motor, accessories) with one sentence of reasoning and its source.
    4. A fan comparison of at least two candidates.
    5. The complete fan description and an annotated drawing viewed from the drive side.
    6. The specification section in three parts, including the performance clause and the submittal list.
    7. A self-review: the pairs you checked for consistency and what you changed as a result.

    V9 Temperature package is None or to 300°F The gas is 500°F; the 500°F tier needs high temperature grease and bearings, shaft seal and shaft cooler.
    V10 Spark resistance is None Combustible dust requires spark-resistant construction.
    V11 Spark resistance is Type A or Type B Types A and B are limited to 250°F; use Type C.
    V12 Abrasion protection is None or bolt-on Abrasive dust needs a liner, and the guide specification does not accept bolt-on liners.
    V13 Flow control is None and drive is not the VFD option The process needs turndown; add an outlet damper or use the VFD.
    V14 Flow control is nested inlet vanes Nested vanes suit clean air; dirty airstreams call for external vanes or an outlet damper.
    V15 Attribute 10 is unitary base or isolators without flex connectors The fan must be isolated, and FanPedia requires flex connectors on all isolated fans.

    From Vocabulary to a Specification You Can Sign

    You started this book learning what a fan is and what CFM means, and you have just corrected a hot duty to standard air, chosen an impeller, an arrangement, a drive, a construction package, a cold-start motor and every accessory, and written them into a specification a factory can build and a reviewer can check. That is the whole job of describing a fan precisely, and it is the last thing this book set out to teach you. Congratulations on finishing FanPedia.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Fan selection is a sequence of linked decisions: requirements, duty correction, impeller, size, arrangement, drive, construction, motor, accessories, then sound and space checks. A later failure sends you back to the step that caused it.
    • Collect requirements with a selection checklist: airstream conditions (temperature range, elevation, contents), space constraints, noise limits and efficiency requirements are the ones most often missed.
    • Read standard-air tables by dividing the actual static pressure by the density ratio; RPM is read directly and brake horsepower is multiplied back by the density ratio.
    • Size the motor for the coldest start, or specify a startup sequence (closed damper or reduced VFD speed) that limits the cold-start load.
    • Choose the arrangement from the bearing and motor location, the drive, the space and the isolation; choose the drive by checking the fan laws against real motor speeds.
    • Check construction against the harshest condition: cold-start pressure for the class, maximum temperature for the package, and temperature limits on spark-resistance types.
    • Tie every accessory to a requirement, and pair isolators with flex connectors.
    • Octave band sound data is compared with an A-weighted limit only after weighting each band and adding logarithmically; dimensional data tables are approximate, and certified drawings confirm the fit.
    • A fan efficiency grade rates the fan's peak efficiency, not the selected operating point; check which metric (FEG or FEI) the project's code names.
    • The complete fan description states one fan; the CSI-format specification (General, Products, Execution) states requirements, with a performance clause that names the air condition and the rating standards, and a submittal list that lets a reviewer prove compliance.
    • Review a specification by checking pairs of clauses that interact, and finish with the capstone: a complete written specification with the reasoning for every choice.
