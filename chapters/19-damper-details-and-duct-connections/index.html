    Damper Details and Duct Connections¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter adds detail to dampers and duct connections. It covers ceiling and wall gravity dampers, flat and airfoil blades, blade flutter, damper actuators and throttling, blast gates and slide gate dampers, and lab exhaust isolation and bypass dampers. It also covers nested inlet vanes and pre-spin, inlet venturis, free-standing and bolt-on inlet boxes and inlet box horns, flexible connectors, tube adapters, bolted flange and slip connections, and the ASA pipe flange bolt pattern. After completing it, readers can specify how a fan connects to its ductwork and controls.

    Where the Fan Meets the Building

    A fan rarely fails on paper. It fails at the damper that flutters, the inlet box that will not line up, or the rigid joint that carries vibration into the ductwork. This chapter gives you the details that decide whether an order installs cleanly. Let's follow the airflow.

    Chapter 18 introduced dampers, inlet vanes, inlet boxes and companion flanges as categories. This chapter goes one level deeper, to the details that appear on an order or a submittal drawing: the type of gravity damper, the blade profile, the actuator, the particular throttling device a pressure blower takes, the inlet box style, and how the fan physically joins the ductwork. The FanPedia accessory pages (pp. 125–145) and technical descriptions (pp. 180–194) are the sources for the product statements below.

    Gravity Dampers: Ceiling and Wall Types¶

    A gravity damper opens when airflow pushes on its blades and closes under its own weight when the fan stops. It needs no power and no controls, which makes it the default backdraft device on small commercial exhaust fans. FanPedia (p. 136) shows three commercial-grade gravity dampers: the butterfly type built into roof-mounted upblast fans, covered in Chapter 18, and two more that matter here.

    A ceiling type gravity damper is a parallel blade, end-pivoted damper that opens automatically when the fan is energized and closes by gravity when power is turned off (FanPedia p. 181). Its job is to stop air from flowing backward through the system while also keeping out rain and snow. As the name says, it goes with fans and openings in a ceiling, where the damper sits in a horizontal plane.

    A wall type gravity damper has the same technical description, word for word: parallel blades, end pivots, opening on fan start, closing by gravity at fan stop, blocking backflow and weather (p. 181). The difference is the mounting plane. A wall type damper serves a fan or opening in a vertical wall, so its blades hang closed rather than lying closed. When you quote either one, confirm the mounting orientation with the customer, because the two are not interchangeable in the field.

    The three gravity damper types compare as follows.

    Gravity damper Blade style Where it is used What it prevents
    Butterfly type Butterfly, integral to the fan Roof-mounted upblast fans Backdraft of outside air; weather when the fan is off
    Ceiling type Parallel blade, end-pivoted Ceiling openings (horizontal plane) Backflow through the system; rain and snow
    Wall type Parallel blade, end-pivoted Wall openings (vertical plane) Backflow through the system; rain and snow

    Damper Blades: Flat or Airfoil, and the Flutter Problem¶

    Industrial outlet dampers come in two blade types (FanPedia p. 137). A flat blade damper (Type 1) uses plain flat blades. It is the simpler, lower-cost construction and is common on parallel and opposed blade outlet dampers where the damper mostly sits at a few fixed settings.

    An airfoil blade damper (Type 2) uses blades with a streamlined, wing-like cross section. In general engineering terms, the airfoil shape lowers the pressure drop across the open damper, reduces turbulence and noise, and makes each blade stiffer for its weight. FanPedia shows parallel and opposed blade outlet dampers in both types, but the parallel blade inlet box damper is offered as Type 2 only, so a quote that pairs an inlet box damper with flat blades is a configuration error to catch early.

    Blade flutter is a rapid, uneven oscillation of damper blades that are only partly open. It happens when the airflow alone is holding the blades in position. FanPedia's description of motorized dampers (p. 182) names the cause: in low-flow conditions, the velocity pressure of the airstream may only partially open automatic damper blades, and the result is blade flutter and potentially more noise. Flutter also wears pivots and bearings over time.

    The cure for flutter is to stop relying on the airstream. A damper actuator is a powered operator, electric or pneumatic, that drives the damper blades to a commanded position instead of letting the air position them. FanPedia shows motorized commercial dampers with an actuator at the center pivot or at the end pivot (p. 136), and industrial outlet dampers with either a manual handle or an actuator (p. 137). Inlet vanes, blast gates and lab exhaust dampers can also be furnished with actuators, which is how any of these devices joins an automatic control loop.

    A Low-Flow Gravity Damper Will Chatter

    A common trap is putting an airflow-opened damper on a fan that will spend long hours at low flow, such as one on a VFD. The blades hover half-open and flutter. If the fan will run well below full flow, quote a motorized damper with an actuator so the blades are driven fully open.

    Damper Throttling¶

    Damper throttling is controlling a fan's airflow by partly closing a damper so that it adds resistance to the system. The fan does not change. The system curve does: closing the damper makes the system steeper, so it crosses the fan curve at a lower airflow and a higher pressure. FanPedia describes this for outlet dampers as "shifting the operating point to the left of the rating point," and notes that the horsepower savings depend on the position on the fan curve and are usually much less than with other methods (pp. 182–183). Throttling is still the least expensive control and is a sensible choice when reduced capacity is needed only occasionally, or when the air is hot, humid or dirty.

    The system curve itself is the familiar square law from Chapter 10: the pressure needed to push air through a fixed system rises with the square of the airflow. Throttling multiplies the system's resistance coefficient.

    \[ P_{sys} = m \, k \, Q^2 \]

    where:

    • \( P_{sys} \) is the static pressure the system needs, in in. wg
    • \( Q \) is the airflow, in CFM
    • \( k \) is the resistance coefficient of the system with the damper wide open
    • \( m \) is the resistance multiplier added by the damper (1.0 when wide open, larger as it closes)

    Blast Gates and Slide Gate Dampers¶

    Pressure blowers and other small high-pressure fans usually discharge into pipe rather than sheet-metal duct, and they have their own throttling devices.

    A blast gate is a wafer-type butterfly valve that mounts to the fan's outlet flange and controls flow all the way to full shutoff (FanPedia pp. 125, 180). It is also known as a waffle damper, wafer-type butterfly valve or butterfly damper. It is available for automatic control, has a maximum temperature of 250°F, and its bolt pattern matches a 125# ASA pipe flange, a standard described later in this chapter. On an order, a blast gate answers the question "Can the customer shut this blower off at the outlet without a separate valve?"

    A slide gate damper is a flat gate that slides across the airway in a frame to throttle flow manually. Twin City Fan offers it for cast aluminum pressure blowers, with a cast aluminum frame and a galvanized steel gate, on the inlet or the outlet (FanPedia pp. 127, 192). It is a set-and-leave adjustment, typically used to trim a blower to its system during startup.

    Lab Exhaust Isolation and Bypass Dampers¶

    Laboratory exhaust fans dilute fume-hood exhaust with outside air drawn through a bypass air plenum and through the fan's nozzle or windband, then throw the mixture high above the roof (FanPedia pp. 4, 6). FanPedia's lab exhaust damper page (p. 138) shows three items on these fans: isolation dampers, bypass dampers and actuators, on models BCIFE and BAIFE and on models TVIFE, QIFE, QFE and TFE. FanPedia names the parts without describing their duty, so the descriptions below follow standard lab exhaust practice.

    A lab exhaust isolation damper sits between the exhaust plenum and an individual fan. It closes when that fan is off, so that a running fan on the same plenum cannot pull air backward through the idle one and so that the idle fan can be serviced. Lab systems often run several fans on one plenum with one on standby, which is why the isolation damper is standard on many of these layouts.

    A lab exhaust bypass damper controls the outside air admitted through the bypass air plenum. As fume hood sashes close and the building's exhaust demand drops, the bypass damper opens to make up the difference, so the fan keeps moving a nearly constant volume and holds the discharge velocity that carries the plume clear of the building. Both dampers are normally fitted with actuators tied to the building control system.

    Two Dampers, Two Different Jobs

    Notice that the bypass damper exists to keep fan airflow constant while the building's demand changes, while the isolation damper exists to make a stopped fan look like a closed wall. One manages flow; the other manages direction.

    Nested Inlet Vanes and Pre-Spin¶

    Inlet vanes control flow by changing how air enters the impeller rather than by adding resistance downstream. Pre-spin is the swirl that inlet vanes or an inlet box damper give the incoming air, turning it in the same direction the impeller rotates (FanPedia pp. 139, 183, 188). Because the air already has some of the rotation the impeller would otherwise have to give it, the impeller does less work on each pound of air. That is why FanPedia credits the inlet box damper with "a savings in horsepower at reduced loads," and why pre-spin control usually saves more power than outlet damper throttling.

    The general engineering basis is Euler's turbomachinery equation, which gives the work an impeller does on each unit mass of air:

    \[ w = U_2 \, V_{\theta 2} - U_1 \, V_{\theta 1} \]

    where:

    • \( w \) is the work done per unit mass of air
    • \( U_1 \) and \( U_2 \) are the blade speeds at the impeller inlet and outlet
    • \( V_{\theta 1} \) and \( V_{\theta 2} \) are the tangential (swirl) components of air velocity at the inlet and outlet

    With no pre-spin, \( V_{\theta 1} \) is zero. Pre-spin in the direction of rotation makes \( V_{\theta 1} \) positive, so the second term reduces \( w \): the fan develops less pressure and moves less air, and it draws less power doing so.

    Nested inlet vanes do the same job as the external inlet vanes of Chapter 18, but the vanes are built into the inlet funnel instead of a separate housing bolted to the inlet (FanPedia pp. 139, 188). They are applied to clean airstreams up to 600°F; external vanes are the choice for contaminated airstreams. Both come standard with a manual handle and can take an actuator. The trade-off is serviceability: replacing nested vanes requires replacing the inlet funnel assembly.

    The table below compares the two vane styles on the points an order turns on.

    Point of comparison External inlet vanes Nested inlet vanes
    Location Own housing, bolted to the fan inlet Within the inlet funnel
    Airstream Contaminated, or high temperature up to 600°F Clean, up to 600°F
    Operator Manual handle standard, actuator optional Manual handle standard, actuator optional

    Inlet Venturis¶

    An inlet venturi is a smooth, bell-shaped entry piece mounted on an open fan inlet, or on the free end of an inlet silencer, so that unducted air enters with as little turbulence and loss as possible. FanPedia pictures it beside the inlet funnels (p. 89), and it shares their aliases: inlet cone, funnel and inlet bell. The practical distinction is placement. An inlet funnel is part of the fan, guiding air into the impeller; an inlet venturi is fitted where air enters the system from the room. FanPedia's inlet silencer description, for example, offers the far end of the silencer with an inlet venturi, an inlet flange or an inlet pipe assembly (p. 188). A venturi is the right answer when a blower or silencer draws straight from open air and the buyer wants the entry to perform like a clean, ducted inlet.

    Inlet Box Styles and the Horn¶

    An inlet box turns the air 90 degrees into the fan when there is no room for a straight run of duct, and it is designed to minimize the system effect of that turn (FanPedia p. 187; system effect is covered in Chapter 25). Chapter 18 separated integral boxes from detached ones. FanPedia lists four styles in total: integral with horn, integral without horn, detached bolt-on, and detached free-standing (p. 141).

    A bolt-on inlet box is a detached box bolted directly to the inlet flange of the fan (FanPedia pp. 142, 187). It has a rectangular inlet flange that connects to the customer's ductwork, a round outlet flange that connects to the fan inlet flange, and a mounting structure of a single base angle, gussets and a reinforcement plate. It is available with a straight or a flared connection, and FanPedia names it TCF's preferred inlet box design.

    A free-standing inlet box is the same concept, except that it is mounted separately from the fan and fully supported at the floor (p. 187). It connects to the fan in one of two ways, and one must be selected: a round outlet flange for a bolted connection, or an outlet collar that joins the fan inlet collar with a rubber sleeve and clamps for a slip connection (p. 142). Its mounting structure is a base plate (foot), gussets and a reinforcement plate. A free-standing box makes sense when the box is too large to hang on the fan, or when the fan and box sit on different supports.

    An inlet box horn is an extension on an integral inlet box that carries the inlet connection out past the fan. FanPedia's note (p. 141) is precise: horns are used when the inlet and discharge airflows would intersect, and the horn lets ductwork be connected without interference. Discharge position and inlet box position together decide whether a horn is needed, which is one more reason both are read from the drive side (Chapter 12).

    Connecting the Fan to the Duct¶

    Every fan ends at two joints, inlet and outlet, and the joint type matters to the installer as much as any rating. Four connection types and one standard appear on Twin City Fan orders.

    A bolted flange connection joins two mating flanges with bolts through matching hole patterns, usually with a gasket between them. It is rigid and airtight, and it is the normal joint between a fan's inlet or outlet flange and a companion flange on the ductwork. On a free-standing inlet box, the round outlet flange is available only with the bolted connection (FanPedia p. 142).

    A slip connection joins a collar to a mating collar or pipe by sliding one over or into the other and sealing the joint, typically with a rubber sleeve held by clamps. FanPedia shows it on free-standing inlet box outlet collars (p. 142) and on welded square-to-round transitions with a slip connection on the outlet (p. 67). Because the sleeve can flex, a slip connection tolerates small misalignment and movement that a bolted joint cannot.

    A flexible connector, also called a flex connector or expansion joint, is a short section of flexible material that joins the fan's inlet or outlet to the ductwork (FanPedia pp. 126, 187). It reduces vibration transmission to and from the connecting ductwork and allows for some misalignment. Flex connectors are required on all isolated fans, are available round or rectangular, can be supplied by TCF or by the customer, and are shipped loose for field mounting. FanPedia also requires a flex connector, in the form of a slip connection, between a fan and a detached inlet box mounted on different planes, such as a fan on an isolation base with its inlet box on grade (p. 143).

    Isolated Fan, Rigid Duct

    Watch out for an order with spring isolators and hard-bolted duct at both ends. The duct ties the fan back to the structure and defeats the isolators, and it can load the fan housing as the springs move. Add flexible connectors at the inlet and outlet; FanPedia lists them as required on all isolated fans.

    A tube adapter and sleeve (FanPedia's full name is tube adapter and rubber sleeve with clamps) is a ready-made flexible outlet connection for pressure blowers (pp. 127, 194). It consists of a 4-inch-long metal collar and flange that bolts to the blower discharge, plus a 6-inch-long, 2-ply molded rubber slip-type connector with two hose clamps that joins the adapter to the pipe line. The sleeve helps isolate vibration and noise from the rest of the system. The connector is rated for pressures up to 5 psi and 180°F. It is also called a flanged adapter with rubber sleeve and clamps, a flange with boot, a mounting flange with boot, or simply a flex connector.

    The ASA pipe flange bolt pattern is the bolt-hole layout of a standard 125-pound-class pipe flange, written "125# ASA" in FanPedia. ASA is the American Standards Association, the predecessor of today's ANSI, and Class 125 is a common pressure class for cast iron pipe flanges, now published in ASME B16.1. A fan component with a 125# ASA bolt pattern bolts directly to an ordinary pipe flange, valve or fitting of the same nominal size. FanPedia states this for the blast gate and for the tube adapter (pp. 125, 180, 194).

    Check the Pipe Size, Not Just the Pattern

    Here's a quick sanity check: when a customer says "it bolts to our pipe," ask for the nominal pipe size and the flange class. A 125# ASA pattern matches only a flange of the same nominal size and class, so both numbers belong on the order.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Ceiling type and wall type gravity dampers share one description (parallel blade, end-pivoted, open on fan start, close by gravity, block backflow and weather); they differ in mounting plane.
    • Flat blade (Type 1) and airfoil blade (Type 2) dampers are both offered for outlet dampers; the parallel blade inlet box damper is Type 2 only.
    • Blade flutter comes from airflow holding blades partly open at low flow; a damper actuator powers the blades to position and prevents it.
    • Damper throttling adds resistance and moves the operating point left on the fan curve; it is inexpensive but saves less horsepower than other methods.
    • A blast gate is a wafer butterfly valve at the outlet flange for flow control to full shutoff, rated to 250°F, with a 125# ASA bolt pattern; a slide gate damper gives manual adjustment on cast aluminum pressure blowers.
    • On lab exhaust fans, isolation dampers seal off idle fans and bypass dampers admit outside air to keep fan flow and plume constant.
    • Pre-spin turns incoming air in the direction of rotation, reducing the work the impeller does; nested inlet vanes deliver it from within the inlet funnel for clean air up to 600°F.
    • An inlet venturi gives an open inlet or silencer end a smooth, low-loss entry.
    • Inlet boxes come integral (with or without a horn), detached bolt-on (TCF's preferred design) and detached free-standing (floor-supported, bolted or slip connection).
    • Bolted flanges are rigid; slip connections and flexible connectors tolerate movement, and flex connectors are required on all isolated fans. The tube adapter and rubber sleeve suits pressure blower pipe lines up to 5 psi and 180°F.
