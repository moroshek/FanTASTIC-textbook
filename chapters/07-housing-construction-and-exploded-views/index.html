    Housing Construction and Exploded Views¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter takes a closer look at how fan housings are built: scroll side plates and wrappers, the cutoff, straightening vanes, horizontal, three-way, and pie split housings, housing framing, access panels, cast housings, discharge cones, belt guards and belt tubes, and roof exhauster shrouds and curb caps. It shows how to read the exploded views FanPedia uses to name every part. After completing it, readers can read an exploded view and explain what each housing feature is for.

    Taking the Fan Apart on Paper

    A customer calls about a "bent piece of sheet metal near the outlet" and wants a replacement. By the end of this chapter you will open the right FanPedia drawing, put a name and a purpose to that part, and know whether the housing can come apart to reach it. Let's follow the airflow.

    In Chapter 3 you learned that a fan housing directs air from the inlet to the outlet and supports the other key components. FanPedia puts it the same way: the housing is the "main structure to support other key components such as inlet, outlet, framing and structural supports," and it is also known as the side, scroll or casing (FanPedia p. 59). Not every fan has one. Plenum fans, plug fans, panel fans and ring fans run without a housing of their own.

    This chapter works through the pieces a housing is built from, the ways a housing can be split, and the guards, covers and panels that attach to it. First, though, you need the tool FanPedia uses to name all of those pieces.

    Reading an Exploded View¶

    An exploded view is a drawing of an assembly with its parts pulled apart along the lines they assemble on, so each part is visible and can carry its own label. Parts that bolt together sit on a shared line of travel, so the drawing shows both what each part is called and roughly where it goes. FanPedia's Fan Basics section opens with eleven pages of them (pp. 9–19), one or two fan families per page, each labeled in the shop's own vocabulary. The Housing Construction pages (pp. 59–66) add housing-only views that zoom in on the shell.

    The table below lists where each family's exploded view sits, so you can open the right page while you read this chapter.

    Fan family Exploded view pages Housing detail pages
    Housed centrifugal, direct drive and belt driven 9–10 60 (SWSI), 61 (DWDI)
    Plenum fans 11–12 none (unhoused)
    Plug fans 13–14 66
    Tubeaxial and vaneaxial fans 15–16 63
    Inline centrifugal and mixed flow fans 17 64
    Panel fans 18 none
    Centrifugal roof exhausters 19 none
    Pressure blowers (cast) none 62
    Square inline fans none 65

    Three habits make an exploded view easier to read:

    1. Find the airflow first: inlet funnel or collar on one side, outlet flange or discharge on the other.
    2. Separate the housing parts (the shell and what is welded or bolted to it) from the rotating parts (impeller, shaft, bearings) and the drive parts (motor, belt, sheave).
    3. Note the caption under the drawing, such as "Arr. 9 Fan" or "Direct Drive Shown," because the same family can show different parts in different arrangements.

    A Drawing Is a Parts List

    Notice that every label in an exploded view is a word you can put on an order or a replacement-parts request. Read the drawing as a parts list with pictures, and the vocabulary of this whole book starts to hang together.

    The Scroll Housing: Side Plates, Wrapper and Cutoff¶

    A housed centrifugal fan's shell is built from three kinds of pieces. FanPedia's SWSI housing view (p. 60) labels them HOUSING SIDE, HOUSING SCROLL and CUTOFF.

    A housing side plate is one of the two flat sides of a scroll housing. On a single width, single inlet (SWSI) fan, the inlet side plate carries the round inlet opening, which in turn holds the inlet collar, inlet funnel or inlet flange; the opposite side plate faces the drive. On a double width, double inlet (DWDI) fan (p. 61), both side plates carry inlets, and each side also supports a bearing on a structural support. Because the side plates hold the inlet hardware and often the bearing supports, they are the stiffest, most load-bearing panels of the shell.

    The scroll wrapper (FanPedia's "housing scroll") is the curved band of sheet metal that wraps around the impeller between the two side plates and runs out to the discharge. In standard fan engineering terms, the scroll's spiral shape gives the space between impeller and housing a gradually growing cross-section. That shape collects the air the impeller throws off, slows it down, and turns part of its velocity into static pressure before it leaves through the outlet. When FanPedia describes housed centrifugal fans as having "single or double width scroll housings" (p. 4), the width it refers to is the width of this wrapper.

    The cutoff is the point near the outlet where the scroll wrapper comes closest to the impeller, like a tongue that separates air leaving through the discharge from air that would otherwise keep circling the housing. FanPedia groups it with the wiper bar as a feature that "helps to direct air through the housing ... to prevent turbulence and to increase efficiency" (p. 59). On the p. 60 drawing the cutoff sits at the discharge end of the scroll, while the wiper bar sits near the inlet funnel; Chapter 18 covers the wiper bar.

    Before you look at the interactive drawing below, recall the inlet and outlet hardware from Chapter 3: the inlet collar is a short cylinder at the inlet opening, the inlet funnel is the shaped piece that guides air into the impeller, and the inlet and outlet flanges are the bolting rims that connect to ductwork. Framing is covered in the next section, and the pedestal is the stand that carries the bearings (and, on some arrangements, the motor).

    Housing Framing and Cast Housings¶

    Housing framing is the structural steel (formed angles or channels) fastened to the outside of the housing to stiffen the sheet metal and give the fan feet to sit on. FanPedia labels FRAMING along the bottom of both the SWSI and DWDI housing views (pp. 60–61), and shows separate STRUCTURAL SUPPORTS on the DWDI fan, where each side plate carries a bearing. Unhoused fans need framing too: the plenum fan views (pp. 11–12) and the square inline view (p. 65) label a FRAME that holds the inlet panel, motor and pedestal together in place of a housing.

    Most fan housings are fabricated: cut from sheet or plate and welded or bolted together, as Chapter 3 described. A cast housing is instead poured as a single metal casting. FanPedia's p. 62 shows pressure blowers built two ways, with a cast aluminum housing and with a cast iron housing, each with its inlet, outlet, pedestal and structural support. A casting gives a pressure blower's narrow housing its shape in one piece. Cast parts appear elsewhere on these blowers as well; FanPedia lists a slide gate damper with a cast aluminum frame for cast aluminum pressure blowers (p. 192).

    Split Housings¶

    Chapter 3 introduced the split housing: a housing built in sections that bolt together, so it can be taken apart. FanPedia names three kinds for centrifugal fans (pp. 156–157 and 194), and each one is named by where the cut runs.

    A horizontal split housing is cut along the housing's horizontal centerline, so the upper half lifts off the lower half. FanPedia calls this the "standard split" and notes that size 807 and above may be split by the shop for shipping purposes.

    A three-way split housing is cut into three sections, up to 180 degrees. FanPedia says this split is "normally required either for shipping or to enable the fan to enter a specific sized opening," such as a doorway or roof hatch on a retrofit job. Three-way splits need additional drafting and engineering time, so they should be identified early in a quote.

    A pie split housing is cut at angles of 90 degrees or greater, removing a wedge of the housing so the impeller can come out "without disturbing inlet or outlet" ductwork. FanPedia shows two styles:

    • Mohawk (newer style): splits between the scroll and the inlet housing side; the inlet side itself has no split. It is not used on double width fans or on fans with attached inlet boxes.
    • Standard (older style): splits all the way down to the funnel or inlet plate. Weld nuts on the inside of the split take bolts from outside the housing, and studs are welded to the outside.

    FanPedia's arrangement descriptions show where pie splits are built in: Arrangements 3SI and 7SI (single inlet fans with an attached or integrated inlet box) and 7DI include a pie split housing "for easy impeller removal" (pp. 164–168).

    The table below summarizes the three splits you have just read about.

    Split Where the cut runs Main reason Note
    Horizontal Along the horizontal centerline Standard split; shipping large sizes Shop may split size 807 and above for shipping
    Three-way Three sections, up to 180 degrees Shipping, or passing through a specific opening Extra drafting and engineering time
    Pie A wedge at 90 degrees or greater Impeller removal without disturbing inlet or outlet Mohawk or standard style

    Splits and Tight Housings Don't Mix Easily

    Watch out for split housings on nominally leak-tight or RTO jobs: every split is another joint that can leak. FanPedia says split housings are "not recommended" for nominally leak-tight construction, and when a split is used there, it needs gasketing, close bolt spacing and centering plates to seal around the shaft seal and inlet (pp. 120–121), so flag it before the quote goes out. Chapter 21 covers those requirements.

    Getting Inside: Housing Access Panels¶

    Chapter 3 introduced the access door, a small bolted or quick-opening door in the housing for inspecting and cleaning the impeller. FanPedia shows access doors on the housings of tubeaxial, vaneaxial, inline centrifugal and mixed flow fans (pp. 63–64) and lists bolted, raised and quick-open styles among its common accessories (p. 125).

    A housing access panel is larger: a whole removable side of the housing rather than a door cut into it. The clearest example is the square inline fan. FanPedia describes square inline centrifugal fans as "built with square housings and removable side panels" (p. 6), and its p. 65 view labels the ACCESS PANEL as "REMOVABLE, 3 SIDES." Taking off a panel exposes the impeller, motor and drive at once, which is why square inline fans are easy to service in tight ceiling spaces.

    Tubular Housings: Straightening Vanes, Discharge Cone and Belt Tube¶

    Inline centrifugal, mixed flow and axial fans use a tubular housing, and their exploded views (pp. 15–17) show three parts a scroll housing does not have.

    Straightening vanes are fixed vanes inside a tubular housing, downstream of the impeller, that take the swirl out of the air leaving the impeller and turn it back into straight-through flow. FanPedia labels STRAIGHTENING VANES on both the inline centrifugal and the mixed flow views (pp. 17 and 64). On a vaneaxial fan the same job is done by the guide vanes of Chapter 4, which FanPedia says "help straighten the airflow as it moves through the fan" (p. 7). Recovering that swirl as useful pressure is why a vaned tubular fan can develop more pressure than a plain tube.

    A discharge cone is a tapered section at the outlet end of the tubular housing. FanPedia's belt driven inline centrifugal and mixed flow view (p. 17) labels it at the discharge, where it guides the air leaving the impeller and vanes toward the outlet connection.

    A belt tube (FanPedia: "BELT TUBE, aka Belt Fairing") is an enclosed passage through the side of a belt driven tubular housing. In an axial or inline fan the motor sits outside the tube while the impeller shaft is inside it, so the belts have to cross the airstream. The belt tube shields the belts and sheaves from the moving air and from whatever the air carries. It appears on the belt driven tubeaxial, vaneaxial, inline centrifugal and mixed flow views (pp. 15–17, 63–64).

    Guards and Covers¶

    A belt guard encloses the belts and sheaves on the outside of a belt driven fan so that no one can reach the moving drive. FanPedia shows one beside the belt tube on the belt driven inline view (p. 17) and lists it as a common accessory (p. 125). FanPedia also notes that spark-resistant axial fans keep a mild steel belt guard (p. 109); Chapter 20 explains why that matters. A related accessory, the shaft/bearing guard and coupling guard, protects the exposed shaft and coupling on direct drive fans (p. 126).

    A motor cover, also called a weather cover, is a hood placed over the motor to protect it from rain, snow and sun. FanPedia lists "Motor Cover / Weather Cover" as a common accessory (p. 126), and the centrifugal roof exhauster view (p. 19) labels a MOTOR COVER on top of the unit, above the MOTOR HOUSING that encloses the motor.

    Guard or Tube? Ask Where the Belt Runs

    Here's a quick check when a drawing shows a belt drive: if the belts cross the airstream inside a tube, you need a belt tube; if they run outside the housing where people can reach them, you need a belt guard. A belt driven inline fan, as on p. 17, has both.

    Plug Fan Hardware: Mounting Panel and Insulation Plug¶

    A plug fan has no housing; it is "plugged" into the wall of the customer's plenum, which acts as the fan housing (FanPedia pp. 5 and 172). Two parts take the place of the housing's structure.

    The mounting panel is the flat plate that fastens the fan to the customer's wall. FanPedia explains that "the mounting panel is mounted to the outside wall and the impeller inside the plenum wall" (p. 5); the inlet funnel and impeller hang inside the plenum, while the shaft, bearings and motor stay outside the process airstream. On Arrangement 9 plug fans the wall carries the whole fan. On the grade-mounted versions (Arrangements 1P, 4P, 8P and 9P), the fan stands on the foundation instead, and FanPedia lists the mounting panel as optional (pp. 170–172).

    The insulation plug is an insulated section mounted with the panel that fills the opening in the plenum wall around the fan, so the wall keeps its insulating value where the fan passes through it. It appears on both plug fan exploded views (pp. 13–14) and on the Arr. 9P detail "Mounting Panel with Insulation Plug" (p. 66). Plug fans often serve hot equipment such as ovens, so the plug matters in high-temperature service. For Arrangement 9 high-temperature plug fans, FanPedia shows the insulated plug as optional up to 500°F on fans with less than 4" plug or wall thickness, and required at 301°F and above on fans with 4" or more plug or wall thickness (p. 113, which refers to Fan Engineering Letter FE-3200).

    Rooftop Hardware: Shroud, Curb Cap, Nozzle and Windband¶

    Roof-mounted fans need parts that seal them to the building and send the exhaust where it should go.

    A curb cap is the base plate or frame of a roof-mounted fan that sits on and covers the roof curb, the raised frame built around the roof opening. FanPedia's centrifugal roof exhauster view (p. 19) stacks CURB CAP directly on ROOF CURB, and its roof mounted axial fans are shown "with hood and curb cap" or "with stack cap and curb cap" (p. 7). On vertical inline and axial fans mounted on a roof, the motor is centered on the curb cap (p. 57).

    A roof exhauster shroud is the sheet-metal shell around the impeller of a centrifugal roof exhauster that guides the air the impeller throws off. FanPedia labels the SHROUD between the impeller and the curb cap on its upblast and downblast views (p. 19), with a SKIRT and bird screen around the downblast unit, where air is turned down and outward. Chapter 5 described these upblast and downblast discharges.

    A nozzle and windband sit at the top of a lab exhaust fan. The nozzle accelerates the contaminated exhaust into a high-velocity jet, and the windband around it draws in fresh outside air, which FanPedia calls entrained flow (pp. 4 and 6). The fresh air mixes with the contaminated air to dilute it and carry it up and away from the building. Chapter 5 introduced entrained flow; here you can see that the nozzle and windband are the housing parts that produce it.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • An exploded view separates an assembly along its assembly lines so each part carries a label; FanPedia's run from p. 9 to p. 19, and its housing details from p. 59 to p. 66.
    • A scroll housing is built from two housing side plates and a curved scroll wrapper; the cutoff at the outlet keeps discharged air from recirculating.
    • Housing framing stiffens the shell and supports it; a cast housing, as on cast aluminum or cast iron pressure blowers, is poured in one piece.
    • A horizontal split housing is the standard split; a three-way split housing is for shipping or passing through a specific opening; a pie split housing lets the impeller out without disturbing the ducts, in Mohawk or standard style.
    • Housing access panels remove a whole side (three sides on square inline fans), while access doors are cut into the housing.
    • Tubular housings add straightening vanes, a discharge cone and, on belt drives, a belt tube; a belt guard and motor cover protect people and the motor outside the airstream.
    • Plug fans use a mounting panel and an insulation plug at the plenum wall; roof fans use a curb cap, a roof exhauster shroud, and on lab exhaust a nozzle and windband.
