    Air, Pressure and the Fan¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter introduces the physical quantities used to describe air in motion: airflow volume in cubic feet per minute, air velocity, static pressure in inches of water gauge, and air density as it changes with temperature and altitude. It defines a fan as a machine that moves air by turning at a given speed and power. Every later chapter builds on this vocabulary. After completing it, readers can state what a fan delivers and in which units.

    Why This Chapter Comes First¶

    Every fan order, catalog page and specification in this book reduces to a short sentence: move this much air, against this much resistance, at these conditions, without the air destroying the fan. Before you can read that sentence on a performance table or write it in a submittal, you need the handful of quantities it is built from: airflow volume in cubic feet per minute, air velocity, pressure in inches of water gauge, fan speed in revolutions per minute, horsepower, and the density of the air itself. This chapter defines each one, shows how they connect, and works a realistic number for every idea so you can check a catalog value instead of just copying it.

    Air Movement: Why We Move Air at All¶

    Air movement is the transfer of a mass of air from one place to another, driven by a difference in pressure. Buildings and plants move air for a small number of practical reasons: to supply fresh air to people, to carry heat into or out of a space, to deliver air a process needs (combustion air for a burner, drying air for a dryer), and to capture and remove something unwanted (fumes from a lab hood, dust from a grinder, steam from a kitchen). In each case the air is not the product; it is the vehicle. The fan's job is to keep that vehicle moving at the rate the job requires.

    Two physical facts make air movement an engineering problem rather than an afterthought. First, air has mass. A cubic foot of air at room conditions weighs about 0.075 pound, which sounds negligible until you multiply it by the volume of a building. Second, air moves only when something pushes it: it always flows from a region of higher pressure toward a region of lower pressure, and it resists being speeded up, turned and squeezed through filters and ducts. A fan supplies the pressure difference that keeps the air moving against that resistance.

    The following table organizes the common jobs of air movement that the paragraph above described, with a typical site for each and the property of the air that matters most for that job.

    Job of air movement Typical site What matters most
    Ventilation (fresh air for people) Office, school, warehouse Enough volume, delivered quietly
    Heating or cooling Air handler, cooling tower, oven Volume and the air temperature
    Process air Burner combustion air, dryer, kiln Volume, pressure and temperature, often high
    Contaminant capture and removal Lab hood, paint booth, dust collector, kitchen hood What the air carries, and where it is discharged
    Pressurization or exhaust balance Clean room, stair tower, plenum Pressure difference between spaces

    Worked Example: How Much Air Is in a Room?¶

    A small machine shop measures 40 ft by 30 ft with a 12 ft ceiling. The owner wants the room air replaced ten times every hour.

    1. Room volume: \( 40 \times 30 \times 12 = 14{,}400 \) cubic feet.
    2. Weight of that air at room conditions: \( 14{,}400 \times 0.075 = 1{,}080 \) pounds, more than half a ton.
    3. Ten changes per hour means the fan must move \( 14{,}400 \times 10 = 144{,}000 \) cubic feet, or about 10,800 pounds of air, every hour.
    4. Per minute, that is \( 144{,}000 \div 60 = 2{,}400 \) cubic feet.

    The last number, 2,400 cubic feet every minute, is the form in which a fan's airflow is ordered and cataloged. You will meet it again below as a duct calculation and as a MicroSim challenge.

    Air Is Heavy Enough to Matter

    Think of a fan as a machine that keeps lifting and throwing a steady stream of mass, not as something that "stirs" weightless air. Once you picture those 10,800 pounds an hour, it stops being surprising that a modest ventilation fan needs a real motor, and that the weight of each cubic foot changes the work the fan must do.

    The Airstream¶

    The airstream is the moving body of air that passes through a fan and its connected ductwork, together with everything that air carries: heat, moisture, dust, fibers, fumes or vapors. FanPedia uses the term constantly because so many construction decisions depend on which parts of a fan sit in the airstream and which sit out of it. The impeller and the inside of the housing are always in the airstream. Bearings, motors and belts may or may not be, depending on how the fan is arranged.

    The properties of the airstream decide what the fan must be built from and how it must be laid out. Temperature changes the air's weight and the strength of metals and seals. Moisture and corrosive gases attack materials. Solids wear blades and build up on them. Flammable vapors and dusts make a spark dangerous. Before looking at a worked example, the following table summarizes those airstream properties and what each one changes in a fan selection; later chapters treat each in depth.

    Airstream property What it changes in the fan Where this book covers it
    Temperature Air density, material strength, bearing and seal choice This chapter; Chapter 21
    Altitude (site elevation) Air density, and therefore pressure and power This chapter
    Moisture Drains, coatings, materials Chapter 2
    Solids (dust, fibers, chips) Impeller type, wear protection Chapter 9
    Corrosive gases Materials and coatings Chapter 20
    Flammable vapors or dusts Spark-resistant construction Chapter 20

    Worked Example: Which Parts Sit in a 300°F Airstream?¶

    A customer needs a belt-driven centrifugal fan to exhaust 300°F air from a curing oven. Compare two arrangements described in FanPedia's technical descriptions:

    • Arrangement 1, single width, single inlet: the impeller is overhung on the end of the shaft, and "the two fan bearings are mounted on the bearing pedestal, out of the airstream," which FanPedia says makes the arrangement suited to high-temperature or contaminated air (FanPedia, p. 162).
    • Arrangement 3, double width, double inlet: the impeller is mounted between the bearings, and "both bearings are located in the airstream," so standard double width, double inlet fans should be used for clean air with air temperatures limited to 130°F (FanPedia, p. 167).

    Trace the airstream: in Arrangement 3 the 300°F air flows directly past both bearings, which is well above the 130°F limit for the standard design. In Arrangement 1 the hot air stays inside the housing while the bearings sit outside on the pedestal. Arrangement 1 is the natural starting point for this job. Arrangements themselves are the subject of Chapter 11; the point here is that you cannot pick one until you know what is in the airstream.

    The Fan¶

    A fan is a machine that moves air by turning a bladed wheel, the impeller, inside or in front of a housing. The rotating blades push on the air, raise its pressure slightly and send it on its way. A motor turns the impeller either directly (the impeller is mounted on the motor shaft) or through belts and pulleys. FanPedia describes the main families by the path the air takes: housed centrifugal fans draw air in along the shaft and discharge it "at a right angle to the fan shaft," while axial and inline fans "move air straight through the fan with a rotating impeller" (FanPedia, pp. 4, 6-7). Chapter 3 names every part, and Chapter 4 compares the families.

    What separates a fan from a compressor is how little it raises the pressure. Atmospheric pressure at sea level is about 14.7 pounds per square inch, and a fan adds only a small fraction to that. Because the air is squeezed so little, it barely changes volume passing through, and for most work you can treat the cubic feet per minute entering a fan as equal to the cubic feet per minute leaving it.

    Worked Example: How Big Is a Fan's Pressure Rise?¶

    A heavy-duty industrial fan develops 10 inches of water gauge (a unit defined in the pressure section below; atmospheric pressure is about 407 of these units).

    1. Pressure rise as a fraction of atmospheric pressure: \( 10 \div 407 = 0.025 \), or about 2.5 percent.
    2. The air's volume therefore shrinks by only about 2.5 percent through the fan; at a typical HVAC pressure of 2 inches of water gauge, about 0.5 percent.
    3. Conclusion: in catalog work the fan's inlet and outlet airflow are taken as the same number, and the fan is rated by the airflow at its inlet.

    Every fan rating states what the fan delivers in a fixed set of quantities: airflow, pressure, speed, power and the air density the rating assumes. The sections that follow define each one, and the chapter closes with an interactive duty statement that puts them back together.

    Airflow Volume and Cubic Feet Per Minute¶

    Airflow volume is the volume of air a fan moves in a unit of time. It is a rate: not "how much air is in the duct" but "how much air passes a point each minute." In the United States the unit is cubic feet per minute, abbreviated CFM. One CFM is one cubic foot of air passing a point every minute. The machine-shop example above needed 2,400 CFM; a large industrial exhaust fan may move several hundred thousand CFM.

    Airflow volume is not the same as the amount of air in pounds. A cubic foot of hot air weighs less than a cubic foot of cool air, so the same CFM carries different masses at different temperatures. The connection is the mass flow equation below; air density, its key ingredient, gets its own section later in this chapter.

    Mass Flow from Airflow Volume¶

    \[ \dot{m} = Q \times \rho \]

    where:

    • \( \dot{m} \) is the mass flow rate in pounds per minute
    • \( Q \) is the airflow volume in cubic feet per minute (CFM)
    • \( \rho \) is the air density in pounds per cubic foot

    Worked Example: Same CFM, Different Air¶

    A process fan moves 10,000 CFM.

    1. At 70°F and sea level, density is 0.075 lb/ft³: \( 10{,}000 \times 0.075 = 750 \) lb of air per minute.
    2. At 400°F and sea level, density falls to about 0.0462 lb/ft³: \( 10{,}000 \times 0.0462 = 462 \) lb per minute.
    3. The fan is moving the same 10,000 CFM in both cases but nearly 40 percent less air by weight when it is hot.

    This is why fan catalogs and specifications always state CFM at a stated condition. Fan airflow is rated as the actual volume at the fan inlet, at the density of the air actually entering the fan. When a specification lists CFM without a temperature and elevation, ask for them before selecting anything.

    Air Velocity¶

    Air velocity is how fast the air is moving at a point, in feet per minute. Velocity and airflow volume are linked by the area the air passes through: the same CFM moves slowly through a large duct and quickly through a small one. Velocity matters because fast air is noisy, costs pressure to push through ducts, and can carry dust that slow air would drop. Chapter 2 turns this relationship into the continuity equation and shows how velocity creates its own pressure; here we need only the basic calculation.

    Airflow, Velocity and Area¶

    \[ Q = V \times A \]

    where:

    • \( Q \) is the airflow volume in CFM
    • \( V \) is the average air velocity in feet per minute
    • \( A \) is the flow area in square feet

    Worked Example: Velocity in a Rectangular Duct¶

    The machine shop's 2,400 CFM flows through a 16 in by 12 in duct.

    1. Convert the duct size to square feet: \( (16 \times 12) \div 144 = 1.333 \) ft².
    2. Solve for velocity: \( V = Q \div A = 2{,}400 \div 1.333 = 1{,}800 \) feet per minute.
    3. If the same air were carried in a 24 in by 16 in duct (2.667 ft²), velocity would halve to 900 feet per minute.

    Check the Units Before the Arithmetic

    Duct sizes come in inches but the equation wants square feet, so divide width times height by 144 (or use \( \pi D^2 \div 576 \) for a round duct of diameter \( D \) inches) before you divide CFM by area. If your answer comes out 144 times too small, you skipped that step.

    Air Pressure¶

    Air pressure is the force air exerts on each unit of area it touches. At sea level the weight of the atmosphere above us presses on everything with about 14.7 pounds per square inch; that is atmospheric pressure, and it falls as you climb higher. We do not feel it because it pushes equally from every side. What moves air, and what a fan creates, is a difference in pressure: a little more than atmospheric on one side, a little less on the other.

    For that reason fan work almost always uses gauge pressure, pressure measured relative to the surrounding atmosphere, rather than absolute pressure. A positive gauge pressure means the air is pushing outward (inside a supply duct, for instance); a negative gauge pressure, often called suction, means the surroundings are pushing in (inside an exhaust duct or at a fan inlet). Air in a duct moves from the higher-pressure end toward the lower-pressure end, and the fan sits in the middle, pulling on its inlet side and pushing on its discharge side. Because fan pressures are so small compared with the atmosphere, pounds per square inch is an awkward unit for them, and the fan industry uses a smaller one.

    Inches of Water Gauge¶

    An inch of water gauge (written in. wg, or sometimes in. w.g. or "inches of water column") is the pressure that holds up a column of water one inch tall. The name comes from the instrument: a manometer is a U-shaped glass tube partly filled with water. Connect one leg to a duct and leave the other open to the room; if the duct pressure is higher than the room's, the water is pushed down on the duct side and up on the open side, and the difference in the two water levels, in inches, is the gauge pressure in inches of water gauge.

    One inch of water gauge is a very small pressure, which is exactly why it suits fans. The conversions below are the ones you will actually use.

    Pressure In inches of water gauge Other units
    1 in. wg 1 0.0361 psi; 5.2 lb/ft²; about 249 Pa
    1 psi 27.7 144 lb/ft²
    Atmosphere at sea level 407 14.7 psi; 29.92 inches of mercury
    Typical commercial HVAC fan 1 to 4 illustrative range

    Worked Example: The Force Behind a Small Number¶

    A 4 ft by 4 ft access door on an air-handling unit sits on a section running at +2.0 in. wg.

    1. Convert to pounds per square foot: \( 2.0 \times 5.2 = 10.4 \) lb/ft².
    2. Multiply by the door area: \( 10.4 \times 16 = 166 \) pounds pushing the door outward.
    3. Expressed in psi, the same pressure is only \( 2.0 \times 0.0361 = 0.072 \) psi, which is why nobody quotes fan pressure in psi.

    The same 166 pounds acts inward on a door in a section under −2.0 in. wg suction, which is why access doors, inspection panels and housing seams are built for the pressure the fan develops, not just for the weather.

    Psi and Inches Are Not Interchangeable

    A common trap is reading "2 psi" on a process sheet where the writer meant 2 in. wg, or the reverse; the two differ by a factor of 27.7, and a fan selected at the wrong one is useless. Whenever a pressure has no unit or an unusual one, confirm it with the customer and restate it in inches of water gauge on the quote.

    Static Pressure¶

    Static pressure (SP) is the pressure air exerts equally in all directions on the walls of a duct or housing, independent of whether the air is moving. It is the "squeezing" pressure, and it is the one a manometer reads when its tube is connected flush to the side of the duct, perpendicular to the flow. Moving air also has a second pressure associated with its speed, called velocity pressure; Chapter 2 defines it and adds the two into total pressure.

    For catalog work, static pressure is the main way to describe resistance. Every filter, coil, damper, grille, hood and length of duct takes away some static pressure as the air passes through it; that loss is its pressure drop. The fan must develop enough static pressure to make up all those drops at the required airflow. When a specification says "12,000 CFM at 3.0 in. wg SP," the 3.0 is the system's total resistance that the fan must overcome. Static pressure is negative (suction) on the inlet side of the fan and positive on the discharge side.

    Worked Example: Adding Up a System's Resistance¶

    A supply system draws outdoor air through a louver, a filter and a coil, then pushes it through ductwork to a diffuser. The design engineer lists these pressure drops at the design airflow (illustrative values):

    Component Side of fan Pressure drop (in. wg) Static pressure reading after it (in. wg)
    Intake louver Inlet 0.15 −0.15
    Filter Inlet 0.50 −0.65
    Coil Inlet 0.75 −1.40 (fan inlet)
    Ductwork and fittings Discharge 0.95 +1.10 at fan outlet, falling to +0.15
    Diffuser Discharge 0.15 0 (room)
    1. Inlet-side drops: \( 0.15 + 0.50 + 0.75 = 1.40 \) in. wg, so the fan inlet sits at −1.40 in. wg.
    2. Discharge-side drops: \( 0.95 + 0.15 = 1.10 \) in. wg, so the fan outlet must be at +1.10 in. wg to push the air out into the room.
    3. The fan must raise the static pressure from −1.40 to +1.10: \( 1.10 - (-1.40) = 2.50 \) in. wg.

    This simple subtraction treats the air velocity at the fan inlet and outlet as equal; the exact definition of fan static pressure, which accounts for velocity pressure, is in Chapter 10. The sim below lets you read each of these points on a manometer.

    Fan Speed¶

    Fan speed is how fast the impeller turns, in revolutions per minute (RPM). It is the single most powerful lever on a fan's performance: at a given size, turning the impeller faster moves more air and develops more pressure, at the cost of much more power and noise. The fan laws in Chapter 25 quantify this; for now it is enough to know that airflow rises in proportion to speed, pressure with the square of speed, and power with the cube.

    How the fan gets its speed depends on the drive. In a direct drive fan the impeller is on the motor shaft, so the fan runs at motor speed. AC induction motors run near a synchronous speed set by the power frequency and the motor's number of poles, and slightly below it under load. FanPedia lists the typical values, organized here by frequency.

    Typical synchronous speed, 60 Hz power Typical synchronous speed, 50 Hz power
    3600 RPM 3000 RPM
    1800 RPM 1500 RPM
    1200 RPM 1000 RPM
    900 RPM 750 RPM

    FanPedia adds that "actual nominal motor speeds vary by motor HP and manufacturer" (FanPedia, p. 71). A motor listed as "1800 RPM" typically turns a little under that under load, which is why catalog direct-drive speeds sit slightly below these round numbers. A belt drive fan uses pulleys, called sheaves, of different diameters so the fan can run at any speed the job requires, not just the four motor speeds. A variable frequency drive can also change a motor's speed electrically; Chapter 15 covers speed control.

    Belt Drive Speed Ratio¶

    \[ N_{fan} = N_{motor} \times \frac{D_{motor}}{D_{fan}} \]

    where:

    • \( N_{fan} \) is the fan speed in RPM
    • \( N_{motor} \) is the motor speed in RPM
    • \( D_{motor} \) is the pitch diameter of the motor sheave in inches
    • \( D_{fan} \) is the pitch diameter of the fan sheave in inches

    The speed of the blade tips, not just the RPM, governs the stress in an impeller, because a larger wheel at the same RPM has faster tips. Tip speed is the distance around the impeller times the revolutions per minute.

    Impeller Tip Speed¶

    \[ V_{tip} = \frac{\pi \times D \times N}{12} \]

    where:

    • \( V_{tip} \) is the tip speed in feet per minute
    • \( D \) is the impeller diameter in inches
    • \( N \) is the fan speed in RPM

    Worked Example: Setting a Belt-Driven Fan's Speed¶

    A belt-driven fan with a 36.5 in impeller (illustrative) is driven by a motor turning 1,760 RPM. The motor sheave is 6 in and the fan sheave is 9 in (pitch diameters).

    1. Fan speed: \( 1{,}760 \times 6 \div 9 = 1{,}173 \) RPM.
    2. Tip speed: \( \pi \times 36.5 \times 1{,}173 \div 12 = 11{,}210 \) feet per minute, roughly 127 miles per hour at the blade tips.
    3. To speed the fan up by about 10 percent, the motor sheave could be enlarged to 6.6 in: \( 1{,}760 \times 6.6 \div 9 = 1{,}291 \) RPM. That would also raise power by roughly a third (\( 1.1^3 = 1.33 \)), so the motor must be rechecked.

    Every fan design has a maximum safe operating speed, and higher fan classes allow higher speeds; Chapter 10 explains fan class and maximum safe speed. A speed change on a job site is therefore never "just a sheave swap": check the class limit and the motor load first.

    Horsepower¶

    Horsepower (hp) is a unit of power, the rate of doing work. One horsepower equals 33,000 foot-pounds of work per minute, or 0.7457 kilowatt. FanPedia's motor pages give the conversion directly: "kW = HP x .7457" (FanPedia, p. 71). In fan work three different horsepowers appear, and mixing them up is a frequent source of confusion:

    • Air horsepower is the useful power delivered to the air: airflow times pressure, converted to horsepower.
    • Brake horsepower (BHP) is the power that must be delivered to the fan shaft. It is larger than air horsepower because no fan is perfectly efficient; this is the number in a catalog performance table.
    • Motor horsepower is the nameplate rating of the motor chosen to supply the brake horsepower, picked from a ladder of standard sizes.

    The constant 6356 in the equation below comes from the unit definitions: one horsepower is 33,000 ft·lb/min, and one inch of water gauge is about 5.19 lb/ft², so \( 33{,}000 \div 5.19 \approx 6{,}356 \). Static efficiency, the ratio of air horsepower (based on static pressure) to brake horsepower, is treated fully in Chapter 10.

    Brake Horsepower from Airflow and Static Pressure¶

    \[ BHP = \frac{Q \times SP}{6356 \times \eta_s} \]

    where:

    • \( BHP \) is the brake horsepower at the fan shaft
    • \( Q \) is the airflow in CFM
    • \( SP \) is the fan static pressure in in. wg
    • \( \eta_s \) is the fan's static efficiency, as a decimal

    Motors come in standard power ratings. The following excerpt from FanPedia's power equivalents table lists the common sizes in the range you will see most often, with their metric equivalents (FanPedia, p. 71).

    Motor rating (hp) 1 1-1/2 2 3 5 7-1/2 10 15 20 25 30 40 50
    Equivalent (kW) 0.75 1.1 1.5 2.2 4 5.5 7.5 11 15 18.5 22 30 37

    Worked Example: From Duty Point to Motor¶

    A fan must deliver 12,000 CFM at 3.0 in. wg static pressure, and the catalog shows a static efficiency of 72 percent at that point (illustrative).

    1. Brake horsepower: \( 12{,}000 \times 3.0 \div (6{,}356 \times 0.72) = 7.87 \), call it 7.9 BHP.
    2. Choose the next standard motor above 7.9 hp from the table: 10 hp (7.5 kW).
    3. Check the motor load: \( 7.9 \div 10 = 79 \) percent. FanPedia notes that most motors are designed to run at 50 to 100 percent of rated load, with maximum efficiency usually near 75 percent; its own example is a 10 hp motor with an acceptable range of 5 to 10 hp (FanPedia, p. 72). A 79 percent load is a good fit.
    4. In metric terms the fan shaft needs \( 7.9 \times 0.7457 = 5.9 \) kW.

    A 7-1/2 hp motor would be overloaded at 7.9 BHP, and a 20 hp motor would run at under 40 percent load, below the range where motor efficiency holds up. Motor selection rules, including service factor and cold starts, are in Chapter 14 and Chapter 25.

    Air Density¶

    Air density is the mass of air in one cubic foot, in pounds per cubic foot (lb/ft³). Every number so far has quietly assumed air at 70°F and sea level, where it weighs 0.075 lb/ft³. That reference condition is called standard air, and fan catalogs publish their tables at standard air; Chapter 2 gives its full definition. Real jobs are hotter, colder or higher, and the air's density changes with them.

    Two things set the density of dry air: its temperature and its absolute pressure, which on a job site is set mostly by altitude. Hot air is thinner because its molecules spread out; air at high elevation is thinner because there is less atmosphere above it pressing down. Engineers express the effect as a density ratio, the actual density divided by standard density.

    A Fan Moves Volume, Not Weight

    Notice what the impeller actually does: each revolution sweeps out roughly the same volume of air no matter how heavy that air is. So at a fixed speed a fan delivers the same CFM in thin air or thick air, while the static pressure it develops and the horsepower it draws both rise and fall in direct proportion to density.

    Density Ratio¶

    \[ DR = \frac{\rho}{0.075} = \frac{530}{T + 460} \times \frac{P_b}{29.92} \]

    where:

    • \( DR \) is the density ratio (1.000 at standard air)
    • \( \rho \) is the actual air density in lb/ft³
    • \( T \) is the air temperature in °F; adding 460 converts it to degrees Rankine, an absolute temperature scale, and 530 is 70°F on that scale
    • \( P_b \) is the absolute pressure in inches of mercury; 29.92 is sea-level standard

    Fan static pressure and brake horsepower scale with the density ratio, while airflow at a given speed does not. That gives the two rules used every day:

    • Reading a catalog for the job: actual SP and BHP = catalog value × DR.
    • Selecting from a catalog for the job: catalog SP to look up = required actual SP ÷ DR.

    The chart that follows plots the density ratio across the temperature and altitude range met in industrial work, and lets you practice both rules. The temperature and altitude sections after it work the examples behind its challenges.

    Air Temperature¶

    Air temperature is the temperature of the air in the airstream at the fan inlet, in °F. It touches a fan selection in two separate ways. The first is density: hotter air is thinner, so a fan handling hot air develops less pressure and draws less power than its standard-air catalog rating at the same speed. The second is materials: heat weakens steel, degrades seals and lubricants, and can overheat bearings and motors, which is why FanPedia devotes a full section to high-temperature construction (FanPedia, pp. 110-116) and treats temperature limits as part of many component descriptions.

    The density side follows directly from the density ratio equation. The table below lists the temperature factor, \( 530 \div (T + 460) \), and the resulting density at sea level for temperatures common in fan work.

    Air temperature (°F) Temperature factor (density ratio at sea level) Density (lb/ft³)
    −20 1.205 0.0903
    40 1.060 0.0795
    70 1.000 0.0750
    100 0.946 0.0710
    200 0.803 0.0602
    250 0.746 0.0560
    400 0.616 0.0462
    600 0.500 0.0375

    Worked Example: A Fan in a 250°F Airstream¶

    A catalog shows a fan delivering a certain CFM at 4.0 in. wg static pressure and some brake horsepower at a stated RPM, all at standard air. The fan will actually handle 250°F air at sea level.

    1. Density ratio: \( 530 \div (250 + 460) = 0.746 \).
    2. Airflow at the catalog RPM: unchanged, because the fan moves volume.
    3. Static pressure actually developed: \( 4.0 \times 0.746 = 2.99 \) in. wg.
    4. Brake horsepower: also multiplied by 0.746, so the fan draws about a quarter less power when hot.

    The fourth step has a catch. When the same fan starts up cold, at perhaps 70°F, it handles air at a density ratio of 1.000 and draws the full standard-air horsepower, about one third more than its hot running load. A motor sized only for the hot condition can overload on every cold start. That cold-start horsepower is part of the motor check in Chapter 25.

    On the materials side, the temperature limits are written into component descriptions throughout FanPedia. Standard double width, double inlet fans with bearings in the airstream are limited to clean air at 130°F (FanPedia, p. 167). Nested inlet vanes are rated for clean airstreams up to 600°F (FanPedia, p. 139). Even the airflow-measuring piezometer ring changes material with temperature: nylon tubing up to 180°F and copper tubing above 180°F (FanPedia, p. 158). Every one of those choices begins with the same question: how hot is the airstream?

    Altitude¶

    Altitude is the elevation of the job site above sea level, in feet. As elevation rises, the atmosphere above the site is thinner, so barometric pressure falls, and with it the density of the air entering the fan. At 5,000 ft, for example, the pressure ratio is 0.832, so even air at 70°F weighs only about 83 percent of what it does at sea level. A fan at a mountain site behaves exactly as if it were handling warm air: same CFM at a given speed, less pressure and less power.

    Worked Example: Selecting a Fan for a 5,000 ft Site¶

    A plant at 5,000 ft elevation needs 2.0 in. wg of actual static pressure from an exhaust fan handling 70°F air.

    1. Density ratio: temperature factor 1.000 times pressure ratio 0.832, so DR = 0.832.
    2. The catalog is printed at standard air, so convert the requirement to catalog conditions by dividing: \( 2.0 \div 0.832 = 2.40 \) in. wg.
    3. Look up the fan in the catalog at the required CFM and 2.40 in. wg, and read the RPM and brake horsepower there.
    4. Convert the catalog BHP back to the site by multiplying by 0.832. The fan runs at the catalog RPM, but the motor sees only 83 percent of the catalog power.

    Selecting at 2.0 in. wg straight from the catalog would leave the fan about 17 percent short of the pressure the system needs. Both temperature and altitude often apply at once, and then the two factors multiply. A dryer exhaust at 120°F on the same 5,000 ft site has \( DR = (530 \div 580) \times 0.832 = 0.914 \times 0.832 = 0.760 \).

    Two Factors, One Habit

    If multiplying here and dividing there feels slippery, that's normal; nearly everyone flips the rule at least once. Anchor it to the one thing you already know for sure, that thin air makes a fan weaker, so a selection for thin air must look up a higher catalog pressure, then work the challenges in the density explorer until the direction is automatic.

    Contaminated Airstreams¶

    A contaminated airstream is an airstream that carries something other than clean air: solid particles such as dust, fibers, chips or grit; liquid droplets or moisture; corrosive gases or mists; flammable vapors or dusts; or fumes hazardous to people. Clean air at an extreme temperature is not, by itself, contaminated, although it raises its own construction questions. Contamination matters because the fan's internal parts are in the airstream: solids erode blades and build up on them until the impeller is out of balance, corrosives eat the metal, moisture collects in the housing, and a spark in a flammable atmosphere can start a fire.

    FanPedia shows several ways the industry responds, and each points to a later chapter:

    • Impeller design. Radial bladed impellers come in paddle, material handling and paper handling versions built for air carrying material (FanPedia, p. 32); see Chapter 9.
    • Arrangement. Arrangement 1 keeps both bearings on the pedestal, out of the airstream, and FanPedia names it for contaminated air applications (FanPedia, p. 162).
    • Accessories. External inlet vanes are described as "used for contaminated airstreams," while nested inlet vanes, which sit inside the inlet funnel, are for clean airstreams (FanPedia, p. 139); see Chapter 18.
    • Discharge and dilution. Lab exhaust fans "dilute contaminated air by drawing in fresh outside air" and send it "up and away from the building" (FanPedia, pp. 4, 6).
    • Construction. Flammable atmospheres call for spark-resistant construction; see Chapter 20.

    Worked Example: Diluting a Lab Exhaust¶

    A lab exhaust fan pulls 5,000 CFM of contaminated air from fume hoods and draws in 3,000 CFM of outside air through its bypass plenum (illustrative values).

    1. Total flow leaving the fan: \( 5{,}000 + 3{,}000 = 8{,}000 \) CFM.
    2. The contaminant is now spread through 8,000 CFM instead of 5,000, so its concentration falls to \( 5{,}000 \div 8{,}000 = 0.625 \), or 62.5 percent of its concentration at the hoods.
    3. The fan must be selected for the 8,000 CFM total, not the 5,000 CFM of hood exhaust, and every wetted part sees the contaminated mixture.

    Ask What Is in the Air Before Picking a Fan

    A common trap is quoting a fan from CFM and static pressure alone, then learning after shipment that the airstream carries grinding dust or solvent vapor. It happens because the request form only had boxes for performance; avoid it by asking every time for temperature, what the air carries, and whether it is flammable or corrosive, and by writing the answers on the quote.

    Putting It Together: What a Fan Delivers¶

    You now have every quantity a fan rating is written in. A complete duty statement, the line on a schedule or specification that tells you what the fan must do, combines them. Using the numbers from this chapter's worked examples, a full duty statement reads:

    12,000 CFM at 3.0 in. wg static pressure, 1,173 RPM, 7.9 BHP, at 0.075 lb/ft³ (70°F, sea level), clean air.

    Read left to right: the airflow volume, the static pressure the system demands at that airflow, the fan speed needed to deliver both, the brake horsepower the shaft will draw, the air density the rating assumes, and what is in the airstream. Leave out the density or the airstream and the line cannot be checked against a catalog; leave out the static pressure and it cannot be selected at all. Chapter 26 shows how to find this line in a performance table. The infographic below lets you check each part.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Air movement is the transfer of a mass of air driven by a pressure difference. Air is heavy: the 2,400 CFM machine-shop example moves about 10,800 pounds of air an hour.
    • The airstream is the moving air and everything it carries. Whether bearings and motors sit in it or out of it drives arrangement choices, as in FanPedia's Arrangement 1 (bearings out of the airstream) versus standard Arrangement 3 (bearings in it, clean air to 130°F).
    • A fan moves air with a rotating impeller and raises pressure only a few percent above atmospheric, so its inlet and outlet CFM are treated as equal.
    • Airflow volume is a rate in cubic feet per minute (CFM), rated at the actual inlet condition. Mass flow equals CFM times density.
    • Air velocity, in feet per minute, equals CFM divided by flow area in square feet: 2,400 CFM in a 16 in by 12 in duct moves at 1,800 feet per minute.
    • Air pressure in fan work is gauge pressure, measured relative to the atmosphere: positive on the discharge side, negative (suction) on the inlet side.
    • Inches of water gauge is the fan industry's pressure unit: 27.7 in. wg equals 1 psi, and 1 in. wg is 5.2 lb/ft².
    • Static pressure describes system resistance. The fan must make up every component's pressure drop; in the worked example it raises static pressure from −1.40 to +1.10 in. wg, a 2.50 in. wg rise.
    • Fan speed in RPM comes from the motor's synchronous speed (3600, 1800, 1200 or 900 at 60 Hz) in direct drive, or from the sheave ratio in belt drive. Airflow rises with speed, pressure with its square, and power with its cube.
    • Horsepower is power: brake horsepower is what the fan shaft needs, and the motor is the next standard size above it, ideally loaded between 50 and 100 percent.
    • Air density at standard conditions is 0.075 lb/ft³. At a fixed speed, CFM stays the same while static pressure and horsepower scale with the density ratio.
    • Air temperature and altitude both thin the air, and their factors multiply. To select from a standard-air catalog, divide the required pressure by the density ratio; to predict actual performance, multiply.
    • A contaminated airstream carries solids, moisture, corrosives, flammables or hazardous fumes. Ask what is in the air before selecting a fan, because it changes the impeller, the arrangement, the accessories and the construction.

    You Can Read a Duty Statement

    You can now take a line like "12,000 CFM at 3.0 in. wg, 1,173 RPM, 7.9 BHP, standard air" and say what every number means, check the horsepower and the velocity yourself, and correct the pressure for a hot airstream or a high-altitude site. That vocabulary is the foundation for every chapter that follows.
