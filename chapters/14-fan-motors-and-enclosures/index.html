    Fan Motors and Enclosures¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter introduces the motors that drive fans: induction and electronically commutated (EC) motors, NEMA frame sizes, C-face and foot mounting, the motor nameplate, service factor, insulation class, and efficiency class. It explains the motor enclosures (open drip proof, totally enclosed fan cooled, totally enclosed air over, totally enclosed non-ventilated, and explosion proof) and when each is used. It also covers conduit boxes, shaft grounding rings, motor bases, and K mount motors. After completing it, readers can read a motor nameplate and choose a suitable enclosure.

    The Motor Line on the Order

    Every fan order carries one dense line about the motor, and a wrong word in it can mean a motor that overheats on a dusty roof or one that is not allowed in a paint booth at all. By the end of this chapter you will read that line field by field and pick the enclosure the job site really needs. Let's follow the airflow.

    Chapter 3 introduced the fan motor as the machine that turns electrical energy into the rotation of the impeller, named its main parts, and showed how a service factor is checked against the fan's brake horsepower. This chapter goes one level deeper. FanPedia (page 71) gives a typical motor specification exactly as it appears on a Twin City Fan order:

    50 HP, 1800 RPM, IN, P, 3 Phase, 60 Frequency, 460 Volt, SD, TEFC, 326T

    Each comma-separated field answers a different question: how much power, how fast, what technology, how efficient, what electrical supply, what duty, what enclosure, and what frame. The sections below take those questions in the order you will need them on a real job: first the motor technology, then the frame and how the motor is mounted, then the ratings on the nameplate, then the enclosure, and finally the accessories and bases that tie the motor to the fan. The chapter ends by decoding the whole line.

    Two Motor Technologies¶

    FanPedia (page 68) says the electric motor is by far the most common way to power a ventilating fan, and that two technologies are used: induction and electronically commutated. The "IN" in the motor line above is the technology field; the other value it can take is "EC".

    Induction Motor¶

    An induction motor is an alternating-current (AC) motor in which the current in the rotor, needed to produce torque, is obtained by electromagnetic induction from the magnetic field of the stator winding (FanPedia page 68). Because the rotor current is induced rather than supplied, an induction motor needs no electrical connection to the rotor: no brushes and no slip rings, which is why it is rugged and why it powers most fans in the industry.

    The AC supply makes the stator's magnetic field rotate. The speed of that rotating field is the synchronous speed, and it depends only on the supply frequency and the number of magnetic poles wound into the stator. The rotor always turns slightly slower than the field, because if it ever caught up, no current would be induced and no torque produced. The difference is called slip. That is why FanPedia (page 71) lists "typical synchronous speeds" of 3600, 1800, 1200 and 900 RPM at 60 Hz, and 3000, 1500, 1000 and 750 RPM at 50 Hz, then adds that actual nominal motor speeds vary by horsepower and manufacturer.

    Synchronous Speed¶

    \[ N_s = \frac{120 \, f}{p} \]

    where:

    • \( N_s \) is the synchronous speed in RPM
    • \( f \) is the supply frequency in hertz (Hz)
    • \( p \) is the number of stator poles (2, 4, 6 or 8 for the speeds listed above)

    Worked Example: The 1800 RPM Motor at 60 Hz and 50 Hz¶

    The motor line above says 1800 RPM and 60 Hz.

    1. Find the poles: \( p = 120 \times 60 / 1800 = 4 \). It is a four-pole motor.
    2. Estimate slip. Suppose the nameplate shows a full-load speed of 1,770 RPM (an illustrative value; the real figure depends on the motor). Slip is \( (1800 - 1770)/1800 = 0.017 \), or about 1.7 percent.
    3. Now suppose the same fan design is quoted for a plant on a 50 Hz grid. A four-pole motor there has a synchronous speed of \( 120 \times 50 / 4 = 1500 \) RPM, which is \( 1500/1800 = 0.83 \) of the 60 Hz speed.
    4. On a direct-drive fan the impeller turns at motor speed, so the fan runs about 17 percent slower. The fan laws in Chapter 25 show that airflow falls in proportion to speed and pressure with the square of speed, so this fan would deliver roughly 83 percent of the airflow at about 69 percent of the pressure.

    Result: frequency is not a detail to fill in later. A 50 Hz job needs its own selection, or a belt drive resized to restore the fan speed.

    EC Motor¶

    An electronically commutated (EC) motor uses on-board electronics to control motor speed (FanPedia page 68). It has two main electronic parts: a rectifier, which converts the AC supply to direct current (DC), and a controller, which sends the right amount of current, in the right direction, at the right time, through each winding. That switching builds magnetic poles in the stator, which interact with permanent magnets in the rotor. Because the electronics, not the grid frequency, decide how fast the field turns, speed can be set by a speed controller or by a 0-10 V signal from an outside source such as a building automation system.

    FanPedia makes two performance claims for EC motors: they run very efficiently even at reduced speeds, and they have a larger usable turndown range than a traditional speed-controllable motor. Turndown is how far below full speed a motor can usefully run. On page 70, FanPedia also describes Twin City Fan's GridPoint EC motor controller, a plug-and-play device that works with TCF's second-generation EC motors. It switches the motor between constant-speed mode and 0-10 V mode and lets a user view and set the operating speed and maximum speed. One controller can be carried from fan to fan, so a technician can commission several EC packages with the same unit.

    Worked Example: Setting an EC Fan from a 0-10 V Signal¶

    An illustrative EC fan has its maximum speed set to 1,700 RPM with the GridPoint controller, and for this example the motor is assumed to scale speed linearly with the control signal, so that 10 V means maximum speed. (Check the installation manual for the actual signal mapping of any real motor.)

    1. The building controller sends 6.0 V. Speed is \( 1700 \times 6.0/10 = 1{,}020 \) RPM.
    2. The balancer wants 10 percent more air. Airflow is proportional to speed, so the target speed is \( 1020 \times 1.10 = 1{,}122 \) RPM, which needs \( 10 \times 1122/1700 = 6.6 \) V.
    3. Compare the belt-driven alternative from Chapter 3: there the same 10 percent change means buying and fitting a new sheave. Here it is a change to a number in the controller.
    Question Induction motor EC motor
    What sets its speed? Supply frequency and number of poles, less slip On-board electronics, set by a controller or a 0-10 V signal
    What is in the rotor? Conductors in which current is induced Permanent magnets
    How is speed varied? An external drive or a sheave change Built in
    Code in the motor line IN EC

    Who Decides the Speed?

    Here is the mental model that sorts every speed question: in an induction motor, the power grid decides the speed and the motor follows it with a little slip; in an EC motor, the electronics decide. Once you ask "who decides the speed?", it is clear why a 50 Hz job changes an induction fan's performance and why an EC fan can be rebalanced from a controller.

    The Motor Nameplate¶

    The motor nameplate is the metal plate fixed to the motor frame that identifies the motor: its manufacturer, serial number and technical characteristics (FanPedia page 69). It is the authority in the field. When a drawing, a submittal and the installed motor disagree, the nameplate tells you what was actually shipped, and a service call almost always starts with a photo of it.

    A nameplate carries the same kinds of information as the motor line on the order: power, speed, electrical supply (phase, frequency and voltage), frame, enclosure, insulation class, service factor and efficiency. FanPedia (page 75) notes that a nameplate with extra information beyond the standard plate can be ordered as a motor modification. The sections that follow explain the frame, insulation, service factor, efficiency and enclosure entries, so that by the end of the chapter every field on the plate has a meaning.

    Frames and Mounting¶

    NEMA Frame Size¶

    A frame size is a standardized code for a motor's mounting dimensions: shaft height, shaft diameter and length, and the bolt pattern of the feet or face. Two motors of the same frame size from different manufacturers bolt into the same place. In imperial units, frames are standardized by NEMA (the National Electrical Manufacturers Association); metric motors use IEC (International Electrotechnical Commission) frames (FanPedia page 74). Typical NEMA sizes run from 42, 48 and 56 through 143, 145, 182, 184, and on up to 444, 445, 447 and 449, with special large-horsepower sizes such as 5011B beyond that.

    Letters after the number describe the frame type. The ones you will see most are listed below.

    • T: the standard post-1964 "T-frame" foot-mount motor (143T, 326T, 444T).
    • C: a face-mounted motor (56C, 184TC); you also need to know whether it has feet.
    • S: a short shaft (284TS, 326TS).
    • U: the older 1952-1964 "U-frame", still seen on some orders.
    • TC: a motor with both feet and a C-face.

    FanPedia also lists D (flange mount), Y (special mounting) and Z (special shaft), all rarely seen on fans. So "326T" in the motor line is a 326 frame of the standard T-frame foot-mount type.

    Shaft Height Is Hiding in the Frame Number

    Here's a shortcut for checking a drawing: on NEMA foot-mounted frames from 143T up, the first two digits divided by 4 give the shaft height in inches, measured from the bottom of the feet. A 326T motor has a shaft height of \( 32/4 = 8 \) inches, and a 184T has \( 18/4 = 4.5 \) inches.

    Foot Mounted Motor and C-Face Motor Mount¶

    A foot mounted motor is supported by feet cast or welded to the bottom of its frame, which bolt down to a motor base, a slide base or a pedestal. It is the default for belt-driven fans and for most direct-drive arrangements, because the feet can sit on any flat, adjustable surface.

    A C-face motor mount uses a machined face on the drive end of the motor, with a pilot and a ring of tapped bolt holes, to bolt the motor directly to a matching face on the fan. The face locates the shaft accurately without shims, so it suits compact direct-drive designs where the motor hangs from the fan. A TC frame has both feet and a C-face.

    FanPedia (page 21) shows all three options on the inlet-mounted direct-drive centrifugal fans, Arrangements 4VI and 4HI: a foot mounted motor, a C-face mounted motor, or a foot/C-face combination motor. When you take an order for one of these arrangements, confirm which mounting the fan is drawn for, because a C-face motor without feet cannot be swapped for a foot-mount motor in the field.

    Ratings on the Nameplate¶

    Insulation Class¶

    The stator windings are copper wires coated with insulation; FanPedia (page 69) describes it as a coat of baked-on varnish around the winding wires. Insulation class is a letter that rates how hot that insulation may run continuously. The classes are A, B, F and H, and the later the letter in the alphabet, the higher the temperature capability. Class A is no longer used by motor manufacturers.

    The commonly published NEMA limits for the hottest spot in the winding are Class B 130 °C, Class F 155 °C and Class H 180 °C (Class A was 105 °C). FanPedia notes that the typical fan motor is rated for Class F insulation but is designed for a Class B temperature rise (the climb from ambient temperature to steady running temperature). The gap between the two is thermal margin that extends winding life. Class H insulation is available as an upgrade from the usual Class F (FanPedia page 75); Chapter 15 covers temperature rise and thermal protection in more detail.

    Motor Service Factor¶

    The service factor (S.F.) is the multiplier above rated horsepower at which a motor may be used (FanPedia page 69). A 100 HP motor with a 1.15 service factor may be run at 115 HP. Other common values are 1.25 and 1.0, and 1.0 applies when the motor is operated on a variable frequency drive (VFD).

    Chapter 3 showed the basic check: the fan's brake horsepower (BHP) at the design point should be at or below the nameplate horsepower, and the service factor is a margin, not extra capacity to plan around. Running in the service factor means running hotter, which spends the insulation's thermal margin. The case where engineers get caught is not the design point at all but a condition the fan passes through, such as a cold start on a hot-gas fan.

    Worked Example: The Cold Start That Overloads the Motor¶

    An illustrative fan exhausts air at 200 °F. At that temperature its selection shows 7.0 BHP, and the submittal proposes a 7-1/2 HP motor with a 1.15 service factor.

    1. At operating temperature, \( 7.0 \le 7.5 \) HP. The motor looks right.
    2. At startup the fan is full of 70 °F air. From Chapter 2, density varies inversely with absolute temperature, so the cold air is denser by \( (200 + 460)/(70 + 460) = 660/530 = 1.245 \).
    3. Fan power is proportional to density at the same speed and airflow, so cold BHP is \( 7.0 \times 1.245 = 8.7 \) BHP.
    4. The most the motor may carry is \( 7.5 \times 1.15 = 8.6 \) HP. The cold-start load is above even the service-factor limit, and on a VFD (service factor 1.0) the limit is only 7.5 HP.

    Result: choose a 10 HP motor, or reduce the load during warm-up, for example with a damper. The motor must be sized for the worst condition the fan will see, not the design temperature alone.

    Motor Efficiency Class¶

    Motor efficiency is the ratio of the useful work a motor delivers to the electrical energy it takes in (FanPedia page 71). The rest becomes heat in the windings, core and bearings. An efficiency class is a named band of minimum efficiencies that a motor must meet.

    FanPedia (page 72) gives two families of names. Twin City Fan designates motors Standard Efficiency (comparable to NEMA High Efficiency) or Premium Efficiency (comparable to NEMA Premium Efficiency). Worldwide, IEC classes are IE1 (standard), IE2 (high, comparable to TCF Standard), IE3 (premium, comparable to TCF Premium) and IE4 (super premium, listed by FanPedia as a future designation). The "P" in the motor line is this field: Premium Efficiency.

    TCF designation NEMA equivalent IEC equivalent
    Standard Efficiency NEMA High Efficiency IE2
    Premium Efficiency NEMA Premium Efficiency IE3
    (not used) (not used) IE4, super premium

    Load matters as much as class. FanPedia notes that most motors are designed to run at 50 to 100 percent of rated load, with peak efficiency near 75 percent; a 10 HP motor therefore has an acceptable load range of 5 to 10 HP and peaks around 7.5 HP. Efficiency drops sharply below about half load, so a badly oversized motor wastes energy even if its class is premium.

    Worked Example: What Two Points of Efficiency Are Worth¶

    A fan draws 40 BHP and runs 6,000 hours a year. Compare two illustrative full-load efficiencies, 93.0 percent and 95.0 percent, at an illustrative electricity price of $0.10 per kWh.

    1. Shaft power: \( 40 \times 0.7457 = 29.8 \) kW (FanPedia's conversion, kW = HP × 0.7457).
    2. Electrical input: \( 29.8/0.930 = 32.1 \) kW versus \( 29.8/0.950 = 31.4 \) kW, a difference of about 0.67 kW.
    3. Annual energy: \( 0.67 \times 6000 = 4{,}020 \) kWh, or about $400 a year.

    Over a motor life of many years, that difference usually pays for the more efficient motor, which is why specifications often require premium efficiency. Use the real efficiencies from the motor data sheet when you run this for a customer.

    Motor Enclosures¶

    A motor enclosure is the casing style around the internal rotating parts of the motor (FanPedia page 71). It decides two things at once: how the motor gets rid of its heat, and how well the windings are protected from whatever is in the surrounding air. FanPedia (page 73) lists five enclosures used by Twin City Fan: ODP, TEFC, explosion proof, TEAO and TENV. The enclosure field in the motor line above is "TEFC".

    Two ideas tie the enclosure to its neighbors on the order. Intended duty describes the operating environment the motor is built for: GD (general duty) for normal, clean and cool air; SD (severe duty) and IEEE 841 for process industries; and DFG for explosion-proof motors in hazardous locations (FanPedia page 73; Chapter 15 covers duty in depth). The IP rating is the international (IEC) code for protection against solids and water; the higher the two digits, the better sealed the motor. FanPedia maps each NEMA enclosure to the IP ratings and duties that go with it, which matters when a specification is written in metric terms.

    ODP Motor¶

    An open drip proof (ODP) motor has an internal fan that pulls air in, blows it across the windings inside the motor, and lets it exit opposite the drive end (FanPedia page 73). The surrounding air passes directly over the windings, which makes ODP motors cool well and cost less. The openings face downward or are shielded so that water dripping from above at a modest angle does not fall in, which is where the name comes from. The same openings let in anything carried by the air, so FanPedia states plainly that ODP motors should not be used in dirty or wet atmospheres. The metric equivalent is IP22, and the duty is general duty.

    Worked Example: Is ODP Acceptable Here?¶

    A contractor asks for ODP motors on two belt-driven Arrangement 9 fans to save money.

    1. Fan A is a supply fan in a clean, dry, indoor mechanical room. The air around the motor is the clean, cool air that general duty assumes. ODP is acceptable.
    2. Fan B is a rooftop exhaust fan whose motor sits outside under the weather, exposed to rain and blowing dust. That is both a wet and a dirty atmosphere, and the ODP motor would draw both through its windings. ODP is not acceptable; Fan B needs a totally enclosed motor.
    3. Record the reason on the quote, so that the substitution is not made later on Fan B to match Fan A.

    TEFC Motor¶

    A totally enclosed fan cooled (TEFC) motor keeps the windings sealed inside the frame. An external fan on the back of the shaft pulls air in through the fan cover and blows it over the outside surface of the motor only (FanPedia page 73); the ribs on a TEFC frame give that air more surface to carry heat away. Because outside air never touches the windings, TEFC is the usual choice for dirty, damp or outdoor locations, and it is the most common enclosure on industrial fans.

    TEFC covers a wide range of protection. FanPedia's table maps TEFC to IP44, IP54, IP55 and IP56, and to general duty, severe duty, IEEE 45 (marine) and IEEE 841 duty at the higher ratings.

    Worked Example: A Specification Written in IP Ratings¶

    A European consultant's specification calls for "IP55 motors, general purpose, non-hazardous area" on a TCF fan.

    1. Find IP55 in the FanPedia table: it maps to TEFC with general or severe duty, and also to the explosion-proof enclosure.
    2. The area is non-hazardous, so the explosion-proof enclosure is not needed and would add cost.
    3. The motor line becomes "... GD, TEFC ...". If a later revision raises the requirement to IP56 for a marine installation, the table points to TEFC with IEEE 45 duty instead.

    TEAO Motor¶

    A totally enclosed air over (TEAO) motor is sealed like a TEFC motor but has no cooling fan of its own. It relies on the fan's own airstream for cooling: FanPedia (page 73) describes it as a motor in the air stream of the fan, either with the impeller mounted on the motor shaft or placed in the air stream of a belt-driven fan or blower. Direct-drive axial and inline fans, where the motor sits inside the housing, are the typical case. The catch is that a TEAO motor is only cooled while air is moving over it, so it suits fans that always run with airflow, not ones that may run against a closed damper.

    TENV Motor¶

    A totally enclosed non-ventilated (TENV) motor has no cooling fan at all, inside or outside. It dissipates its heat through the frame and body of the motor only (FanPedia page 73). Without forced cooling, a TENV motor runs hotter and usually needs a larger frame than a standard motor of the same horsepower. It is chosen when an external fan or fan cover would be a problem, for example where lint or fibers would clog a fan cover, and the larger frame is acceptable. FanPedia maps TENV to IP56 with general or severe duty.

    Explosion Proof Motor¶

    An explosion proof (EXPL) motor has an external fan that helps cool it, like a TEFC motor, but its internal parts are engineered so that an arc or explosion inside the motor cannot ignite a dangerous surrounding atmosphere containing flammable dust or gas (FanPedia page 73). Its intended duty code is DFG, and the hazardous location it is built for is specified under the National Electrical Code (NEC) by group number. FanPedia lists the groups TCF commonly sees as Group D (gases), Group F and Group G (dusts, with Group G covering food-industry dusts), and refers to the NEC for full definitions along with class and division ratings. FanPedia also notes that ATEX applications, the European system for explosive atmospheres, require a special motor.

    Worked Example: Exhaust from a Paint Spray Room¶

    A customer needs an exhaust fan for a room where solvent-based paint is sprayed. The plant's engineer has classified the area around the fan as a hazardous location for flammable vapors.

    1. The motor sits inside the classified area, so it needs an explosion-proof enclosure with DFG duty for the stated gas group. Ask the customer for the class, division and group in writing; the classification is theirs to make, not the fan supplier's.
    2. Ask what is in the airstream. Solvent vapor passes through the fan itself, so the fan also needs spark resistant construction (Chapter 20), and a belt-driven arrangement such as Arrangement 9 keeps the motor and bearings out of the airstream.
    3. If the job is in Europe under ATEX, flag that a special motor is required and do not substitute a standard EXPL motor.

    An Explosion Proof Motor Does Not Make a Spark Resistant Fan

    A common trap is to order an EXPL motor and assume the whole fan is now safe for flammable air. The motor enclosure only protects against ignition from inside the motor; a rubbing impeller or a dropped tool can still strike a spark in the housing. Whenever the motor needs EXPL, check the airstream separately and add spark resistant construction when the gas or dust passes through the fan.

    Two Questions Settle Most Enclosure Choices

    If five enclosures, several IP codes and a handful of duty codes feel like a lot to hold at once, that is normal; most people need a few real orders before it sticks. Reduce it to two questions you already know how to ask: is the area classified as hazardous, and what is in the air around the motor? Then try the sorter below and see how many jobs those two questions decide.

    4 Belt-driven fan whose motor is mounted in the fan's air stream. TEAO FanPedia lists the air stream of a belt-driven fan or blower as a TEAO location.
    5 Textile plant where lint would clog an external fan cover; the customer accepts a larger frame. TENV TENV has no fan or fan cover to clog; the cost is a hotter motor in a larger frame.
    6 Exhaust from a solvent paint spray room; the area around the motor is classified for flammable gases and vapors. EXPL A classified hazardous location for gases needs an explosion-proof (DFG) motor for its NEC group.
    7 Grain-handling area classified as a hazardous location for combustible dust. EXPL Combustible dust locations are NEC hazardous locations; the motor must be explosion proof for the dust group.
    8 Paper mill process fan; the specification calls for IEEE 841 motors. TEFC FanPedia maps IEEE 841 duty to the TEFC enclosure (IP56).
    9 Metric specification: IP55, general purpose, non-hazardous area. TEFC IP55 maps to TEFC or EXPL; with no hazardous location, TEFC is the right choice and EXPL adds needless cost.
    10 Metric specification: IP22, clean indoor location. ODP IP22 is the metric equivalent of ODP in FanPedia's table.

    Motor Accessories¶

    Conduit Box¶

    The conduit box is the motor's power connection point: a small enclosure on the side or top of the frame where the supply wiring, run in conduit, connects to the motor leads (FanPedia page 69). Its location is named looking from the drive side of the motor, at the shaft: F1 and F2 are the two side locations and F3 is on top; FanPedia (page 69) says the box is usually at F1 or F2. Its motor modifications page (page 75) gives F1 or F3 as standard; F2 is standard on IEC frames but is a special modification for NEMA.

    The conduit box location matters because it has to clear the fan. On centrifugal fans with motor position L, FanPedia (page 52) says the motor is normally ordered with an F1 conduit box, and F2 is selected if there are clearance issues; some swingout arrangements must have F2 (page 53). Chapter 13 covers conduit box positions with the motor position codes. Related options include extended leads (48 inches is standard) and an externally mounted conduit box at the cable opening outside the fan (FanPedia page 75), which is useful when the motor sits inside the housing.

    Shaft Grounding Ring¶

    A variable frequency drive (VFD) varies motor speed by switching the supply voltage on and off very rapidly. That fast switching can induce a voltage on the motor shaft. The charge builds until it discharges to ground through the easiest path, which is usually across the thin film of grease in the motor bearings. Each discharge burns a tiny pit in the bearing race; over months, millions of them leave a washboard pattern called fluting, and the bearing becomes noisy and fails early. FanPedia (page 72) notes that precautions may be needed against these shaft currents, citing NEMA MG-1 Part 31.

    A shaft grounding ring is a ring of conductive fibers mounted around the motor shaft that gives those charges a low-resistance path to ground, so they bypass the bearings. FanPedia (page 75) lists it as a motor modification designed to protect motor bearings from electrical charges induced by a VFD, available in internal and external versions. The related option is insulated bearings, whose outer race is coated with insulating material to block the current path through the bearing.

    Worked Example: Adding a VFD to an Existing Fan¶

    A plant wants to add a VFD to a 40 HP fan that has run across the line for years. The order review goes like this.

    1. Check the load against the new service factor. On a VFD the service factor is 1.0 (FanPedia page 69), so the fan's maximum BHP must now be at or below 40, not 46.
    2. Protect the bearings. The existing motor has no shaft grounding ring, so quote one, internal or external depending on the motor, and ask the motor supplier whether insulated bearings are recommended for this frame.
    3. Check the motor's suitability for inverter use; Chapter 15 covers inverter-duty motors.
    4. Record that the VFD was added, so a later bearing failure is investigated for shaft current rather than blamed on lubrication alone.

    Electricity Takes the Easy Path

    Notice that a shaft grounding ring does not stop shaft voltage; it gives the charge an easier way home than through the bearing. Insulated bearings work from the other side by making the bearing a harder path. Think of the two as "open a better path" and "close the bad one".

    Motor Bases and Mounting on the Fan¶

    Motor Base¶

    A motor base is the structure that supports the motor on the fan or its base and, on a belt drive, lets the motor move to set belt tension and alignment. The base type depends on the fan type and on the motor's frame size. FanPedia (pages 76 to 78) groups them as follows; Chapter 15 covers their adjustment hardware in detail.

    • NEMA type slide base: for motors from 48 to 445 frame, on Arrangements 1, 3, 9, 9F, 9H and 9ST, plenum fans and pedestal plug fans.
    • Heavy-duty slide rails: two rails per motor, for very large 440, 500 and 5000 series frames.
    • Automotive pivot base: used mostly in automotive applications, on Arrangement 9H, 9SS swingout and specially mounted Arrangement 3 fans.
    • Adjustable bases, standard post mount: 48 to 215 frame; on tubular centrifugal and axial fans (Arrangement 9) and plenum and plug fans; saddle or flat mount.
    • Adjustable bases, pivot/bolted design: 254 to 445 frame; saddle or flat mount.
    • Bolt-on mount: 48 to 286 frame; saddle mount; on tubeaxial, vaneaxial and centaxial fans in Arrangement 9.

    Worked Example: Matching the Base to the Motor¶

    Three belt-driven fans on one job each need a motor base.

    1. An Arrangement 1 centrifugal fan with a 326T motor: the NEMA slide base covers 48 to 445 frame on Arrangement 1, so 326T fits.
    2. An Arrangement 9 tubular centrifugal fan with a 256T motor: the standard post-mount adjustable base stops at 215 frame, so use the pivot/bolted design, which covers 254 to 445.
    3. An Arrangement 9 vaneaxial fan with a 184T motor: the bolt-on mount covers 48 to 286 frame on vaneaxial fans and uses a saddle to sit on the round housing.

    If the motor size changes during submittal review, for example from 215T to 254T, recheck the base: the change crosses the line between the post-mount and pivot/bolted designs.

    Motor Base Saddle¶

    A motor base saddle is the curved support that lets a motor base sit on a round fan housing. Tubeaxial, vaneaxial and inline fans have cylindrical housings, so a flat base cannot bolt directly to them; the saddle is shaped to the housing's curve on one side and carries the flat motor base on the other. FanPedia's exploded views of belt-driven tubeaxial, vaneaxial and inline centrifugal and mixed flow fans label it on each (pages 15 to 17), and the adjustable bases on page 78 are offered as either "saddle mount" or "flat mount", the flat version being for fans with flat mounting surfaces.

    K Mount Motor¶

    A K mount motor, also known as a piggyback or scroll mounted motor, is mounted directly on the scroll of a centrifugal fan's housing rather than on the floor or a separate base (FanPedia page 54). It uses an adjustable motor base (post mount or pivot/bolted design) and is used on SWSI and DWDI Arrangement 3 fans. Because the motor rides on the curved scroll, its location is described by a clock position around the housing; FanPedia shows positions such as 9, 10:30, 12, 1:30 and 3 o'clock for upblast (UBD), top horizontal (THD), downblast (DBD) and bottom horizontal (BHD) discharges. A K mount keeps the motor and fan together as one compact assembly instead of placing the motor on the floor beside the fan.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • An induction motor runs a little below its synchronous speed, \( N_s = 120f/p \), so the supply frequency sets fan speed on a direct drive; an EC motor sets its own speed electronically from a controller or a 0-10 V signal.
    • The motor nameplate is the field authority for what was shipped; the motor line on the order lists power, speed, technology, efficiency, electrical supply, duty, enclosure and frame.
    • A NEMA frame size standardizes mounting dimensions; the letters after the number (T, C, S, U, TC) describe the frame type. Motors are foot mounted, C-face mounted, or both.
    • Insulation class (B, F, H) rates winding temperature; fan motors typically have Class F insulation with a Class B rise. Service factor is margin, not capacity, and drops to 1.0 on a VFD. Check the worst operating condition, such as a cold start.
    • TCF efficiency classes are Standard (NEMA High, IE2) and Premium (NEMA Premium, IE3); efficiency peaks near 75 percent load.
    • The motor enclosure sets cooling and protection: ODP for clean, dry air; TEFC for dirty, damp or outdoor locations; TEAO in the fan's airstream; TENV with no fan; explosion proof for NEC-classified hazardous locations, which still requires a separate check of the fan's own spark resistance.
    • The conduit box location (F1, F2, F3) must clear the fan; a shaft grounding ring protects motor bearings from VFD-induced shaft currents.
    • A motor base is chosen by fan type and frame size; a motor base saddle seats it on a round housing; a K mount motor rides on the scroll of an Arrangement 3 centrifugal fan.

    You Can Read the Motor Line

    You can now take a line like "50 HP, 1800 RPM, IN, P, 3 Phase, 60 Frequency, 460 Volt, SD, TEFC, 326T", say what every field means, and decide whether that enclosure belongs on the job site in front of you. That is the skill behind every motor question on a quote.
