    Discharge Positions and Rotation¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter introduces the conventions used to describe a fan's orientation. It establishes the rule that a fan is viewed from the drive side, then covers discharge positions and angles (up blast, top horizontal, bottom horizontal, down blast, and the angular discharges), inlet box position, rotation, inline configurations, and the standard W, X, Y, Z and axial motor positions. After completing it, readers can describe which way a fan discharges and where its motor sits.

    Why Orientation Needs a Shared Language¶

    A fan that arrives on a job site with its outlet pointing the wrong way is not a small problem. The housing of a centrifugal fan is cast or welded around a fixed scroll, so the outlet cannot simply be turned to meet the duct. The installing contractor either rebuilds the ductwork, returns the fan, or waits for a replacement, and all three cost time and money. Almost every one of these mistakes traces back to two people describing the same fan from opposite sides.

    This chapter gives you the vocabulary that prevents that mistake. You will learn the single viewing rule the industry uses, then the names for which way the impeller turns, which way the outlet points, where an inlet box sits, and where the motor goes. Chapter 13 turns these names into the compact codes written on orders; this chapter makes sure you can see what each code means before you write one.

    Point It the Right Way the First Time

    Every order you write, quote you check, or drawing you approve carries a rotation, a discharge and a motor position. Get them right and the fan bolts straight onto the duct; get one wrong and a crane sits idle while the outlet points at the wrong wall. By the end of this chapter you will read any fan's orientation as easily as reading a clock. Let's follow the airflow.

    Viewed From the Drive Side¶

    Before you can name a fan's rotation or discharge, you must agree where the observer is standing. A fan housing seen from one side is the mirror image of the same housing seen from the other side: the outlet that points right from one side points left from the other, and a wheel that turns clockwise from one side turns counterclockwise from the other. Without a fixed viewpoint, every orientation word is ambiguous.

    The convention that resolves this is to describe a centrifugal fan viewed from the drive side. The drive side is the side of the fan where the drive is located: the sheave and belts on a belt-driven fan, the coupling on a coupled fan, or the motor on a direct-drive fan. FanPedia's discharge pages show a person standing beside the bearing pedestal and looking at the housing, under the heading "Rotation View From Drive Side" (FanPedia pp. 46-49). Rotation, discharge position, inlet box position and the centrifugal motor positions are all read from that spot.

    Two practical consequences follow. First, on a single-inlet fan with the drive on the pedestal, the drive side is the side opposite the inlet, so an installer standing at the inlet sees everything mirrored. Second, on a double-width, double-inlet (DWDI) fan, both faces of the housing have inlets, so "the inlet side" means nothing; only the end of the shaft carrying the drive identifies the viewpoint.

    Worked example. A field technician sends a photo taken from the inlet of an Arrangement 1 single-inlet fan. In the photo the outlet is at the top of the housing and points to the technician's left, and the rotation arrow on the housing appears counterclockwise. The order says CW, Top Horizontal. Is the fan wrong?

    1. The photo was taken from the inlet side, which on this fan is opposite the drive side.
    2. Mirror the photo left to right. The outlet now points right, and the rotation arrow now reads clockwise.
    3. From the drive side, the fan is clockwise with a top horizontal outlet pointing right, which is exactly what was ordered.

    The fan is correct. The only error was the viewpoint, which is the most common cause of a false "wrong fan" report.

    Ask Where the Photo Was Taken

    A common trap is comparing a job-site photo or sketch with the order without asking which side it was taken from. Before you agree that a fan is wrong, find the sheave, coupling or motor in the picture; if it is on the far side of the housing, mirror the image before you compare. Check it from the drive side.

    Rotation¶

    Rotation is the direction in which the impeller turns. For centrifugal fans it is stated as clockwise (CW) or counterclockwise (CCW), judged from the drive side. Rotation is not a free choice once the housing is built: a centrifugal impeller must turn toward the outlet, so that the air spinning off the blades travels around the scroll and leaves through the outlet. A backward-inclined or airfoil wheel turned the wrong way still moves some air, but at a fraction of its rated performance, while drawing power and making noise.

    Axial impellers use a different reference. FanPedia's airflow and rotation pages state that axial rotation "is determined by viewing the impeller from the discharge side of the fan," and name the result left-hand (LH) or right-hand (RH) rotation (FanPedia pp. 37-41). The same pages warn that airflow direction and rotation must both be correct for the fan to deliver full performance, and that an impeller turning the wrong way will not move air in the proper direction. Chapter 13 develops the axial convention fully; for now, remember that the viewing rule depends on the fan family.

    The following list summarizes the two viewpoints just described:

    • Centrifugal fans: view from the drive side; rotation is CW or CCW.
    • Axial impellers: view from the discharge side; rotation is LH or RH.

    Worked example. After a motor replacement on a belt-driven SWSI fan, a facilities technician bumps the motor to check rotation. Standing at the sheave guard (the drive side), they see the wheel turning counterclockwise. The nameplate and order say CW.

    1. The observer is on the drive side, so no mirroring is needed: the wheel really is turning CCW.
    2. The required rotation is CW, so the wheel is turning backward.
    3. On a three-phase motor, interchanging any two of the three supply leads reverses the motor. The electrician swaps two leads, and the next bump shows clockwise rotation.

    Had the technician stood at the inlet, the wheel would have appeared clockwise and the backward wheel would have been missed. That fan would then have run at reduced airflow with no obvious fault, which is the expensive version of this mistake.

    Discharge Position¶

    The discharge position of a centrifugal fan is the direction in which air leaves the housing outlet, described from the drive side. Because the housing scroll wraps around the impeller in the direction of rotation, the outlet always sits where the spinning air naturally leaves the wheel. That ties discharge position to rotation: a given outlet direction can be built on a clockwise housing or on a counterclockwise housing, and the two are mirror images that are not interchangeable.

    FanPedia defines eight standard discharge positions for single-width, single-inlet (SWSI) centrifugal fans, each 45 degrees apart (FanPedia pp. 46-47). Each has a full name and a three-letter abbreviation:

    • UBD, Upblast: the outlet points straight up.
    • TAU, Top Angular Up: the outlet is near the top and points upward at 45 degrees.
    • THD, Top Horizontal: the outlet is at the top and points horizontally.
    • TAD, Top Angular Down: the outlet is near the top and points downward at 45 degrees.
    • DBD, Downblast: the outlet points straight down.
    • BAD, Bottom Angular Down: the outlet is near the bottom and points downward at 45 degrees.
    • BHD, Bottom Horizontal: the outlet is at the bottom and points horizontally.
    • BAU, Bottom Angular Up: the outlet is near the bottom and points upward at 45 degrees.

    The name alone does not tell you whether a horizontal outlet points left or right. That comes from the rotation. On a clockwise fan, air at the top of the wheel is moving to the right, so a CW top horizontal outlet points right; on a counterclockwise fan, the same outlet points left. A complete discharge description therefore always pairs a rotation with a position.

    Discharge Angle¶

    The discharge angle is the number of degrees from the upblast position to the actual outlet direction, measured in the direction of rotation. Upblast is both 0 and 360 degrees, and FanPedia writes it as 360. Each step of 45 degrees moves the outlet one position around the housing, so the angle and the name carry the same information: FanPedia lists "CW 90" as also known as "Clockwise 90°" for Top Horizontal, and so on around the circle (FanPedia pp. 46-47).

    Measuring in the direction of rotation is what makes the system work for both hands. A CW 90 outlet and a CCW 90 outlet are both top horizontal, one pointing right and the other pointing left. If you want the outlet direction as a single compass-style angle, measured clockwise from straight up as seen from the drive side, it is:

    \[ \theta = \begin{cases} N & \text{for clockwise rotation} \\ 360^\circ - N & \text{for counterclockwise rotation} \end{cases} \]

    where \( N \) is the discharge angle in degrees (45 to 360) and \( \theta \) is the direction the air leaves, with 0 degrees straight up, 90 degrees to the right, 180 degrees straight down, and 270 degrees to the left (360 is treated as 0).

    Worked example. A submittal lists a fan as CCW 135. Which way does the air leave?

    1. The angle is \( N = 135^\circ \) and the rotation is counterclockwise.
    2. \( \theta = 360^\circ - 135^\circ = 225^\circ \), which is down and to the left as seen from the drive side.
    3. An outlet that points downward at 45 degrees from near the top of the housing is Top Angular Down, so CCW 135 is TAD with the air heading down and to the left.

    A CW 135 fan is also TAD, but with \( \theta = 135^\circ \) the air heads down and to the right. The two housings are mirror images.

    The table below gathers the eight positions with their angles and the outlet direction for each rotation, using the rule you just applied.

    Abbreviation Name Discharge angle Air leaves toward (CW) Air leaves toward (CCW)
    UBD Upblast 360 Straight up Straight up
    TAU Top Angular Up 45 Up and right Up and left
    THD Top Horizontal 90 Right Left
    TAD Top Angular Down 135 Down and right Down and left
    DBD Downblast 180 Straight down Straight down
    BAD Bottom Angular Down 225 Down and left Down and right
    BHD Bottom Horizontal 270 Left Right
    BAU Bottom Angular Up 315 Up and left Up and right

    The Angle Follows the Wheel

    Notice that the discharge angle is not a compass bearing; it counts degrees in whichever direction the wheel turns, starting from upblast. That is why one table of names serves both rotations, and why "CW 90" and "CCW 90" are the same name with mirror-image housings.

    Even upblast and downblast, which point the same way for both rotations, are not interchangeable between hands. On a CW upblast fan the outlet sits on the left side of the housing top, because that is where air on a clockwise wheel is moving upward; on a CCW upblast fan it sits on the right. Duct centerlines on a drawing reveal the difference immediately.

    Before working through the interactive explorer below, make sure the three ideas it uses are clear: rotation is read from the drive side, the discharge angle counts from upblast in the direction of rotation, and a view from the opposite side is a mirror image.

    Up Blast Discharge Position¶

    The up blast discharge position (UBD, 360 degrees) points the outlet straight up. It is the natural choice when a fan exhausts vertically through a roof or into a stack, because the air leaves the housing already moving in the direction it must travel and no elbow is needed at the outlet. Avoiding an elbow close to the outlet matters because a fan's outlet velocity profile is uneven for several duct diameters downstream, and an elbow placed in that region costs pressure that the fan rating does not account for. Chapter 25 treats this loss, called system effect, in detail.

    Worked example. A dust collector exhaust fan sits on a pad beside a building and must discharge into a vertical stack directly above it.

    1. The air must leave going up, so the discharge position is UBD.
    2. The stack foundation was poured before the fan was selected, and from the drive side its centerline sits to the left of the fan shaft.
    3. For UBD the outlet sits on the left side of the housing top for CW and on the right side for CCW, so only the CW housing puts the outlet over the existing foundation. The order reads CW 360.
    4. Had the order been written as "upblast" with no rotation, the factory could have built either hand, and half the time the outlet would miss the stack.

    Top Horizontal Discharge¶

    Top horizontal discharge (THD, 90 degrees) puts the outlet at the top of the housing, pointing sideways. It suits a fan that feeds an overhead horizontal duct, a common layout in mechanical rooms and plants where ducts run near the ceiling. Because the air leaves at the top of the housing and travels horizontally, the duct can start at roughly the height of the housing top without an elbow.

    THD also has a practical interaction with the motor. FanPedia's technical descriptions for Arrangements 1, 3 and 3F note a height restriction for THD: the motor "may not fit below the discharge" in one of the four standard motor positions, a limit described later in this chapter and in detail in Chapter 13 (FanPedia pp. 162-163).

    Worked example. A supply fan in a penthouse must feed a duct running to the east at the ceiling. The fan will be set so that, standing on the drive side, the observer faces north.

    1. Facing north, east is on the observer's right.
    2. The air must leave horizontally at the top, toward the right: THD.
    3. From the discharge table, a CW top horizontal outlet throws air to the right. The order reads CW 90 (CW THD).
    4. Had the fan been set with the drive side facing south, east would be on the observer's left, and the same duct would need CCW 90.

    The same duct can need either hand, depending on which way the fan is set on its base, which is why layout drawings must show the drive side.

    Bottom Horizontal Discharge¶

    Bottom horizontal discharge (BHD, 270 degrees) puts the outlet at the bottom of the housing, pointing sideways. It suits fans that feed a duct running at floor level or below grade, a tunnel, or an under-floor plenum. A bottom horizontal outlet keeps the duct low, which helps when headroom is limited or when the discharge feeds equipment at grade.

    BHD has its own interaction with the motor. FanPedia notes that on Arrangements 1, 3 and 3F a BHD outlet can collide with the motor in one standard position, because the motor "interferes with outlet opening of housing" (FanPedia pp. 162-163).

    Worked example. A boiler forced-draft fan must deliver air into a windbox inlet located low on the boiler front, to the observer's left when they stand on the fan's drive side.

    1. The air must leave horizontally at the bottom of the housing: BHD.
    2. From the discharge table, a CW bottom horizontal outlet throws air to the left.
    3. The order reads CW 270 (CW BHD).
    4. Because a CW BHD outlet projects to the left at floor level, the motor cannot be placed on the left side of the fan in the position the outlet occupies. The motor position must be chosen with the outlet in mind, as the motor position section below shows.

    Down Blast Discharge Position¶

    The down blast discharge position (DBD, 180 degrees) points the outlet straight down. It suits a fan mounted above the space it serves: a fan on a platform or mezzanine discharging down into a duct or chamber below it, or a fan supported on structural steel above a process. A downblast fan's outlet usually connects through a floor opening or into a plenum, so the support frame must leave room around the outlet flange.

    Like upblast, downblast points the same direction for both rotations, but the housings differ: a CW downblast outlet sits on the right side of the housing bottom, and a CCW downblast outlet on the left, because those are the sides where the wheel moves air downward.

    Worked example. A fan on a mezzanine must discharge through a floor sleeve into a supply chamber below. The sleeve is already cast in the slab, and the architect's drawing shows the sleeve centerline offset to the right of the fan shaft when viewed from the drive side.

    1. Air must leave straight down: DBD.
    2. The outlet must sit to the right of the shaft.
    3. A CW downblast outlet sits on the right of the housing bottom, so the order reads CW 180.
    4. Ordering CCW 180 would put the outlet on the left, off the sleeve, and the slab would need a new opening.

    Angular Discharge¶

    An angular discharge is any of the four positions that point the outlet at 45 degrees rather than straight up, straight down or horizontally: TAU (45), TAD (135), BAD (225) and BAU (315). Angular discharges let the outlet meet a duct that rises or falls at an angle, or clear an obstruction without a full elbow. They are named by where the outlet is (top or bottom of the housing) and which way the air heads (up or down). Not every fan type offers all four: FanPedia's DWDI discharge pages list seven positions and omit Bottom Angular Down, a limit covered in Chapter 13 (FanPedia pp. 48-49).

    Top Angular Up Discharge¶

    Top angular up discharge (TAU, 45 degrees) places the outlet near the top of the housing with the air heading upward at 45 degrees, one step past upblast in the direction of rotation. For a CW fan the air heads up and to the right; for a CCW fan, up and to the left. A TAU outlet is a good fit when an exhaust duct must climb toward a roof penetration that is offset from the fan, because it replaces an elbow with the housing's own geometry.

    Top Angular Down Discharge¶

    Top angular down discharge (TAD, 135 degrees) places the outlet near the top of the housing with the air heading downward at 45 degrees. For a CW fan the air heads down and to the right; for a CCW fan, down and to the left. Because the outlet projects down beside the housing, it can collide with a motor placed on that side; FanPedia lists TAD among the discharges that restrict motor position on Arrangements 1, 3 and 3F (FanPedia pp. 162-163).

    Bottom Angular Up Discharge¶

    Bottom angular up discharge (BAU, 315 degrees) places the outlet near the bottom of the housing with the air heading upward at 45 degrees. For a CW fan the air heads up and to the left; for a CCW fan, up and to the right. BAU is one step short of upblast in the direction of rotation, and it suits a duct that leaves low and climbs away from the fan at an angle, for instance to rise over a walkway beside the unit.

    Inlet Box Position¶

    Some centrifugal fans are fitted with an inlet box, a sheet-metal box attached to the fan inlet that turns the incoming air 90 degrees so the inlet duct can approach from the side, top or bottom instead of along the shaft. An inlet box also lets the bearing on an Arrangement 3 fan sit outside the airstream; Chapter 18 covers inlet boxes as accessories. The inlet box position states where the box's opening faces.

    FanPedia defines inlet box position by an angle measured around the fan as viewed from the drive side, in 45-degree steps from 45 to 360, with 360 at the top and the angle increasing clockwise (FanPedia p. 144). Unlike the discharge angle, this angle does not change direction with rotation: it is a fixed clock around the housing. Each position also has a description of how the air enters:

    • 45 — Angular Down Intake (box at the upper right)
    • 90 — Horizontal Right Intake (box on the right)
    • 135 — Angular Up Intake (box at the lower right)
    • 180 — Bottom Up Intake (box at the bottom)
    • 225 — Angular Up Intake (box at the lower left)
    • 270 — Horizontal Left Intake (box on the left)
    • 315 — Angular Down Intake (box at the upper left)
    • 360 — Top Down Intake (box at the top)

    The descriptions repeat for the angular positions: 45 and 315 are both Angular Down Intake, and 135 and 225 are both Angular Up Intake. The number, not the description, is what pins down the side.

    Worked example. A fan with an inlet box must take air from a horizontal duct that arrives from the observer's left when standing on the drive side, and the fan's outlet is CW top horizontal, pointing right.

    1. The box opening must face left to meet the duct: Horizontal Left Intake.
    2. Horizontal Left Intake is position 270.
    3. Check for conflicts: the outlet is at the top pointing right, and the box is on the left, so the two connections do not compete for the same space.
    4. The order specifies CW 90 discharge with the inlet box at 270.

    If the duct instead arrived from above at an angle on the left, the position would be 315, Angular Down Intake.

    Two Angles, Two Rules

    Watch out for treating the inlet box angle like the discharge angle. The discharge angle counts from upblast in the direction of rotation, but the inlet box angle is always counted clockwise from the top as seen from the drive side, so a CCW fan's inlet box at 90 is still on the right. Write each angle with its own label on the order so no one converts one into the other.

    Before working through the next interactive element, recall that the inlet box angle runs clockwise from the top in 45-degree steps and that two pairs of positions share a description.

    Inline Configuration¶

    Inline, mixed flow and axial fans move air straight through a cylindrical housing, so they have no scroll outlet to point. Their orientation is instead described by an inline configuration: whether the fan's axis runs horizontally or vertically, which way the air flows, and how the fan is supported. FanPedia lists three horizontal configurations, HOR (Horizontal, No Brackets), HCH (Horizontal, Ceiling Hung) and HBM (Horizontal, Base Mount), and ten vertical configurations whose codes begin VU (vertical up), VD (vertical down) or VR (vertical reversible), such as VUI (Vertical Up, Floor Mount Brackets on Inlet) and VDH (Vertical Down with Hood) (FanPedia pp. 50-51). For example, an inline fan hung from roof joists in a horizontal duct run is HCH. Chapter 13 covers the full horizontal and vertical lists.

    Motor Position¶

    The motor position states where a belt-drive motor sits relative to the fan. On a direct-drive fan the motor is on the shaft and has no separate position, but on a belt-driven fan the motor can be placed on several sides of the fan, and the choice affects floor space, access for belt changes, and whether the motor collides with the outlet or the inlet duct. Like rotation and discharge, the motor position of a centrifugal fan is read from the drive side.

    Different fan families use different naming systems for motor position, and FanPedia's motor position pages show each one (FanPedia pp. 52-57):

    • Arrangements 1, 3 and 3F use the four AMCA standard positions W, X, Y and Z.
    • Arrangements 9 and 9F use L and R; FanPedia lists the standard as CW (L) / CCW (R).
    • "K" mount motors, also called piggyback or scroll-mounted motors, sit directly on the housing scroll and are located by clock positions such as 12 o'clock or 1:30.
    • Plenum fans and swingout fans have their own position sets; Chapter 13 covers the plenum positions.
    • Axial and inline Arrangement 9 fans use the letters A through H.

    Worked example. A distributor receives two quotes for "the same" belt-driven SWSI fan. One lists "Arr. 9, CW, motor position L"; the other lists "Arr. 1, CW, motor position W". Are they the same?

    1. The arrangement differs. In Arrangement 9 the motor hangs on the side of the fan's own bearing pedestal; in Arrangement 1 the motor sits on the floor or a base beside the pedestal (Chapter 11).
    2. The motor position systems differ because the arrangements differ: L and R describe the two sides of a pedestal-mounted motor, and W, X, Y and Z describe four floor positions around an Arrangement 1 or 3 fan.
    3. The quotes are therefore for different assemblies with different footprints and different base requirements. The distributor should ask which arrangement the job needs before comparing prices.

    Read the Arrangement Before the Letter

    Here's a shortcut: a motor position letter means nothing until you know the arrangement, because each arrangement family has its own letters. Find the arrangement on the order first, then look up that arrangement's motor position page.

    Motor Positions W X Y Z¶

    The motor positions W, X, Y and Z are the four standard belt-drive motor locations that AMCA defines for centrifugal fans, and FanPedia states that the motor on an Arrangement 1 or Arrangement 3 SWSI fan "can be mounted in any of the four AMCA standard motor positions: W, X, Y or Z" (FanPedia pp. 162-163). The positions are read facing the drive side. FanPedia's illustration for Arrangements 1 and 3 places W and X on the right-hand side of the fan and Y and Z on the left-hand side; on an Arrangement 3F, whose extended frame carries the motor, the illustration shows two positions, Z and W, also known as L and R (FanPedia p. 52).

    Not every position works with every discharge. The technical descriptions list restrictions for Arrangements 1, 3 and 3F where the outlet and the motor would collide or the motor would not fit under the outlet. For example, with a BHD discharge the motor "interferes with outlet opening of housing," so on a CW fan motor position Z is not allowed and on a CCW fan motor position W is not allowed (FanPedia pp. 162-163). Chapter 13 sets out the complete restriction list.

    Worked example. Return to the boiler forced-draft fan from the bottom horizontal section: an Arrangement 1 fan, CW 270 (CW BHD), with the outlet projecting to the left at floor level. Maintenance wants the motor on the left side of the fan for belt access from an aisle.

    1. Facing the drive side, the left-hand positions are Y and Z.
    2. FanPedia's restriction for a CW BHD discharge rules out Z, because the motor would interfere with the housing outlet.
    3. That leaves Y on the left side, or W and X on the right side.
    4. The engineer checks Y against the drawing and confirms it clears the outlet, and the order reads CW 270, motor position Y. If it did not clear, the motor would move to the right side and the aisle access would be rearranged instead.

    Every Orientation Choice Is Linked

    Think of rotation, discharge, inlet box and motor position as four hands on one clock face: moving one can push another out of a legal position. That is why experienced application engineers sketch the drive-side view with all four before they write any of them on the order.

    Axial Motor Positions¶

    Axial motor positions locate the belt-drive motor on an Arrangement 9 axial, mixed flow or inline fan in a horizontal configuration. FanPedia labels eight positions A through H around the fan as seen in the discharge view: A at the top, then B, C, D, E, F, G and H in turn around the housing, with E at the bottom (FanPedia p. 56). A duct- or ceiling-mounted fan can take any of the eight; FanPedia's floor-mounted illustration shows only the upper five, G, H, A, B and C, since the floor supports sit below the fan. For vertical configurations FanPedia specifies no motor position for roof- and duct-mounted fans: the motor is centered on the curb cap or between the support brackets (FanPedia p. 57).

    Putting It Together: Reading a Full Orientation¶

    A complete orientation for a belt-driven centrifugal fan pairs the arrangement with four facts, each read from the drive side. The table below summarizes the conventions covered in this chapter.

    Item What it states How it is read
    Rotation Direction the impeller turns CW or CCW from the drive side (axial: LH or RH from the discharge side)
    Discharge position Direction the air leaves the outlet One of eight names, 45 degrees apart
    Discharge angle Degrees from upblast to the outlet Counted from 360 in the direction of rotation
    Inlet box position Where the inlet box opening faces 45 to 360, clockwise from the top, from the drive side
    Motor position Where the belt-drive motor sits Letters set by the arrangement family

    Worked example. A specifying engineer's schedule reads: "Arr. 3 SWSI, CCW, discharge THD, inlet box 90, motor position X." Sketch it from the drive side.

    1. CCW rotation with THD: the outlet is at the top and the air leaves to the left (\( \theta = 360^\circ - 90^\circ = 270^\circ \)).
    2. Inlet box 90 is Horizontal Right Intake, so the inlet duct arrives from the right.
    3. Motor position X is on the right-hand side of the fan.
    4. Check conflicts: the outlet is at the top heading left, so it neither collides with the right-hand motor nor competes with the right-hand inlet box duct. THD on a CCW fan restricts position Z, which is not the one chosen.

    The sketch confirms the schedule is buildable, and it is the drawing the installer will compare against when the fan arrives.

    You Can Read a Fan From Across the Room

    You just learned to fix the viewpoint at the drive side, name all eight discharge positions with their angles, place an inlet box, and locate a motor in each arrangement family's system. That is the orientation half of every fan order handled, and Chapter 13 turns it into codes.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Centrifugal fan orientation is read viewed from the drive side, the side where the sheave, coupling or motor is. A view from the inlet side is a mirror image.
    • Rotation is CW or CCW from the drive side for centrifugal fans; axial impeller rotation is LH or RH viewed from the discharge side. A wheel turning the wrong way delivers only a fraction of its rated performance.
    • The discharge position is one of eight directions 45 degrees apart: UBD, TAU, THD, TAD, DBD, BAD, BHD and BAU. Each name is valid for both rotations, with mirror-image housings.
    • The discharge angle counts from upblast (360) in the direction of rotation, so CW 90 and CCW 90 are both top horizontal, pointing right and left respectively.
    • Up blast, top horizontal, bottom horizontal and down blast suit vertical stacks, overhead ducts, low ducts and downward discharges; the four angular discharges point the outlet at 45 degrees.
    • Inlet box position runs from 45 to 360 clockwise from the top, from the drive side, and does not change with rotation.
    • Inline configuration describes axis orientation, flow direction and support for inline, mixed flow and axial fans.
    • Motor position depends on the arrangement: W, X, Y, Z for Arrangements 1, 3 and 3F, L and R for Arrangement 9, clock positions for K-mount motors, and A through H for axial Arrangement 9 fans. Some discharges rule out some motor positions.
