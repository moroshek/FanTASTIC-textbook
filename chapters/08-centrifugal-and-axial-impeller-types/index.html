    Centrifugal and Axial Impeller Types¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter introduces the impeller, the part that does the work of moving air. It covers blades, back plates, shrouds, and hubs; the main centrifugal types (single width single inlet, backward inclined, and forward curved); mixed flow impellers; and axial impellers with fixed or adjustable pitch, including hub position on the intake or discharge side and reversible impellers. It also covers straight bore and taper lock hubs and why impellers are balanced. After completing it, readers can identify an impeller type and explain how air flows through it.

    Welcome to the Impeller Chapter

    Every fan you will ever quote, specify, or troubleshoot has exactly one part that actually moves the air, and that part is the impeller. By the end of this chapter you will be able to look at a wheel on a shop floor or a picture in a catalog and say what type it is, which way the air goes through it, and what the order has to say about it. Let's follow the airflow.

    The Part That Does the Work¶

    In Chapter 3 you met the impeller as one part among many: housing, shaft, bearings, motor, inlet, and outlet. Every other part of a fan exists to hold the impeller, spin it, or guide air into and out of it. The impeller is the only part that adds energy to the airstream. When it turns, its blades push on the air, raising the air's velocity and pressure, which is the total pressure rise you studied in Chapter 2.

    In Chapter 4 you sorted fans into three families by the path the air takes. That sorting is really a statement about the impeller inside. This chapter opens the fan up and looks at the impeller itself: what its parts are called, how the main centrifugal and axial types differ, how an axial impeller's hub position follows from the airflow direction, how the hub fastens to the shaft, and why every impeller is balanced before it ships. Chapter 9 then goes further into airfoil, backward curved, radial, and material handling designs.

    Impeller Parts¶

    Before comparing impeller types, it helps to name the parts that most of them share. FanPedia's centrifugal impeller drawing (FanPedia PDF p. 31) labels four of them: the blade, the back plate, the impeller front plate, and the hub. A fifth idea, the blade's leading edge, appears on every axial airflow page (FanPedia PDF pp. 37–41).

    Impeller Blade¶

    An impeller blade is the surface that pushes on the air. Every impeller, centrifugal or axial, has a set of blades arranged evenly around the axis of rotation, and the shape, angle, and number of those blades decide most of what the impeller can do: how much pressure it can build, how efficiently it runs, and how well it tolerates dust. Blade shape is also the quickest way to identify an impeller type in the field. A blade that leans or curves back, away from the direction of rotation, belongs to the backward family; a blade whose tip curves toward the direction of rotation belongs to a forward curved impeller; a blade that runs straight out from the center is radial.

    On an axial impeller the blades are shaped like propeller blades. FanPedia describes their action in one sentence: the concave side of the blade "cups the air and pushes it away from the impeller" (FanPedia PDF p. 37). The concave side is the hollow, inside face of the curved blade.

    Worked Example: Checking Rotation from the Blades¶

    A facilities technician calls about a new housed centrifugal fan that "runs but doesn't move much air." You ask them to lock out the motor, open the access door, and describe the blades. They report flat blades whose outer ends lean back, away from the direction the arrow on the housing shows.

    1. Flat, single-thickness blades that lean backward identify a backward inclined impeller (FanPedia PDF p. 31).
    2. On a backward inclined impeller, the blade tips should trail the direction of rotation. The technician's description says the tips lean away from the arrow, which is correct for the arrow.
    3. You then ask them to bump the motor and watch the shaft. If the shaft turns against the arrow, the wiring is reversed. A centrifugal impeller turning backward still moves some air, but far less than its rating, which matches the complaint.
    4. The fix is to swap two motor leads (a job for an electrician) and recheck the rotation.

    The blade shape told you what "correct" looks like before anyone measured anything. Chapter 12 shows how rotation is formally designated on a drawing.

    Blade Leading Edge¶

    The blade leading edge is the edge of the blade that meets the incoming air first. On an axial impeller, FanPedia states that "air is drawn through the impeller from the leading edge of the blades" (FanPedia PDF p. 37), so the leading edge always faces the intake side of the fan. On a centrifugal impeller the leading edge is the inner edge of each blade, near the inlet, and the opposite edge at the outer diameter is the trailing edge, or blade tip, where air leaves. Knowing which edge leads is how you check that an axial impeller is mounted facing the right way: if the leading edges face the discharge, the impeller is backward on its shaft.

    Impeller Back Plate and Impeller Shroud¶

    The impeller back plate is the solid disc on the closed side of a centrifugal impeller. The blades are welded to it, and on most centrifugal impellers the hub is attached to it, so the back plate carries the blade loads to the hub and shaft. It also forms one wall of the air passage, so air entering the open side has nowhere to go except outward between the blades.

    The impeller shroud is the ring-shaped plate on the inlet side of a centrifugal impeller. FanPedia labels it the impeller front plate (FanPedia PDF p. 31), and both names are common in the trade. The shroud has a large hole in its center where air enters, and it closes the other side of the blade passages so air is guided outward instead of leaking around the blade edges. Together, the back plate and shroud sandwich the blades. Not every impeller has both: FanPedia's open paddle impeller has neither, and its material handling impeller has a back plate only (FanPedia PDF p. 32), designs that Chapter 9 covers.

    Back Plate Fins¶

    Back plate fins are small radial vanes welded to the outside of the back plate, on the side away from the blades. FanPedia lists this accessory as "fins on impeller back plate" and notes that it is also known by these names, any of which may appear on drawings and orders (FanPedia PDF p. 126):

    • thrust fins
    • thrust vanes or anti-thrust vanes
    • back plate fins
    • back pressure fins
    • cooling fins

    FanPedia also gives their purpose: the fins "reduce the thrust load on the bearings," and they "can create a negative pressure behind the impeller to draw air into the fan through the shaft hole in the housing," which "helps reduce the possibility of leakage of the airstream to atmosphere" (FanPedia PDF pp. 126, 184). Thrust load is the push along the shaft's axis that the bearings must resist. Back plate fins therefore show up when bearing life or airstream leakage around the shaft is a concern.

    Impeller Hub¶

    The impeller hub is the thick central piece that connects the impeller to the shaft. Its bore, the hole through its center, fits the shaft, and the hub transmits the motor's torque into the blades. On a centrifugal impeller the hub is fastened to the back plate; on an axial impeller the hub is the center body that the blades attach to. FanPedia's axial overview (FanPedia PDF p. 35) names five axial parts: the blade, the blade tip, the hub, the hub boss (the raised center of the hub that holds the bore), and, on adjustable pitch impellers, the shank, which is the root of a blade that fits into the hub.

    Worked Example: Reading a Hub Callout on a Replacement Order¶

    A plant sends a purchase order for "one replacement impeller, same as existing," with a photo of the old wheel. Before it can be quoted, the hub has to be described. You work through it like this:

    1. What fastens the hub to the shaft? The photo shows set screws on the side of the hub and a keyway in the bore. That is a straight bore hub (defined later in this chapter).
    2. What size is the bore? A straight bore must match the shaft diameter, so you need the shaft size from the nameplate data or the original order. The photo cannot give it.
    3. Is it centrifugal or axial? A back plate and front plate are visible, so it is centrifugal, and the hub sits on the back plate.
    4. Anything attached to the back plate? The photo shows short vanes on the back of the back plate, so the replacement must also have back plate fins.

    The order now names the hub type, the bore, and the fins instead of "same as existing." That is the habit to build: name each part, and an order can be checked.

    Before moving on, the table below collects the parts you have just read about.

    Part Where it is What it does
    Blade Around the axis, between plates (centrifugal) or on the hub (axial) Pushes on the air
    Blade leading edge Edge of the blade that meets the incoming air Faces the intake; shows mounting direction
    Back plate Closed side of a centrifugal impeller Carries blades to the hub; closes the air passage
    Shroud (front plate) Inlet side of a centrifugal impeller Guides air outward; closes the blade passages
    Back plate fins Outside of the back plate Reduce bearing thrust; help limit shaft-hole leakage
    Hub Center of the impeller Connects the impeller to the shaft

    Centrifugal Impellers¶

    A centrifugal impeller draws air in along the shaft axis and throws it outward, at a right angle to the shaft, off the blade tips. Air enters through the shroud opening, turns, and is flung outward through the passages between the blades by the impeller's rotation. FanPedia's housed centrifugal fan description states the result: air is drawn in through one or both sides and "is discharged at a right angle to the fan shaft" (FanPedia PDF p. 4). In a housed fan the scroll housing collects the air leaving the blade tips and turns its velocity into useful pressure; in a plenum fan, the plenum does that job; in an inline centrifugal fan, the tubular housing turns the air back in line with the duct.

    FanPedia lists the centrifugal impeller types built into housed centrifugal fans: backward inclined, airfoil, backward curved, radial tip, forward curved, and radial bladed (FanPedia PDF p. 4). This chapter covers backward inclined and forward curved; the others are in Chapter 9.

    One number connects impeller size and speed to how hard the impeller works on the air: the tip speed, the velocity of the blade tips as they travel around the circle. Higher tip speed means the blades can build more pressure, and it also means more stress in the impeller. For an impeller diameter \( D \) in inches turning at \( N \) revolutions per minute, the circumference in feet is \( \pi D / 12 \), and the tip speed is:

    \[ V_{tip} = \frac{\pi D N}{12} \]

    where \( V_{tip} \) is the tip speed in feet per minute (ft/min), \( D \) is the impeller diameter in inches, and \( N \) is the speed in rpm.

    Worked Example: Tip Speed of a Centrifugal Impeller¶

    A 30-inch centrifugal impeller runs at 1,200 rpm (illustrative values, not a specific catalog size).

    \[ V_{tip} = \frac{\pi \times 30 \times 1200}{12} = 9{,}425 \text{ ft/min} \]

    If a belt change raises the speed to 1,800 rpm, the tip speed rises in proportion to 14,137 ft/min, 50% higher. The fan laws in Chapter 25 show that pressure rises with the square of speed, so this change raises the pressure the impeller can develop by a factor of \( 1.5^2 = 2.25 \). The impeller's structure has to be rated for that speed, which is why a speed increase is always checked against the maximum speed the current catalog allows for that fan and its construction.

    Same Spin, Different Path

    A centrifugal impeller works mostly by flinging air outward, so its pressure comes from the radius the air travels through. An axial impeller works by lifting air along the shaft, like a propeller. Once you see which way the air leaves the wheel, the housing, the arrangement, and even the drawing conventions start to make sense.

    Single Width Single Inlet¶

    A single width single inlet (SWSI) impeller takes air in through one side only. It has one back plate, one shroud, and one inlet opening, and its housing has one inlet. The alternative is the double width double inlet (DWDI) impeller, which is essentially two single-width impellers placed back to back on a common center plate, drawing air in from both sides; it is covered in Chapter 9. FanPedia's housed centrifugal fan figure shows both (FanPedia PDF p. 4), and the arrangement pages list the SWSI arrangements separately (FanPedia PDF pp. 20–22).

    Because a SWSI impeller has one open side and one closed side, its shaft can come in through the closed side. That allows the impeller to hang on the end of the shaft, with no bearing on the inlet side to block the airflow.

    Worked Example: Can This Be Arrangement 4?¶

    A mechanical contractor asks for "a direct-drive fan, impeller on the motor shaft, double inlet, to save space." You check it against FanPedia:

    1. Arrangement 4 is direct drive with the "impeller mounted to motor shaft" and appears on the SWSI arrangement pages (FanPedia PDF p. 20).
    2. A double inlet impeller takes air from both sides, so a shaft cannot end inside it with a motor on one side without blocking one inlet. FanPedia's double width, double inlet arrangement page lists only Arrangement 3 and 7 variants (3, 3DI, 3F, 7, and 7DI) (FanPedia PDF p. 23), and its orientation page shows those arrangements with the impeller between two bearings (FanPedia PDF p. 30).
    3. The request is inconsistent. You go back to the contractor with two options: an Arrangement 4 SWSI fan, or a double width fan in Arrangement 3 or 7.

    The words "single width single inlet" carry a real constraint on arrangement, so they belong on every centrifugal order.

    Impeller Orientation¶

    Impeller orientation describes where the impeller sits on the shaft relative to the bearings. FanPedia names two orientations for centrifugal fans (FanPedia PDF p. 30):

    • Overhung impeller: the impeller is mounted on the end of the shaft, outboard of the bearings. FanPedia lists Arrangements 1, 8, 9, and 10, and Arrangement 4, where the impeller hangs on the motor shaft.
    • Center hung impeller: the impeller is mounted between the bearings. FanPedia lists Arrangement 3 and Arrangement 7, single and double width.

    Orientation matters for the order because it fixes which arrangements are possible and where the bearings sit relative to the airstream. Chapter 11 develops the arrangements in full.

    Backward Inclined Impeller¶

    A backward inclined impeller has flat, single-thickness blades tilted backward, so each blade's outer end trails behind its inner end as the impeller turns. FanPedia's caption reads "impeller with flat, single thickness, backward inclined blades" (FanPedia PDF p. 31). Its close relatives are the backward curved impeller, whose single-thickness blades are curved backward, and the airfoil impeller, whose backward inclined blades have a hollow airfoil section; both are in Chapter 9.

    Backward inclined impellers are widely used for general ventilation and clean or lightly dusty air because they combine good efficiency with a useful property called a non-overloading power curve. On a fan curve, which Chapter 10 covers in detail, the brake horsepower of a backward inclined fan at a fixed speed rises to a peak and then falls as airflow keeps increasing. A motor sized for that peak cannot be overloaded at that speed, wherever the fan ends up operating on its curve.

    Worked Example: Will the Motor Overload?¶

    A rooftop filtered supply unit is being selected for a building whose duct system is still being designed. The engineer worries that the real duct resistance may be lower than calculated, which would push the fan to more airflow than planned. FanPedia notes that filtered units use either forward curved or backward inclined impellers (FanPedia PDF p. 4). Which is safer here?

    1. Lower system resistance moves the operating point to the right on the fan curve, toward more airflow.
    2. With a backward inclined impeller, power peaks and then falls toward higher airflow, so a motor sized for the peak power at the selected speed covers this case.
    3. With a forward curved impeller, power keeps rising with airflow (next section), so the same shift can overload a motor that was sized only for the design point.
    4. When the system resistance is uncertain, the backward inclined unit, or a forward curved unit with a motor sized for the highest airflow it could reach, protects the motor.

    Forward Curved Impeller¶

    A forward curved impeller has many short, shallow blades that curve forward, so their tips point in the direction of rotation. FanPedia's caption is "impeller with single thickness, forward curved blades," and its drawing shows the end ring and tie rods that hold the narrow blades in place (FanPedia PDF p. 31). The forward curve throws air off the tips at high velocity, so a forward curved impeller moves a given airflow at a lower speed and in a smaller size than a backward inclined impeller, which suits compact, low-pressure equipment. The trade-offs are lower efficiency, a power curve that keeps rising with airflow (an overloading curve), and shallow blade pockets that collect dust, so forward curved impellers belong in clean air.

    Mixed Flow Impeller¶

    A mixed flow impeller is a hybrid of axial and centrifugal designs: air leaves it partly along the shaft and partly outward. FanPedia calls it a "hybrid axial-centrifugal impeller design" used in mixed flow fans with tubular housings (FanPedia PDF p. 6), and describes its shape: "airfoil blades taper from impeller front plate to hub end resulting in a larger tip width" (FanPedia PDF p. 32). Because the air turns only partway, a mixed flow fan can sit in a straight duct like an axial fan while developing more pressure than a comparable axial impeller.

    Now that the centrifugal family and the mixed flow hybrid have been described, the comparison below summarizes them.

    Impeller Blade shape (FanPedia caption) Air leaves Power curve Best airstream
    Backward inclined Flat, single thickness, tilted backward Outward, at a right angle to the shaft Non-overloading Clean to lightly dusty
    Forward curved Single thickness, curved forward; many short blades Outward, at a right angle to the shaft Overloading Clean
    Mixed flow Airfoil blades tapering from front plate to hub, larger tip width Partly along the shaft, partly outward Depends on design; see the catalog Clean

    Do Not Judge a Wheel by Its Width

    A common trap is calling any wheel with many narrow blades "forward curved" without checking which way the blades curve against the rotation. Before you write the impeller type on an order, find the rotation arrow and confirm the blade tips point with it (forward curved) or trail it (backward inclined).

    Axial Impellers¶

    An axial impeller moves air parallel to the shaft. Its blades are attached to a central hub and, as the impeller turns, the concave side of each blade "cups the air and pushes it away from the impeller" (FanPedia PDF p. 37). Air enters at the blades' leading edges and leaves straight through, so an axial fan can sit in a duct with no change of direction. Axial impellers are used in tubeaxial and vaneaxial fans, roof mounted axial fans, and panel fans; FanPedia notes that inline centrifugal fans can share the same arrangements but use different impellers (FanPedia PDF p. 28).

    FanPedia lays out three steps for configuring an axial fan (FanPedia PDF p. 36):

    1. Select the model and impeller type, for example "Model TCTA with 'TCTA' impeller."
    2. Choose the airflow direction: air through the impeller then over the motor (airflow = PM), or air over the motor then through the impeller (airflow = MP).
    3. Determine the rotation the impeller needs to produce that airflow direction.

    FanPedia adds two notes to every axial airflow page: airflow direction and rotation "must be correct for the fan to operate at 100% performance," and if the impeller rotates in the wrong direction, "you will not get any airflow in the proper direction" (FanPedia PDF pp. 37–40). For axial fans, rotation is determined by viewing the impeller from the discharge side of the fan.

    Fixed Pitch and Adjustable Pitch Impellers¶

    The pitch of an axial blade is its angle relative to the plane of rotation. A steeper pitch takes a bigger bite of air per revolution.

    A fixed pitch impeller has its blades set at one angle that cannot be changed. FanPedia shows fixed pitch impellers as cast or fabricated (FanPedia PDF p. 35); its axial type pages mark the "TCTS," "TCTA," "Z Series," and "L Series" impellers as fixed (FanPedia PDF p. 34).

    An adjustable pitch impeller has separate blades whose shanks are clamped into the hub, so the blade angle can be reset to a different pitch. Resetting the pitch moves the fan to a different performance curve without changing the impeller diameter or speed. FanPedia marks the "B Series," "E Series," "TCVX," "A," and "AXIAD" impellers as adjustable and the "C Series" as adjustable only (FanPedia PDF p. 33). Blade count and pitch angle are covered in more depth in Chapter 9.

    Fiberglass Axial Impeller¶

    A fiberglass axial impeller has blades, and sometimes a hub, molded from fiberglass-reinforced plastic instead of metal, which suits corrosive airstreams that would attack steel or aluminum. FanPedia shows a fiberglass group (FanPedia PDF p. 34): the "TF" and "FG7" impellers, marked fixed, and the adjustable "F4/F6" impellers with an aluminum hub and "FG4/FG6" impellers with a fiberglass hub. Whether the hub is aluminum or fiberglass is part of the impeller designation, so it has to be stated on the order.

    Worked Example: Configuring an Axial Fan¶

    A customer needs a direct-drive tubeaxial fan to exhaust warm air from a process area, and asks how the order should describe the impeller. Following FanPedia's three steps:

    1. Model and impeller. Suppose the selection, made from the current catalog, is a fan with a fixed pitch cast impeller. (The model is not the point of this example.)
    2. Airflow direction. With airflow = PM, air passes through the impeller and then over the motor. With airflow = MP, air passes over the motor first. In a direct-drive axial fan the motor is in the airstream either way; when that is a problem, a belt-driven arrangement is the answer, which Chapter 11 covers. Here the customer chooses MP to match an existing duct layout.
    3. Rotation. Rotation is read from the discharge side. The rotation must match the impeller hand (left hand or right hand), because FanPedia shows both left hand and right hand fixed and adjustable impellers (FanPedia PDF pp. 42–43). A right hand impeller is ordered with right hand rotation.

    The finished line on the order states the impeller, the airflow direction (MP), and the rotation, and the next section shows how MP also fixes the hub position.

    Hub on Intake Side and Hub on Discharge Side¶

    FanPedia gives each airflow direction an alternative name based on where the hub's boss faces (FanPedia PDF pp. 36–40):

    • Hub on intake side: airflow = PM, air through the impeller then over the motor. The hub faces the incoming air.
    • Hub on discharge side: airflow = MP, air over the motor then through the impeller. The hub faces the leaving air.

    In FanPedia's side views (pp. 37–40) the motor is on one side of the impeller and the hub on the other, so in both builds the hub faces away from the motor: in PM the side away from the motor is the intake, and in MP it is the discharge.

    The hub bore follows the same choice. On FanPedia's left hand and right hand pages, a standard bore hub goes with MP airflow and a reverse bore hub goes with PM airflow, for both adjustable and fixed cast impellers (FanPedia PDF pp. 42–43). In other words, "MP," "hub on discharge," and "standard bore hub" all describe the same build, and "PM," "hub on intake," and "reverse bore hub" describe the other.

    Airflow code Air path Also called Hub bore (FanPedia pp. 42–43)
    PM Through impeller, then over motor Hub on intake Reverse bore hub
    MP Over motor, then through impeller Hub on discharge Standard bore hub

    One Letter Order, Three Answers

    Read the airflow code left to right as the air's path; FanPedia capitalizes the P in "imPeller" to make the point, so PM means impeller first, then motor. If the impeller comes first, the hub faces the intake and the bore is reversed; if the motor comes first, the hub faces the discharge and the bore is standard.

    Reversible Impeller¶

    A reversible impeller is an axial impeller designed to move air in either direction along the shaft. FanPedia shows one reversible impeller in two modes (FanPedia PDF p. 41): standard flow, with airflow = MP and the hub on the discharge side, and reverse flow, with airflow = PM and the hub on the intake side. In both modes, air is drawn in at the blades' leading edge for that flow direction. FanPedia labels the rotation the same in both modes; since rotation is always viewed from the discharge side and the discharge switches ends when the flow reverses, the motor's actual direction of turning reverses with the flow. Reversible impellers are used where a single fan must both supply and exhaust, such as a space that is ventilated in one direction during some operations and the other direction at other times.

    Two Codes, Many Names

    If PM, MP, hub on intake, hub on discharge, standard bore, and reverse bore feel like six things to memorize, most people feel the same on first pass. There are really only two builds with three names each, so learn the air path for one of them and the other is just its opposite; the configurator above lets you test that before it goes on an order.

    Hub Types¶

    The hub's bore has to grip the shaft tightly enough to carry full motor torque and stay put under vibration, and it must also come off when the impeller or shaft needs service. FanPedia shows two common ways to do this, for both centrifugal and axial impellers (FanPedia PDF pp. 44–45). A third, the Trantorque hub, is in Chapter 9.

    Straight Bore Hub¶

    In a straight bore hub, "the bore of the hub is straight through. Shafts are keyed and mounted to the hub" (FanPedia PDF p. 45). A key, a small square bar, sits half in a keyway in the shaft and half in a matching keyway in the hub, so it carries the torque. On centrifugal impellers, set screws through the hub press on the key and shaft to stop the hub sliding along the shaft. On axial impellers FanPedia shows two versions: with key and set screws, and with key, retaining washer, and bolt at the shaft end (FanPedia PDF p. 44). A straight bore has to be machined to suit one shaft diameter.

    Taper Lock Hub¶

    In a taper lock hub, "the hub bore is tapered with respect to the fan shaft. The hub is locked to the shaft using a tapered bushing" (FanPedia PDF p. 45). The bushing has a straight bore that fits the shaft and a tapered outside that fits the hub. Tightening the bushing's screws draws it into the tapered hub, which squeezes the bushing onto the shaft for a tight clamping fit along its whole length. Loosening the screws, and using the bushing's removal holes, releases it. FanPedia's axial version is shown "with key and hardware" (FanPedia PDF p. 44).

    Worked Example: Choosing Between Two Hubs¶

    A plant engineer replaces impellers every few years because of wear and wants the change-outs to be quick. The current impeller has a straight bore hub that has seized to the shaft twice. Compare the two hub types for this customer:

    Question Straight bore hub Taper lock hub
    How is torque carried? Key in matching keyways Clamping fit of the bushing, with a key
    How is it held in place along the shaft? Set screws (or washer and bolt on axial) The bushing's grip on the shaft
    What does removal require? Pulling the hub off a close-fitting bore Loosening the bushing screws to release the taper
    Does the hub bore have to match the shaft? Yes, machined to one shaft diameter The bushing's bore matches the shaft

    For this customer, the taper lock hub answers the stated problem: the taper releases the grip when the screws are backed out, so change-outs do not depend on forcing a close-fitting bore off the shaft. The hub type then goes on the replacement order along with the shaft diameter. Confirm in the current catalog that the impeller is offered with that hub.

    Impeller Balancing¶

    An impeller is never perfectly symmetric. Slight differences in plate thickness, weld beads, and blade position leave more mass on one side of the axis than the other. When the impeller spins, that off-center mass is pulled outward and the whole rotor is pushed around in a circle once per revolution, which the bearings, shaft, and structure feel as vibration. Impeller balancing is the process of measuring that unbalance, usually by spinning the impeller on a balancing machine, and correcting it by adding or removing small weights until what is left, the residual unbalance, is within an allowed limit. Detailed balancing procedures are outside the scope of this course; what an application or sales engineer needs is why it matters and how the limit is set.

    The force from a given unbalance grows with the square of speed. For a residual unbalance \( U \) in ounce-inches turning at \( N \) rpm, the rotating force is approximately:

    \[ F \approx 1.77 \, U \left( \frac{N}{1000} \right)^2 \]

    where \( F \) is the force in pounds-force (lbf), \( U \) is the unbalance (the unbalance mass in ounces multiplied by its distance from the axis in inches), and \( N \) is the speed in rpm.

    The allowed residual unbalance is set by a balance quality grade, \( G \), from the international balancing standard ISO 21940-11 (formerly ISO 1940-1). The grade is a speed in millimeters per second; a smaller grade is tighter balance. The fan industry standard AMCA 204 assigns balance grades by fan application category, with grades such as G6.3 and G2.5 common for industrial fans. For an impeller weighing \( W \) pounds running at a maximum service speed of \( N \) rpm, the permissible residual unbalance is:

    \[ U_{per} = \frac{6.015 \, G \, W}{N} \]

    where \( U_{per} \) is the permissible residual unbalance in ounce-inches, \( G \) is the balance grade in mm/s, \( W \) is the impeller weight in pounds, and \( N \) is the speed in rpm.

    Worked Example: How Much Unbalance Is Allowed?¶

    A 200 lb impeller runs at 1,200 rpm and is balanced to grade G6.3 (illustrative values; the grade for a real fan comes from the specification or catalog).

    1. Permissible residual unbalance: \( U_{per} = 6.015 \times 6.3 \times 200 / 1200 = 6.32 \) oz·in.
    2. If the balancer corrects at a radius of 15 in, the largest uncorrected mass allowed there is \( 6.32 / 15 = 0.42 \) oz, less than half an ounce on a 200 lb wheel.
    3. At G2.5 the limit drops to \( 2.51 \) oz·in, or \( 0.17 \) oz at 15 in.
    4. The force from the G6.3 limit at 1,200 rpm is \( 1.77 \times 6.32 \times 1.2^2 \approx 16 \) lbf. Run the same impeller at 1,800 rpm and the force is about 36 lbf; at 2,400 rpm, twice the speed, about 64 lbf, four times as much.

    That last step is the practical lesson: an impeller balanced for one speed is not automatically acceptable at a higher one, which is one more reason a speed increase is checked against the catalog before it is made.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • The impeller is the only part of a fan that adds energy to the air; its blades push the air, and the blade leading edge meets the incoming air first.
    • A centrifugal impeller's blades sit between a back plate and a shroud (FanPedia's impeller front plate); back plate fins on the back reduce bearing thrust and help limit leakage at the shaft hole.
    • The hub connects the impeller to the shaft. A straight bore hub is keyed and held by set screws (or a washer and bolt); a taper lock hub clamps to the shaft through a tapered bushing.
    • A centrifugal impeller throws air outward at a right angle to the shaft. A single width single inlet impeller takes air from one side and can be overhung; double width impellers are center hung in Arrangements 3 and 7.
    • Backward inclined impellers have flat blades tilted against rotation and a non-overloading power curve; forward curved impellers have many short blades curved with rotation, run slower and smaller, and have an overloading power curve.
    • A mixed flow impeller is a hybrid whose airfoil blades taper from front plate to hub, sending air partly along the shaft.
    • An axial impeller moves air parallel to the shaft, with fixed pitch (cast or fabricated) or adjustable pitch blades; fiberglass axial impellers come in both.
    • Airflow PM is hub on intake with a reverse bore hub; airflow MP is hub on discharge with a standard bore hub. A reversible impeller runs standard flow MP and reverse flow PM. Axial rotation is viewed from the discharge side, and wrong rotation gives no airflow in the proper direction.
    • Impeller balancing limits residual unbalance to \( U_{per} = 6.015\,G\,W/N \); the vibration force from unbalance grows with the square of speed.

    You Can Name the Wheel

    You can now name every major impeller part, tell backward inclined from forward curved by the blades alone, and turn an axial airflow code into a hub position and bore. That is exactly what an impeller line on an order needs.
