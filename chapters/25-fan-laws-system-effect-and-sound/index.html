    Fan Laws, System Effect and Sound¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter applies performance concepts to real decisions. It covers the fan laws (airflow varies with speed, pressure with speed squared, power with speed cubed), speed and size change calculations, the fan power equation, cold start horsepower, specific speed, free delivery, shutoff pressure, and the peak pressure point. It explains surge, static and peak efficiency, the efficiency curve, Fan Energy Index, inlet and outlet system effect, AMCA installation types, fans in parallel and series, flow control efficiency, and sound pressure and octave bands. After completing it, readers can predict how a fan's performance changes with speed and installation.

    Welcome to Fan Laws, System Effect and Sound

    Sooner or later a customer calls and asks, "What if we speed it up?", "Why is it short on air?" or "Will it be too loud?" This chapter gives you the tools to answer all three with numbers instead of guesses, often on the back of the quote sheet. Let's follow the airflow.

    Chapter 10 showed how to read one fan curve at one speed and find where it crosses a system curve. This chapter puts that picture to work. It starts with the landmarks on a fan curve and the efficiency curve that rides on top of it, then develops the fan laws that predict a new curve when speed, size or air density changes. With those in hand it turns to where a fan should be selected, how the installation steals performance through system effect, how two fans combine, and how sound is described once it leaves the fan.

    The examples reuse the illustrative backward inclined fan from Chapter 10 (call it Curve A) so the numbers connect. At its rated speed of 1,380 RPM and standard air, Curve A delivers 10,000 CFM at 4.0 in. wg fan static pressure and absorbs 9.0 BHP. It is an illustrative curve, not a published Twin City Fan rating.

    Airflow (CFM) Fan static pressure (in. wg) BHP Static efficiency

    2,000 4.8 5.0 30%
    4,000 4.9 6.0 51%
    6,000 4.8 7.0 65%
    8,000 4.5 8.1 70%

    12,000 3.2 9.5 64%

    Landmarks on the Fan Curve¶

    Three points on any fan curve have names, and they show up in specifications, field reports and customer questions.

    Shutoff pressure is the static pressure a fan develops at zero airflow, when the inlet or outlet is completely blocked. Curve A's shutoff pressure is 4.6 in. wg. Shutoff is not a zero-power condition: Curve A still absorbs 4.0 BHP at zero flow, and all of it goes into churning and heating the trapped air. Shutoff pressure tells a duct designer the most pressure the fan can ever put on the ductwork at that speed, which matters when a damper slams shut on a light-gauge duct or a sealed enclosure.

    Free delivery is the opposite end of the curve: the airflow a fan moves when it discharges against zero static pressure, with nothing connected to resist it. Extending Curve A's last two rows to zero pressure puts free delivery at about 16,500 CFM. Because static pressure is zero there, static efficiency is also zero, even though the fan is moving the most air it can. Free delivery is a useful ceiling for a propeller fan in an open wall, but a ducted fan never runs there, and a catalog CFM quoted "at free delivery" says nothing about performance against a real system.

    The peak pressure point is the highest static pressure on the curve and the airflow at which it occurs. On Curve A it is 4.9 in. wg at 4,000 CFM. The peak pressure point is the boundary of the stall region from Chapter 10: to its right the pressure falls steadily as airflow rises, and the fan is stable; to its left the curve is flat or rising and the fan is not. On a forward curved fan, with its mid-curve dip, the boundary is the right-hand peak.

    Operating to the left of the peak can produce surge: an unstable, cycling condition in which airflow and pressure oscillate instead of settling at one operating point. Where a system curve crosses a fan curve that is flat or rising, a small drop in flow raises the fan's pressure no further, so the fan and the air volume stored in the ductwork trade pressure back and forth. The symptoms are a rhythmic pulsing or "breathing" of the ductwork, a rumbling noise, fluctuating motor amps and vibration. The remedy is to move the operating point right of the peak: a smaller fan, a slower speed, less system resistance, or a bleed of air through a bypass.

    A Damper Can Push a Good Fan Into Surge

    A common trap is checking the selection only at the design point. An outlet damper throttled hard at low load moves the operating point left along the fan curve, and it can cross the peak pressure point. Check the lowest airflow the process will ask for, and if it falls left of the peak, use speed control or inlet vanes for that range instead of the damper.

    Efficiency on the Curve¶

    The Fan Power Equation¶

    Chapter 10 built brake horsepower from air power. Written in one line, that relation is the fan power equation: the shaft power a fan needs equals its airflow times its pressure, divided by a units constant and by its efficiency.

    Fan Power Equation¶

    \[ BHP = \frac{Q \times P}{6356 \times \eta} \]

    where:

    • \( BHP \) is brake horsepower at the fan shaft
    • \( Q \) is airflow in CFM at fan inlet density
    • \( P \) is fan pressure in in. wg (static or total)
    • \( \eta \) is the matching efficiency (static with static pressure, total with total pressure), as a decimal
    • 6356 converts CFM × in. wg to horsepower

    The equation runs in any direction. Given BHP from a catalog, it yields efficiency; given an efficiency target, it yields the BHP to expect.

    Static Efficiency¶

    Static efficiency is the fan's air power based on fan static pressure divided by its brake horsepower. Rearranging the fan power equation gives it directly.

    Static Efficiency¶

    \[ \eta_s = \frac{Q \times P_{sf}}{6356 \times BHP} \]

    where:

    • \( \eta_s \) is static efficiency, as a decimal
    • \( P_{sf} \) is fan static pressure in in. wg

    Static efficiency is always lower than total efficiency at the same point, because fan static pressure leaves out the outlet velocity pressure the fan has also produced. Specifications for ducted fans usually state static efficiency because the duty is stated in static pressure, and catalog performance tables give BHP so the reader can compute it.

    Worked Example: Two Fans for the Same Duty¶

    A plant needs 20,000 CFM at 5.0 in. wg fan static pressure, standard air, running 6,000 hours a year. Two candidate selections (illustrative) meet the duty. Fan X absorbs 21.5 BHP; Fan Y absorbs 24.0 BHP.

    1. Fan X: \( \eta_s = 20{,}000 \times 5.0 / (6356 \times 21.5) = 100{,}000 / 136{,}654 = 0.732 \), or 73.2%.
    2. Fan Y: \( \eta_s = 100{,}000 / (6356 \times 24.0) = 100{,}000 / 152{,}544 = 0.656 \), or 65.6%.
    3. Power difference: 2.5 BHP × 0.746 kW/hp = 1.87 kW at the shaft.
    4. Annual energy: 1.87 kW × 6,000 h = 11,190 kWh a year, before motor losses. At an illustrative $0.10 per kWh, that is about $1,120 a year, every year.

    A 7.6-point efficiency gap is worth more over the fan's life than a modest difference in purchase price. The comparison is fair only because both fans were evaluated at the same duty with the same pressure basis; comparing Fan X's static efficiency with Fan Y's total efficiency would be meaningless.

    The Efficiency Curve¶

    The efficiency curve is a plot of a fan's efficiency against airflow at one speed, drawn on the same airflow axis as the pressure and BHP curves. Curve A's static efficiency column traces its shape: zero at shutoff (no airflow), rising to a broad top, then falling back to zero at free delivery (no static pressure). Every fan's static efficiency curve has this hump, because static efficiency is zero at both ends of the pressure curve by definition. What differs between fan types is how high the hump rises and how wide it is. The efficiency curve turns "can this fan do the duty?" into "how well will it do it?", which is the question that drives operating cost.

    Peak Efficiency¶

    Peak efficiency is the highest efficiency on a fan's efficiency curve, and the airflow and pressure where it occurs. Curve A peaks at about 70% static efficiency between 8,000 and 10,000 CFM. Selecting at or near peak efficiency is the single best way to keep both power and noise down, since a fan running well off its peak wastes energy as turbulence. Peak efficiency is a property of the fan design at a given point of rating, not of one operating speed, which the fan laws below will make clear.

    Worked Example: Same Duty, Different Position on the Curve¶

    A customer needs 12,000 CFM at 3.2 in. wg. Curve A at 1,380 RPM passes exactly through that point, so it "meets the duty," but its static efficiency there is 64% and it absorbs 9.5 BHP. The duty sits to the right of the efficiency peak.

    Now consider the next larger size of the same design, selected so that 12,000 CFM at 3.2 in. wg falls at its peak of 70% (illustrative). Its BHP from the fan power equation is:

    \[ BHP = \frac{12{,}000 \times 3.2}{6356 \times 0.70} = \frac{38{,}400}{4{,}449} = 8.63 \text{ BHP} \]

    The larger fan saves about 0.87 BHP, roughly 9%, at the same airflow and pressure. It also turns slower, which usually means less noise and longer bearing life. Against that, it costs more and takes more space. Peak efficiency does not decide the selection on its own, but a selection far from peak should always prompt the question, "Why not the next size?"

    The Fan Laws¶

    What the Fan Laws Say¶

    The fan laws are a set of proportionality rules that predict how a fan's airflow, pressure and power change when its speed, its size or the air density changes. They hold for geometrically similar fans (the same design scaled up or down, or the same fan at a different speed) operating at the same point of rating, meaning the same relative position on their curves. Under those conditions the efficiency does not change, so every point on a fan curve maps to a point on the new curve.

    Fan Laws¶

    \[ \frac{Q_2}{Q_1} = \frac{N_2}{N_1} \left( \frac{D_2}{D_1} \right)^3 \]
    \[ \frac{P_2}{P_1} = \left( \frac{N_2}{N_1} \right)^2 \left( \frac{D_2}{D_1} \right)^2 \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} \]
    \[ \frac{BHP_2}{BHP_1} = \left( \frac{N_2}{N_1} \right)^3 \left( \frac{D_2}{D_1} \right)^5 \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} \]

    where:

    • \( Q \) is airflow in CFM, \( P \) is fan pressure in in. wg and \( BHP \) is brake horsepower
    • \( N \) is fan speed in RPM
    • \( D \) is impeller diameter (any consistent unit)
    • \( \rho \) is air density in lb/ft³
    • subscript 1 is the known condition and subscript 2 the new one

    For one fan at one density, the diameter and density ratios are 1 and the laws shrink to three short rules that are worth memorizing. Airflow varies directly with speed: each revolution sweeps the same volume, so 10% more speed moves 10% more air.

    Pressure varies as speed squared. Pressure developed by an impeller is proportional to the square of its tip speed, the same velocity-squared relation behind velocity pressure in Chapter 2. A 10% speed increase raises pressure by \( 1.1^2 = 1.21 \), or 21%. The same rule explains why system resistance rises with airflow squared: the system curve through zero and the fan law for pressure are the same parabola.

    Power varies with speed cubed. Power is airflow times pressure, so it carries one power of speed from airflow and two from pressure. A 10% speed increase raises BHP by \( 1.1^3 = 1.331 \), or 33%; a 20% speed reduction cuts it to \( 0.8^3 = 0.512 \), or about half; half speed needs one-eighth the power. This is the relationship behind the power savings of speed control in Chapter 18, and also the reason a modest speed-up can overload a motor that was comfortably sized.

    The Fan Laws Slide Along the System Curve

    Notice that airflow goes with speed and pressure with speed squared, so pressure goes with airflow squared: exactly the shape of a system curve through zero. When you change a fan's speed on a pure-resistance system, the operating point slides along the system curve and stays at the same efficiency. That is why the fan laws work so well for speed changes and why they need care when the system has a fixed pressure component.

    Speed Change Calculation¶

    A speed change calculation applies the fan laws to find the new speed, pressure and power when a fan on the same system must deliver a different airflow. It is the most common fan-law job: a building gets a new zone, a process adds a hood, or a test-and-balance report says the fan is short.

    Worked Example: Speeding Up for More Air¶

    Curve A runs at 1,380 RPM, delivering 10,000 CFM at 4.0 in. wg and 9.0 BHP. The system has no fixed pressure component (its curve passes through zero). The owner wants 11,500 CFM through the same system.

    1. Speed ratio. Airflow goes with speed: \( N_2/N_1 = 11{,}500 / 10{,}000 = 1.15 \).
    2. New speed. \( N_2 = 1{,}380 \times 1.15 = 1{,}587 \) RPM.
    3. New pressure. \( P_2 = 4.0 \times 1.15^2 = 4.0 \times 1.3225 = 5.29 \) in. wg. This is also the system's resistance at 11,500 CFM, as it must be.
    4. New power. \( BHP_2 = 9.0 \times 1.15^3 = 9.0 \times 1.521 = 13.69 \) BHP.
    5. Checks. A 15% airflow increase needs 52% more power, so the 10 hp motor is now overloaded and a 15 hp motor is required. The new speed must also be below the maximum safe speed for the fan's class from Chapter 10, and the drive must be resized for the new sheave ratio.

    The efficiency at the new point is still 70%: \( 11{,}500 \times 5.29 / (6356 \times 13.69) = 0.699 \). The calculation took five lines and a calculator, which is why the fan laws are the first tool to reach for when a customer asks "what if?"

    Size Change Calculation¶

    A size change calculation applies the fan laws to the next larger or smaller size of a geometrically similar fan. At the same speed, airflow scales with the cube of the diameter ratio, pressure with its square and power with its fifth power. If the next size up has a diameter 1.12 times larger (illustrative), then at the same RPM it moves \( 1.12^3 = 1.405 \) times the airflow at \( 1.12^2 = 1.254 \) times the pressure and absorbs \( 1.12^5 = 1.762 \) times the power. Size changes are usually paired with a lower speed, so the larger fan meets the same duty more slowly and more efficiently. Real product lines are not always perfectly similar from size to size, so for an order the catalog or selection software for the actual size governs; the fan laws give the estimate.

    Cold Start Horsepower¶

    The density term in the fan laws matters most for hot fans. Cold start horsepower is the brake horsepower a fan selected for hot air absorbs when it starts and runs on cold, denser air before the airstream reaches its operating temperature. At the same speed the fan moves the same CFM, but BHP rises in proportion to density. Chapter 10's oven exhaust fan absorbs 3.7 BHP on 200°F air; on 70°F air at start-up it absorbs the full standard-air value of 4.6 BHP, about 24% more, and on 0°F air it would absorb \( 4.6 \times 1.152 = 5.3 \) BHP. For high-temperature fans the gap is larger: a fan running on 600°F air has a temperature factor of 0.500, so its cold start horsepower is twice its running horsepower. The motor must be sized for the cold start condition, or the start-up must be managed with a damper, inlet vanes or speed control, a choice developed in Chapter 27.

    Sanity-Check Any Fan-Law Answer With the Exponents

    Here's a quick self-check: for a speed change, the pressure ratio must equal the airflow ratio squared and the power ratio must equal the airflow ratio cubed. If 15% more air came out with less than 50% more power, an exponent is wrong somewhere.

    Specific Speed¶

    Specific speed is a single number that characterizes a fan design by combining its speed, airflow and pressure at the point of rating, usually the peak efficiency point.

    Specific Speed¶

    \[ N_s = \frac{N \sqrt{Q}}{P^{3/4}} \]

    where:

    • \( N_s \) is specific speed (in the units used; not dimensionless as written)
    • \( N \) is speed in RPM, \( Q \) is airflow in CFM and \( P \) is pressure in in. wg

    Because the fan laws leave it unchanged when speed or size changes, specific speed belongs to the design, not to one fan. Curve A at its rated point gives \( 1{,}380 \times \sqrt{10{,}000} / 4.0^{0.75} \approx 48{,}800 \). Low specific speeds suit high-pressure, low-flow duties, the territory of radial and narrow centrifugal impellers; high specific speeds suit high-flow, low-pressure duties, the territory of axial fans. Values compare only when computed in the same units and pressure basis.

    Where to Select: Duty Point and Fan Energy Index¶

    Duty Point¶

    The duty point is the airflow and pressure, at a stated air density, that a specification requires the fan to deliver: for example, "10,000 CFM at 4.0 in. wg fan static pressure, standard air." It is the customer's target. The operating point from Chapter 10 is what actually happens where the fan and system curves cross, and the selection point is the catalog speed and BHP at which the fan meets the duty. On a good job all three coincide. When they do not, the gap usually traces to a missing density correction, a system resistance estimated wrong, or system effect, which this chapter covers next. The duty point is also where efficiency requirements are judged.

    Worked Example: Restoring the Duty Point After System Effect¶

    Chapter 10 left Curve A short of air: an elbow on its inlet added an illustrative 0.5 in. wg of system effect at 10,000 CFM, so the effective system curve passes through 4.5 in. wg at 10,000 CFM, and the fan at 1,380 RPM settles at about 9,560 CFM and 4.11 in. wg, absorbing about 8.80 BHP. The duty point is still 10,000 CFM. The fan laws find the fix.

    1. Speed ratio. Airflow goes with speed: \( 10{,}000 / 9{,}560 = 1.046 \).
    2. New speed. \( 1{,}380 \times 1.046 = 1{,}444 \) RPM.
    3. Pressure check. \( 4.11 \times 1.046^2 = 4.50 \) in. wg, matching the effective system curve at 10,000 CFM.
    4. Power. \( 8.80 \times 1.046^3 = 10.08 \) BHP.
    5. Motor. 10.08 BHP is above a 10 hp nameplate. Service factor is margin, not capacity (Chapter 14), so the motor goes to 15 hp, or the elbow is replaced with an inlet box or straight duct and the original 1,380 RPM and 10 hp stand.

    The 9,560 CFM point sits at about 70% efficiency on Curve A, so the speeded-up fan still operates at peak efficiency. The cost of the elbow is about 1.1 BHP, around 12% more power, every hour the fan runs.

    Fan Energy Index¶

    Efficiency at the duty point is now regulated. The Fan Energy Index (FEI) is the ratio of the electrical input power of a reference fan to the electrical input power of the actual fan, both delivering the same airflow and pressure. It is defined in AMCA Standard 208. The reference fan's power comes from a formula based only on the duty's airflow and pressure, so it is the same for every manufacturer.

    Fan Energy Index¶

    \[ FEI = \frac{FEP_{ref}}{FEP_{act}} \]

    where:

    • \( FEP_{ref} \) is the fan electrical input power of the reference fan at the duty point, in kW
    • \( FEP_{act} \) is the fan electrical input power of the actual fan at the same duty point, in kW, including motor, drive and any speed-control losses

    An FEI of 1.00 means the fan uses exactly the reference power; above 1.00 it uses less, below 1.00 it uses more. Because FEI is calculated at a duty point, one fan model has a different FEI at every point on its curve. Energy codes and standards, including ASHRAE Standard 90.1 and several state regulations, set minimum FEI values at the design point, so FEI is now part of many specifications. The older Fan Efficiency Grade, which rated a fan by its peak efficiency alone, is covered in Chapter 27.

    Worked Example: Same Fan, Two Duty Points¶

    Curve A is driven by a belt with 97% efficiency and a motor with 93% efficiency (illustrative). The reference powers below are illustrative values of the kind selection software reports from the AMCA 208 formula.

    Step Duty 1: 10,000 CFM at 4.0 in. wg Duty 2: 14,000 CFM at 2.0 in. wg
    BHP from Curve A 9.0 9.4
    Shaft power, kW (× 0.746) 6.71 7.01
    Actual electrical input, kW (÷ 0.97 ÷ 0.93) 7.44 7.77
    Reference electrical input, kW (illustrative) 9.0 6.8

    At Duty 1, near peak efficiency, the fan passes a 1.00 minimum with room to spare. At Duty 2, the same fan at the same speed sits far right on its curve at 47% static efficiency and fails. Nothing about the fan changed; its position on the curve did. A selection that fails FEI is usually fixed by choosing a different size so the duty lands closer to peak efficiency.

    System Effect in Detail¶

    Chapter 10 introduced system effect as the loss of fan performance caused by the way a fan is connected to its system, and its values come from AMCA Publication 201, Fans and Systems. This section separates the inlet and outlet cases and adds the vocabulary AMCA uses to rate and correct for them.

    AMCA Installation Type¶

    A fan's catalog rating depends on whether ducts were connected to its inlet and outlet during the test, because a duct changes how air approaches and leaves the impeller. The AMCA 210 test standard therefore defines four AMCA installation types, by whether each side of the fan is free (open to the surroundings) or ducted:

    • Type A: free inlet, free outlet
    • Type B: free inlet, ducted outlet
    • Type C: ducted inlet, free outlet
    • Type D: ducted inlet, ducted outlet

    A certified catalog rating states which installation type it applies to. Applying a rating made for one type to a job installed as another is a common source of field shortfall: a fan rated with a ducted outlet but installed discharging straight into a plenum loses the benefit of the outlet duct, and that loss is an outlet system effect. The installation type is part of the duty statement, just as density is.

    Worked Example: Classify the Job, Then Check the Rating¶

    An outside sales engineer is quoting four fans on one project and must confirm each rating basis:

    1. A plug (plenum) fan in an air handler cabinet. It draws from the open cabinet and discharges into the cabinet: free inlet, free outlet, Type A.
    2. A centrifugal fan with an open inlet funnel in an equipment room, discharging into a supply duct. Free inlet, ducted outlet, Type B.
    3. A roof upblast exhaust fan connected to a kitchen exhaust duct from below, discharging to atmosphere. Ducted inlet, free outlet, Type C.
    4. An inline fan in the middle of a duct run. Ducted on both sides, Type D.

    For each, the engineer compares the job's type with the type printed on the catalog rating. Where they match, the rating applies as published. Where they differ, the engineer either asks for a selection at the job's installation type or adds the appropriate system effect from AMCA 201 to the design pressure before selecting. A fan in the field never knows which page of the catalog it was selected from; it only knows how it was installed.

    Inlet System Effect¶

    Inlet system effect is the loss of fan performance caused by conditions at the fan inlet that keep air from entering the impeller smoothly and evenly. The impeller is designed for air arriving straight and uniform; anything else makes some blades work harder than others. The common causes are an elbow bolted directly to the inlet, which crowds air to one side; a plenum wall or obstruction too close to an open inlet; and swirl, or prerotation, in the incoming air. Swirl in the direction of impeller rotation reduces airflow and pressure much as an inlet vane does; swirl against rotation raises power. Inlet losses are usually larger than outlet losses for the same fault. FanPedia's answer to the elbow case is the inlet box, which "is designed to minimize the system effect of a 90 degree turn into the fan" (FanPedia p. 187).

    Effective Duct Length¶

    Air leaves a centrifugal fan outlet with a lopsided velocity profile, fastest along the outer wall of the scroll. It needs a length of straight duct to even out. Effective duct length is the length of straight outlet duct needed for that profile to become uniform, which AMCA 201 defines from the outlet velocity and the duct's equivalent diameter.

    Effective Duct Length¶

    \[ L_e = D_e \left( 2.5 + \frac{V - 2{,}500}{1{,}000} \right) \text{ for } V > 2{,}500 \text{ fpm}, \qquad L_e = 2.5\,D_e \text{ for } V \le 2{,}500 \text{ fpm} \]

    where:

    • \( L_e \) is the effective duct length (100% recovery), in the units of \( D_e \)
    • \( D_e \) is the equivalent duct diameter, \( \sqrt{4ab/\pi} \) for a rectangular outlet of sides \( a \) and \( b \)
    • \( V \) is the outlet velocity in fpm

    For a 24 × 30 in. outlet at 3,500 fpm, \( D_e = \sqrt{4 \times 24 \times 30 / \pi} = 30.3 \) in., and \( L_e = 3.5 \times 30.3 = 106 \) in., about 8.8 ft of straight duct before the first elbow.

    Outlet System Effect¶

    Outlet system effect is the loss of fan performance caused by the outlet connection: no outlet duct, an outlet duct shorter than the effective duct length, an elbow or damper too close to the outlet, or an abrupt expansion. Its size depends on how much of the effective duct length is present and on the outlet velocity, so a high-velocity fan with an elbow right at the outlet pays the most. An elbow placed after a full effective duct length adds only its ordinary duct loss; the same elbow bolted to the outlet adds a system effect on top. Where the outlet orientation forces a turn, choosing the discharge position so the elbow turns in the same direction as the scroll (Chapter 12) reduces the loss.

    System Effect Curve¶

    A system effect curve is a chart in AMCA 201 that gives system effect, in in. wg, against the velocity at the fan inlet or outlet. AMCA 201 presents a family of these curves, identified by letter, and tables that assign a curve to each connection: a given elbow at a given distance from the inlet, an outlet duct of a given fraction of the effective duct length, and so on. Because system effect is a velocity pressure loss, each curve rises with velocity squared. The procedure is to identify each faulty connection, look up its curve, read the system effect at the job's velocity, and add the result to the design pressure, as in the worked example above. Adding system effect to the designer's system curve produces the effective system curve the fan actually works against.

    System Effect Takes a Second Pass

    If the inlet cases, outlet cases, effective duct length and lettered curves feel like a lot, that's normal; most people need to work one real job before the tables make sense. Start with the two questions you already know, "What is on the inlet?" and "What is on the outlet?", and look up only what is actually there.

    Controlling Flow Efficiently¶

    Flow control efficiency describes how much of the possible power saving a flow-control method delivers when airflow is reduced. Chapter 18 compared the three methods at 70% airflow with illustrative values: an outlet damper needs about 86% of design power, variable inlet vanes about 67% and speed control about 34%. The damper is least efficient because it throws away pressure the fan has already made; FanPedia notes that outlet damper horsepower savings are "usually much less than other methods" (FanPedia p. 182).

    The 34% figure is the ideal cube law, and it holds only when the system curve passes through zero. Many systems have a fixed pressure component: a VAV system holding duct static pressure for its terminal units, or a process that must overcome a fixed back-pressure. Suppose 1.5 in. wg of a 4.0 in. wg design must be held at every flow. At 70% airflow the system needs \( 1.5 + 2.5 \times 0.7^2 = 2.73 \) in. wg, not \( 4.0 \times 0.7^2 = 1.96 \) in. wg, so air power falls to \( 0.7 \times 2.73 / 4.0 = 48\% \) of design rather than 34%. Speed control still wins, but by less, and the fan's efficiency shifts as the slowed curve crosses the system curve at a new relative position. Drive and motor losses at low load reduce the saving further.

    Fans in Parallel and in Series¶

    Two fans can be combined to serve one system, and the combination is predicted by adding their curves, not their ratings.

    Fans in parallel draw from and discharge into a common system side by side. At any pressure, their airflows add: the combined curve is found by doubling the airflow of each point of a single fan's curve. Parallel fans give redundancy and staging, and they add the most air when the system is flat (low resistance). On a steep system the second fan adds little, because more airflow costs a great deal more pressure. Parallel fans must each operate right of the peak pressure point, or one fan can take most of the flow while the other stalls, a form of the surge problem described above, and each needs a backdraft damper if it may run alone.

    Fans in series move the same air one after the other, outlet of one to inlet of the next. At any airflow, their pressures add: the combined curve is found by doubling the pressure of each point of a single fan's curve. Series fans suit steep, high-resistance systems. A multi-stage blower is a series arrangement inside one housing.

    The table below shows Curve A combined two ways on two illustrative systems: the steep design system from Chapter 10 (4.0 in. wg at 10,000 CFM) and a flat system needing only 1.0 in. wg at 10,000 CFM.

    System One fan Two in parallel Two in series
    Steep (4.0 in. wg at 10,000 CFM) 10,000 CFM at 4.0 in. wg about 11,000 CFM at 4.8 in. wg about 12,300 CFM at 6.0 in. wg
    Flat (1.0 in. wg at 10,000 CFM) about 14,000 CFM at 2.0 in. wg 20,000 CFM at 4.0 in. wg about 15,100 CFM at 2.3 in. wg

    Sound Pressure and Octave Bands¶

    Chapter 10 described fan sound by its sound power level, the total acoustic power a fan radiates, independent of where it is installed. A listener, a microphone or a property-line noise ordinance does not hear sound power. They respond to sound pressure.

    Sound Pressure Level¶

    Sound pressure level (Lp) is the magnitude of the pressure fluctuations of sound at one location, expressed in decibels relative to a reference pressure of 20 micropascals, roughly the threshold of human hearing.

    Sound Pressure Level¶

    \[ L_p = 20 \log_{10} \left( \frac{p}{20 \times 10^{-6}} \right) \]

    where:

    • \( L_p \) is the sound pressure level in dB
    • \( p \) is the root-mean-square sound pressure in pascals

    Unlike sound power, sound pressure depends on the surroundings: the distance from the fan, the size and hardness of the room, and reflections from nearby surfaces. Outdoors, away from reflecting surfaces, sound pressure level falls about 6 dB each time the distance from the source doubles. A sound pressure figure is therefore meaningless without its distance and setting. Manufacturers publish sound power because it belongs to the fan; acoustical consultants convert it to sound pressure at the listener's location for the actual room or site.

    Ask Three Questions About Any Noise Limit

    Want to save yourself an hour of rework? When a specification says "85 dB," ask: sound power or sound pressure, at what distance and in what setting, and overall or by octave band. The answers decide whether the catalog data can show compliance at all.

    Octave Band¶

    Fan sound is not one tone but a spread of frequencies, and its effect depends on where the energy falls: low-frequency rumble passes through walls and silencers more easily, while the ear is most sensitive in the middle range. An octave band is a range of frequencies whose upper limit is twice its lower limit, identified by its center frequency. Fan sound data is published in eight octave bands:

    Band Center frequency (Hz) Approximate range (Hz)
    1 63 44 to 88
    2 125 88 to 177
    3 250 177 to 354
    4 500 354 to 707
    5 1,000 707 to 1,414
    6 2,000 1,414 to 2,828
    7 4,000 2,828 to 5,657
    8 8,000 5,657 to 11,314

    Chapter 10's 10-blade impeller at 1,770 RPM has a blade pass frequency of 295 Hz, which falls in the 250 Hz band, so that band will usually stand out in the fan's data. Octave band data lets an acoustical engineer choose a silencer that attacks the right frequencies; a single overall number, such as an A-weighted level that discounts low frequencies to match the ear, cannot. Reading octave band sound data in a submittal is part of Chapter 27.

    You Can Predict a Fan's Next Move

    You can now take one fan curve and predict what happens when the speed, size or air density changes, find the speed that restores a duty point lost to system effect, and judge a selection by its efficiency and FEI. That is the core of every "what if?" call an application engineer takes.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Landmarks: shutoff pressure is the pressure at zero airflow, free delivery the airflow at zero static pressure, and the peak pressure point the top of the pressure curve. Select right of the peak; left of it the fan can stall and surge.
    • Fan power equation: \( BHP = Q \times P / (6356 \times \eta) \). Static efficiency uses fan static pressure; compare candidate fans only on the same pressure basis.
    • The efficiency curve is zero at shutoff and free delivery and peaks in between. Selections near peak efficiency use less power and make less noise.
    • Fan laws: airflow varies with speed, pressure with speed squared, power with speed cubed. Airflow varies with diameter cubed, pressure with diameter squared and power with diameter to the fifth. Pressure and power vary directly with density; CFM does not.
    • Speed change: a 15% airflow increase on the same system needs 15% more speed, 32% more pressure and 52% more power. Always recheck the motor and the maximum safe speed.
    • Cold start horsepower is the BHP on cold, dense air; a 600°F fan absorbs twice its running power at a 70°F start.
    • Duty point and FEI: the duty point is what the specification requires; FEI compares the fan's electrical input power with a reference fan at that point, and the same fan can pass at one duty point and fail at another.
    • System effect: classify the job by AMCA installation type (A, B, C, D), allow an effective duct length of straight outlet duct, keep elbows off the inlet, and add any system effect from AMCA 201 curves to the design pressure.
    • Flow control: speed control saves the most power, but less than the ideal cube law when the system has a fixed pressure component.
    • Combining fans: parallel fans add airflow at the same pressure and help most on flat systems; series fans add pressure at the same airflow and help most on steep systems.
    • Sound: sound power belongs to the fan; sound pressure depends on distance and surroundings. Fan sound data comes in eight octave bands from 63 to 8,000 Hz.
