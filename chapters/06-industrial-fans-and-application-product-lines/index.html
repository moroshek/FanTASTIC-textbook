    Industrial Fans and Application Product Lines¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter finishes the product-line survey with the heavier-duty and application-specific fans: plug fans, vaneaxial fans, pressure blowers, radial bladed and radial tip fans, duct fans, filtered supply fans, utility sets, air kits, paint booth exhaust fans, and dust collection fans. It closes with how laboratory exhaust fans use bypass flow and plume dilution to discharge contaminated air safely. After completing it, readers know the full range of fan families and product lines the rest of the book refers to.

    From the Plant Floor to the Lab Roof

    The calls that test a new engineer or rep rarely ask for "a fan." They ask for something that can live in the wall of a hot curing oven, chew through paper trim, or throw lab fumes high above a roof. By the end of this chapter you will know which product line each of those calls is really asking for, and why. Let's follow the airflow.

    Chapters 4 and 5 sorted fans into families and walked through the roof, wall and building-ventilation products most people picture when they hear the word "fan." This chapter finishes the survey with the lines that show up on industrial orders and in application-specific specifications. Some are defined by how they are built, such as a plug fan that mounts through a plenum wall or a vaneaxial fan with a vane section behind its impeller. Others are defined by the job they do: dust collection, paint booth exhaust and laboratory fume exhaust. A customer may describe either the hardware ("we need a radial blade") or the problem ("our cyclone keeps plugging the fan"), and you should land on the same product.

    Plug Fans: The Impeller Goes In, the Drive Stays Out¶

    Chapter 5 described the plenum fan, an unhoused centrifugal fan that sits inside an air-filled box (the plenum) and pressurizes it, with the plenum walls doing the job a scroll housing would otherwise do. A plug fan is the industrial cousin of that idea. FanPedia describes it as an unhoused centrifugal fan "plugged" into the wall of a plenum: a mounting panel fastens to the outside of the plenum wall, the impeller sits inside, and air moves through an inlet funnel into the impeller and out into the pressurized space (FanPedia p. 5). The current Twin City Fan catalog page adds the feature that sells the design: the gusseted mounting panel separates the motor and drive components from the process air, and the fan can be installed and removed as a unit.

    That separation is the key difference from a plenum fan, whose motor, shaft and bearings live in the air the impeller moves. FanPedia names the typical industrial uses directly: high-temperature ovens and OEM paint booths, where the air being recirculated is either too hot or too dirty to wash over a motor (FanPedia p. 5). The current catalog lists high-temperature construction to 1000 °F, spark resistant construction, ATEX construction, special materials and a pedestal design for floor mounting as options.

    One Wall, Two Environments

    Think of the mounting panel as a property line: process air on one side, motor and bearings on the other. Almost every plug fan decision, from arrangement to temperature option, comes back to protecting that line.

    Plug fans come in several arrangements, the standard ways the drive, bearings and impeller are put together (Chapter 11 covers arrangements in full). FanPedia's technical descriptions define six for plug fans, and they differ on two questions: is the fan direct drive or belt driven, and does the customer's wall carry the whole fan, or does the fan stand on the foundation ("grade")? The "P" suffix marks the pedestal, grade-mounted version.

    • Arrangement 4: direct drive, impeller mounted on the motor shaft; compact and low maintenance because there is no fan shaft, bearings or belts. High airstream temperatures may limit its use.
    • Arrangement 8: direct drive with the motor shaft coupled to a separate fan shaft; the whole assembly hangs on the wall. It is the least common plug arrangement because of its length.
    • Arrangement 9: the most common plug arrangement, fully supported by the customer's wall, belt driven on an adjustable motor base.
    • Arrangements 4P, 8P and 9P: the same three, mounted to grade. FanPedia gives two reasons to choose them: the customer's wall may not be sufficient by itself, and foundation mounting helps meet lower vibration requirements. The mounting panel is optional on the P versions.

    Worked example: choosing a plug fan arrangement. An OEM builds curing ovens and wants a recirculating fan mounted through the oven's side wall. The oven runs hot, the OEM wants belt drive so the fan speed can be trimmed after startup, and its engineer says the oven wall panel was never designed to carry a large motor. Work through the three questions in order:

    1. Direct or belt? The customer asked for belt drive, so Arrangements 9 and 9P remain.
    2. Wall or grade? The wall cannot carry the full weight, so the grade-mounted version wins: Arrangement 9P.
    3. Temperature? A hot airstream would have ruled out Arrangement 4 (impeller on the motor shaft); 9P keeps the motor outside the oven, and high-temperature construction is still ordered as a separate option.

    The answer is an Arrangement 9P plug fan with high-temperature construction. Twin City Fan's current plug fan lines include backward curved high-efficiency models (BEPL, BFPL) and airfoil and backward inclined models for non-HVAC duty (AFPL, BCPL); check the catalog for the sizes and limits of each.

    Vaneaxial Fans and Roof Mounted Panel Fans¶

    Chapter 5 introduced the tubeaxial fan: an axial impeller turning inside a plain tubular housing. Air leaving any axial impeller carries a swirl, because the blades push it around the axis as well as along it. A vaneaxial fan adds a section of stationary guide vanes inside the housing to straighten that airflow as it moves through the fan (FanPedia p. 7). Straightening matters because swirling air carries energy that does not show up as useful pressure in the duct; the vanes turn part of that rotational energy into static pressure. That is why the current Twin City Fan catalog describes vaneaxial fans as built for large volumes of air at moderate to high pressures, in a tubular package that uses minimal space. Current models include TCVA (cast aluminum impeller), TCVX (adjustable-pitch aluminum impeller) and TCVS (steel impeller), in direct and belt driven versions; FanPedia's exploded views show the guide vanes sitting behind the impeller in both drive types (FanPedia p. 16).

    Worked example: why a vaneaxial earns its vanes. A process supply job calls for 20,000 CFM through a 36-inch round duct, with a system pressure too high for a plain tubeaxial to deliver efficiently, and there is no floor space for a housed centrifugal. First check the duct velocity. The flow area is

    \[ A = \pi \left(\frac{36}{2 \times 12}\right)^2 = 7.07 \ \text{ft}^2 \]

    so the average velocity is \( V = 20{,}000 / 7.07 \approx 2{,}830 \) fpm. For standard air, velocity pressure is \( VP = (V/4005)^2 \approx 0.50 \) in. wg. That is a meaningful slice of energy riding in the axial velocity alone; any swirl the impeller adds on top is energy the duct cannot use. A vaneaxial fan's vane section recovers part of that swirl as static pressure, so the same inline package reaches pressures a tubeaxial cannot. The decision follows from the job: large volume, moderate-to-high pressure, straight duct, little space. That combination is the vaneaxial's home ground. (The flow, diameter and the claim that a tubeaxial falls short are illustrative; confirm with ratings from the current catalog.)

    The panel fan from Chapter 4 is an axial impeller centered in a flat, panel-shaped housing, usually wall mounted. A roof mounted panel fan uses that panel fan as its base model and adds roof accessories such as a stack cap or a hood (FanPedia p. 8). With a stack cap the air moves in a straight line up through the fan. With a hood the air moves straight through the fan and is then deflected down and outward for exhaust, or flows in the reverse direction for supply. On a drawing, the giveaway is a flat panel unit with a weatherhood or stack cap sitting on a curb, rather than the round tube of a roof mounted axial fan.

    Heavy-Duty Centrifugals for Dirty Air and High Pressure¶

    The next three product lines are all housed centrifugal fans: a scroll housing around a rotating impeller, with air entering along the shaft and leaving at a right angle to it (FanPedia p. 4). What separates them is the blade shape and the airstream each is built to survive. A particulate-laden airstream carries solids such as dust, chips, fibers or sticky material, and it destroys or clogs impellers designed for clean air. Chapter 8 treats impeller types in depth; here the goal is to recognize the product lines.

    Radial Bladed Fans¶

    A radial bladed fan has flat blades that point straight out from the hub, like the spokes of a wheel. Material striking a flat radial blade tends to slide off rather than build up, and the blades can be made from heavy plate. The current Twin City Fan catalog calls the line the "workhorse" of the industry: heavy-gauge, all-welded construction proven by years of handling dirty, abrasive, sticky or bulky particulate-laden airstreams.

    FanPedia shows five radial bladed impeller styles (FanPedia p. 32), each a variation on the same flat blade:

    • Paddle impeller (open type): single-thickness radial paddle blades with no plates.
    • Paddle impeller with end rings: the open design with front and back end rings added.
    • Material handling impeller (wool): the open design with a full back plate.
    • Paper handling impeller: a full back plate with gussets for extra rugged durability.
    • Air handling impeller: heavy-gauge blades welded to both a back plate and a front plate.

    The current catalog's model codes follow those names: RBO and RBR (paddle impellers), RBW (back plate wool impeller), RBP (paper handling impeller), RBA (air handling impeller) and JRW (compact design).

    Worked example: reading the impeller from the airstream. A paper converting plant wants to convey edge trim from a slitter line to a baler. The airstream is long, light strips of paper, the material passes through the fan, and the customer complains that the previous fan "wrapped up." Start with the family: material passing through the fan rules out the high-efficiency backward inclined and airfoil designs, which are meant for cleaner air, and points to a radial bladed fan. Then pick the impeller: FanPedia names a paper handling impeller with a full back plate and gussets, and the catalog sells it as model RBP. Nothing has been sized yet, but the line and the impeller are narrowed, which is the first job on any industrial inquiry. Confirm materials and any abrasion resistant option against the current catalog.

    Radial Tip Fans¶

    A radial tip fan splits the difference between a radial blade and a forward curved blade. FanPedia defines its impeller as a blade curved forward at the entering edge and radial at the tip of the leaving edge (FanPedia p. 31). The radial tip suits dirty, particulate-laden air, and the curved entry lets air onto the blade more smoothly. The current catalog states the payoff: with high airflows, the radial tip fan is much more efficient than a comparable radial blade fan. Twin City Fan sells radial tip fans as RTF (industrial) and HRT (industrial, high specific speed), alongside the high-efficiency HAF (airfoil) and HIB (backward curved) fans meant for high temperatures, corrosive airstreams and light particulate.

    To see why that efficiency matters, recall that the power a fan draws grows with airflow and pressure and shrinks with efficiency. Fan brake horsepower is

    Fan Brake Horsepower¶

    \[ \text{BHP} = \frac{Q \times TP}{6356 \times \eta} \]

    where:

    • \( Q \) is the airflow in cubic feet per minute (CFM)
    • \( TP \) is the fan total pressure in inches of water gauge (in. wg)
    • \( \eta \) is the fan total efficiency, as a decimal
    • 6356 is the unit constant that converts CFM × in. wg to horsepower

    Worked example: radial blade or radial tip? A dryer exhaust carries light, dusty air at 30,000 CFM and 12 in. wg total pressure. Suppose a radial bladed selection runs at 60% total efficiency and a radial tip selection runs at 72% (illustrative efficiencies, not catalog values). Then

    \[ \text{BHP}_{\text{radial blade}} = \frac{30{,}000 \times 12}{6356 \times 0.60} \approx 94.4 \]
    \[ \text{BHP}_{\text{radial tip}} = \frac{30{,}000 \times 12}{6356 \times 0.72} \approx 78.7 \]

    The radial tip draws about 15.7 BHP less for as long as it runs, which on a continuous process fan adds up quickly. The radial blade still wins when the airstream is bulky, sticky or abrasive enough that efficiency must give way to durability. That trade-off, efficiency at high airflow versus survival in the worst material, is the question to ask whenever a customer says "dirty air."

    Ask What Passes Through the Impeller

    On any dirty-air inquiry, ask one question first: "Does the material go through the fan, or is the fan on the clean side of a filter?" Through the fan points you to radial bladed or radial tip; clean side opens up the high-efficiency backward inclined designs.

    Pressure Blowers¶

    A pressure blower is a housed centrifugal built for high pressure at relatively low airflow. FanPedia notes that pressure blowers have narrower housings and impeller widths than standard centrifugal fans (FanPedia p. 4). The current catalog adds two practical traits: performance stays stable through the operating range, and the blower can be turned down to zero flow with a discharge damper. Impeller choices include radial back plate, backward curved, open radial and shrouded types, and housings are fabricated steel, cast aluminum or cast iron (FanPedia p. 62 shows the cast versions). Typical uses are dust collection, flue gas recirculation, material handling, explosion-proof processes and wet well station exhaust. Cast aluminum pressure blowers can carry a slide gate damper for manual airflow adjustment (FanPedia p. 192).

    The following table pulls the three heavy-duty lines together.

    Product line Blade shape Best fit Trade-off
    Radial bladed fan Flat radial blades Abrasive, sticky, bulky particulate passing through the fan Rugged, but less efficient at high airflow
    Radial tip fan Curved at entry, radial at tip High airflow, dirty particulate-laden air More efficient than radial blade at high airflow; not for the bulkiest material
    Pressure blower Several, in a narrow housing High pressure at relatively low airflow Small volumes; turned down with a damper

    Packaged Centrifugal Product Lines¶

    Several product lines package a centrifugal fan for a common job, from a complete outdoor unit down to bare impellers for an OEM's enclosure. Four appear on industrial orders often enough to learn by name.

    Utility Sets¶

    A utility set is a compact, belt- or direct-driven single-inlet centrifugal package for general exhaust and supply in commercial and light industrial buildings. The current Twin City Fan catalog notes it works indoors, and outdoors with a weather cover that encloses the motor and drives, with housings in lock-seam galvanized steel, welded heavy-gauge steel, aluminum or stainless steel. FanPedia ties the term to an arrangement: for Class I and II fans in sizes 122 through 365, Arrangement 10 units are commonly called ventilating sets, with the adjustable motor base mounted inside the bearing pedestal (FanPedia p. 167). That makes Arrangement 10 more compact than Arrangement 9, and it is suitable for roof or outdoor installations with the optional weather cover.

    Current utility set models are grouped by impeller:

    • Backward inclined: DCV (direct drive), BCV (belt driven), BCVR (belt driven, kitchen exhaust), BCVSH (smoke and heat).
    • Airfoil: BAV (belt driven).
    • Forward curved: DDF (direct drive), FCV (utility set).

    Worked example: matching three requests to utility set models. A contractor sends three line items for the same building. Item one is a rooftop kitchen exhaust fan; the catalog's kitchen exhaust utility set is BCVR, and the outdoor location means the weather cover goes on the order. Item two is a fan that must run during a fire to remove smoke and heat; that points to BCVSH, the smoke and heat version, and to the UL listings shown for the line (UL 705, UL 762 and UL smoke and heat). Item three is a small indoor general exhaust fan where the customer wants no belts to service; a direct drive model, DCV (backward inclined) or DDF (forward curved), fits. Notice what you did: you used the application to choose within one product line before anyone opened a performance table. Sizing comes later, with the methods in Chapter 26.

    Duct Fans¶

    A duct fan is a double-inlet scroll fan mounted inside an enclosure: air is drawn through both inlets of the fan and discharged at a right angle to the fan shaft (FanPedia p. 4). The enclosed unit is installed as a package in the duct system, the network of ducts that carries air to and from the fan.

    Worked example: what the second inlet buys you. A double-width, double-inlet (DWDI) wheel behaves roughly like two single-width, single-inlet (SWSI) wheels of the same diameter placed back to back. At the same speed and pressure it moves roughly twice the air. If a SWSI wheel of a given diameter moves about 4,000 CFM at 1 in. wg, the DWDI version of that diameter would move on the order of 8,000 CFM at the same pressure and speed (illustrative numbers). That is a large volume from a compact wheel, which is what fits inside an enclosure. When you see a drawing with a scroll housing inside a rectangular box and inlets on both sides of the wheel, read it as a duct fan, not a utility set (single inlet) or a square inline fan (straight-through tube).

    Filtered Supply Fans and Air Kits¶

    A filtered supply fan delivers filtered outside air into a building. FanPedia describes filtered configurations as roof mounted units with forward curved or backward inclined impellers that pull outside air through filters and move it downward into the building (FanPedia p. 4). The current catalog describes them as belt driven centrifugal fans that supply make-up air to buildings such as manufacturing plants, warehouses and auditoriums, replacing air lost through industrial processes, fume hood exhaust or general ventilation. Models include BCRFS (single side intake) and LPSF (louvered penthouse). On a site, look for a roof unit with a filter section and a supply opening pointing down into the curb.

    An air kit is a fan sold without a housing, as a component for someone else's enclosure. The current Twin City Fan catalog describes air kits as forward curved impellers in a double-width, double-inlet arrangement. The forward curved design allows a low operating speed and economically sized shafting. Air kits provide reliable, quiet operation in ovens and plenums where it is not desirable to have the fan bearings in the airstream, and they move large volumes (up to 100,000 CFM) at static pressures up to 6 in. wg in a compact design. Air kits are supplied as a single fan kit (one impeller centered in the plenum) or a twin fan kit (two impellers on a common shaft, spaced so the restriction is the same at all four inlets). FanPedia shows the air kit as an Arrangement 3 assembly whose enclosure and insulation are provided by others in the field, and notes that air kits need support supplied by Twin City Fan or others rather than a pedestal (FanPedia pp. 83, 115).

    An Air Kit Is Not a Finished Fan

    A common trap is quoting an air kit as if the housing, supports and insulation come with it. FanPedia is explicit that the enclosure and insulation are provided by others, so write the scope split on the quote and confirm who supplies the supports before the order is released.

    Application Fans: Paint Booths and Dust Collectors¶

    The next two product lines are named for the job, not the hardware. Each one draws on fan types you already know, and the skill is recognizing which construction features the job demands.

    A paint booth exhaust fan removes fumes and overspray from a spray booth. The current Twin City Fan application page sets out the requirements: airflow must be precise, with velocity low enough to keep a consistent flow into and out of the booth but high enough to carry fumes and overspray out of the work area. The impeller must be easy to clean and service, and efficiency and noise also matter. Twin City Fan answers the cleaning need with swingout construction on centrifugal fans, and clamshell or swingout construction on axial fans. FanPedia describes swingout fans as having the whole impeller, shaft and bearing assembly mounted on a large door that swings open, and clamshell fans as opening to give complete access to the fan interior without removing ductwork (FanPedia p. 123). The commonly used fans include the TCBS paint booth exhaust tubeaxial fan, TCVA vaneaxial fans, plug and plenum fans, and single-width airfoil and backward inclined centrifugals.

    A dust collection fan is the fan that moves air through a dust collector. The current application page states the split that matters: backward inclined fans are used on the clean side of a filter, and radial bladed fans are used for cyclone applications, where the dust-laden air reaches the fan. Twin City Fan also builds top-mounted and side-mounted units, and lists radial tip fans (HRT, RTF) and pressure blowers among the fans commonly used on collectors. The position of the fan relative to the filter is the first fact to pin down, because it decides between a high-efficiency clean-air fan and a rugged dirty-air fan.

    Application What the job demands Typical answer
    Paint booth exhaust Precise velocity, easy impeller cleaning, efficiency, low noise Swingout or clamshell construction; tubeaxial, vaneaxial, plug, plenum or SWSI centrifugal
    Dust collector, clean side of filter Efficient air moving after the dust is removed Backward inclined centrifugal
    Dust collector, cyclone (dirty side) Dust-laden air through the fan Radial bladed fan

    Laboratory Exhaust: Dilute, Then Throw It High¶

    Chapter 4 introduced the laboratory fume exhaust fan, and Chapter 5 explained entrained flow, the outside air a lab fan's nozzle or windband pulls into the exhaust jet. This section completes that picture with the inline product line and the flow bookkeeping behind every lab exhaust selection.

    An inline lab exhaust fan is a lab exhaust fan built on a tubular inline centrifugal or mixed flow fan: the housing is a tube and air moves straight through the fan with a rotating impeller (FanPedia p. 6). Current Twin City Fan inline models include the induced-flow, mixed flow TVIFE (direct drive) and QIFE (belt driven), and the venturi nozzle QFE (mixed flow) and TFE (inline centrifugal). The housed centrifugal versions, BAIFE and BCIFE, use scroll housings instead. FanPedia shows isolation dampers, bypass dampers and actuators on both groups (FanPedia p. 138).

    The purpose of all this hardware is plume dilution: mixing fresh outside air into the contaminated exhaust so it leaves the stack at a lower concentration, then discharging it at high velocity so the plume rises up and away from the building. The current catalog explains the stakes: lab fumes can be hazardous in concentrated form, and the exhaust must not return into the building or the surrounding areas where people may be present. Two streams of outside air do the diluting (FanPedia pp. 4, 6):

    • Bypass flow is fresh outside air drawn in through a bypass air plenum at the fan inlet, controlled by bypass dampers. It enters before the impeller, so it passes through the fan. In common practice the bypass damper also opens as fume hood sashes close, keeping the fan's flow and stack velocity steady while the lab's exhaust varies.
    • Entrained flow is outside air pulled into the jet at the nozzle or windband after the air leaves the fan. It never passes through the impeller.

    Three Streams, One Plume

    Notice where each stream joins: contaminated air and bypass air go through the impeller together, and entrained air joins only after the fan. Once you place each stream on that path, every lab exhaust number on a submittal falls into place.

    Putting the streams together gives the lab exhaust total flow: the full volume leaving the stack as a plume. FanPedia's lab exhaust diagrams label the parts as contaminated air, bypass flow, fan flow, entrained flow and total flow (FanPedia pp. 4, 6). The relationships are:

    Lab Exhaust Fan Flow and Total Flow¶

    \[ Q_{\text{fan}} = Q_{\text{contaminated}} + Q_{\text{bypass}} \]
    \[ Q_{\text{total}} = Q_{\text{fan}} + Q_{\text{entrained}} \]

    where:

    • \( Q_{\text{contaminated}} \) is the contaminated air drawn from the fume hoods, in CFM
    • \( Q_{\text{bypass}} \) is the fresh air drawn through the bypass air plenum, in CFM
    • \( Q_{\text{fan}} \) is the fan flow, the air that actually passes through the impeller, in CFM
    • \( Q_{\text{entrained}} \) is the outside air entrained at the nozzle or windband, in CFM
    • \( Q_{\text{total}} \) is the lab exhaust total flow leaving the stack, in CFM

    Worked example. A lab's fume hoods exhaust 8,000 CFM of contaminated air. The bypass damper admits 2,000 CFM, so the fan flow is 10,000 CFM. If the fan's nozzle entrains another 10,000 CFM (an illustrative value; real entrainment comes from the manufacturer's rating for the selected fan and nozzle), the total flow is 20,000 CFM. Contaminated air is then \( 8{,}000 / 20{,}000 = 40\% \) of the plume. At night, when sashes close and the hoods exhaust only 5,000 CFM, the bypass damper opens to 5,000 CFM and the fan flow stays at 10,000 CFM; with the same 10,000 CFM entrained, the contaminated share falls to 25%.

    Don't Size the Fan to the Total Flow

    The impeller only moves the fan flow; the entrained air is extra volume that the nozzle adds outside the fan. Select the fan on contaminated plus bypass flow, and report total flow separately as the plume volume.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • A plug fan mounts through a plenum wall, keeping motor and drive out of the process air; Arrangement 9 is most common, and P versions mount to grade.
    • A vaneaxial fan uses guide vanes to straighten swirl for moderate to high pressure in a compact tube; a roof mounted panel fan is a panel fan with a stack cap or hood.
    • Radial bladed fans survive abrasive, sticky or bulky material; radial tip fans handle dirty air more efficiently at high airflow; pressure blowers give high pressure at relatively low volume.
    • Utility sets are packaged Arrangement 10 ventilating sets; duct fans are double-inlet scroll fans in an enclosure; filtered supply fans filter outside make-up air; air kits are forward curved DWDI impellers for enclosures built by others.
    • Paint booth exhaust fans need precise velocity and easy cleaning; dust collection fans depend on fan position: backward inclined on the clean side, radial bladed for cyclones.
    • Inline lab exhaust fans achieve plume dilution with bypass flow and entrained flow. Fan flow is contaminated plus bypass; lab exhaust total flow adds entrained flow.
