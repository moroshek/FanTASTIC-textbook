    Fan Performance, Curves and Ratings¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter explains how a fan's performance is described and rated. It covers fan static and total pressure, brake horsepower, fan efficiency, tip speed and outlet velocity; the fan curve, the system curve, and the operating point where they meet; the stall region; overloading and non-overloading power curves; density, temperature, and altitude corrections; sound power, decibels, and sones; system effect; the AMCA 210 rating standard; and fan class and maximum safe speed. After completing it, readers can read a fan curve and find where a fan will operate in a system.

    Welcome to Fan Performance

    Every fan order eventually comes down to one question from the customer: will it move the air I need against the resistance I have? This chapter teaches you to answer that from a fan curve and a catalog rating, instead of from a hunch. By the end you will find the point where a fan and a duct system agree, and you will know what changes that point. Let's follow the airflow.

    What a Fan Performance Rating Says¶

    Fan performance is the complete statement of what a fan does at one set of conditions: how much air it moves, how much pressure it develops, how fast it turns, how much power it takes, and the air density all of those numbers assume. A single performance rating is therefore never just "10,000 CFM." It is 10,000 cubic feet per minute at a stated pressure, at a stated speed, with a stated power, for air of a stated density. Remove any one of those qualifiers and the number cannot be checked, compared or ordered.

    Chapter 1 introduced the raw quantities: airflow volume in cubic feet per minute (CFM), static pressure in inches of water gauge (in. wg), fan speed in revolutions per minute (RPM), and horsepower. Chapter 2 added velocity pressure, total pressure and standard air, the reference condition of 0.075 lb/ft³ that catalogs rate fans at. Fan performance ties those quantities together for one particular fan. The same fan, turning at the same speed, can deliver a whole family of airflow and pressure combinations, and which combination it actually delivers depends on the system it is connected to. That dependence is why this chapter needs both a fan curve and a system curve before it can say where a fan will run.

    The five quantities that make up a complete rating, and what each one tells you on a job, are:

    • Airflow volume (CFM). The volume of air passing through the fan each minute, measured at the fan inlet density. This is what the customer usually asks for first.
    • Pressure (in. wg). The pressure the fan develops to push that airflow through the system, stated as fan static pressure or fan total pressure (both defined below).
    • Speed (RPM). How fast the impeller turns to deliver that airflow and pressure. Speed decides the motor and drive, and it is checked against the fan's maximum safe speed.
    • Brake horsepower (BHP). The power the fan shaft absorbs at that point, which decides the motor size.
    • Density (lb/ft³). The air density the other four numbers assume. Catalog ratings assume standard air unless stated otherwise.

    Worked Example: Reading One Rating Line¶

    A catalog performance table lists, for one fan size, a row for each airflow and a column for each static pressure. Suppose an application engineer reads the following cell for an illustrative belt-driven centrifugal fan (the numbers are illustrative, not from a specific Twin City Fan catalog):

    Quantity Value in the cell What it means for the order
    Airflow volume 10,000 CFM The volume the customer asked for
    Static pressure 4.0 in. wg The resistance the fan must overcome at that flow
    Speed 1,380 RPM The fan shaft speed the drive must produce
    Brake horsepower 9.0 BHP The shaft power, before drive losses
    Density 0.075 lb/ft³ Standard air; a hot or high-altitude job must be corrected

    Reading the cell correctly produces three action items. First, the motor must deliver at least 9.0 horsepower at the fan shaft plus any belt drive losses, so a 10 hp motor is the likely choice; motor selection is covered in Chapter 14. Second, the drive must turn the fan at 1,380 RPM, and that speed must be below the maximum safe speed for the fan's class. Third, if the job air is not at standard density, the 4.0 in. wg and 9.0 BHP do not apply as written; the section on density correction below shows how to convert them. A sales engineer who quotes "10,000 CFM" from this cell without the other four numbers has not quoted a fan.

    Performance Is a Pair, Not a Number

    Notice that a fan never "has" an airflow by itself. It has a curve of possible airflow and pressure pairs, and the duct system picks one of them. Keep asking "at what pressure?" every time you see a CFM, and the rest of this chapter will fall into place.

    The Fan Outlet: Area, Velocity and Velocity Pressure¶

    Before a rating can be split into static and total pressure, three outlet quantities need names, because the difference between the two pressures lives at the fan outlet.

    Fan outlet area is the inside cross-sectional area of the fan's discharge opening, in square feet, as the fan manufacturer defines it for rating purposes. For a housed centrifugal fan it is the inside width times the inside height of the discharge collar; for an axial fan it is the open annulus or circle at the discharge. It is a fixed property of the fan size, it appears in catalog dimension and performance data, and it is the area used to compute outlet velocity. Accessories that change the outlet change this area: FanPedia describes an evasé as a section of ductwork bolted to the fan discharge whose outlet is larger than its inlet, which "ultimately expands the outlet area of the fan" in order "to reduce the outlet velocity of the fan and to increase the static pressure capability" (FanPedia p. 146). FanPedia also notes that for some fans the published ratings include the influence of an evasé, so check the catalog notes before assuming the bare-fan outlet area applies.

    Outlet velocity is the average speed of the air leaving the fan, in feet per minute (FPM), found by dividing the airflow volume by the fan outlet area. It follows directly from the airflow continuity equation in Chapter 2:

    Outlet Velocity¶

    \[ V_o = \frac{Q}{A_o} \]

    where:

    • \( V_o \) is the outlet velocity in feet per minute (FPM)
    • \( Q \) is the airflow volume in cubic feet per minute (CFM)
    • \( A_o \) is the fan outlet area in square feet (ft²)

    Outlet velocity pressure is the velocity pressure that corresponds to the outlet velocity: the kinetic energy of the leaving air, expressed in inches of water gauge. It uses the velocity pressure equation from Chapter 2, which for standard air reduces to a simple form:

    Outlet Velocity Pressure at Standard Air¶

    \[ P_{v,o} = \left( \frac{V_o}{4005} \right)^2 \]

    where:

    • \( P_{v,o} \) is the outlet velocity pressure in in. wg
    • \( V_o \) is the outlet velocity in FPM
    • 4005 is the constant that applies to air at the standard density of 0.075 lb/ft³

    For air at another density \( \rho \) in lb/ft³, the general form is \( P_v = \rho \left( V/1096 \right)^2 \), which gives the same answer at 0.075 lb/ft³. Outlet velocity pressure is the bridge between the two pressure ratings: it is exactly the amount by which fan total pressure exceeds fan static pressure.

    Worked Example: Outlet Velocity for a Ducted Fan¶

    A fan moves 10,000 CFM through an outlet with an inside area of 4.0 ft². The outlet velocity is:

    \[ V_o = \frac{10{,}000}{4.0} = 2{,}500 \text{ FPM} \]

    The outlet velocity pressure at standard air is:

    \[ P_{v,o} = \left( \frac{2{,}500}{4005} \right)^2 = (0.624)^2 = 0.39 \text{ in. wg} \]

    Now compare a smaller fan delivering the same 10,000 CFM through a 2.5 ft² outlet. Its outlet velocity is 4,000 FPM and its outlet velocity pressure is \( (4{,}000/4005)^2 = 1.00 \) in. wg, more than two and a half times as much. Outlet velocity matters on the job for three reasons:

    • Kinetic energy that may be wasted. A high outlet velocity carries energy that a short or abrupt duct connection throws away as turbulence instead of recovering as static pressure.
    • Noise and duct design. High velocity at the discharge raises noise and makes the first duct fitting after the fan more critical.
    • Fan class. The industry fan class limits, covered later in this chapter, are stated partly in terms of outlet velocity.

    A smaller, faster fan may meet the same airflow at a lower first cost, but it pays for it with a higher outlet velocity pressure.

    Fan Total Pressure and Fan Static Pressure¶

    A fan adds energy to air, and the pressure ratings measure how much. Two definitions are in common use, and a quotation that mixes them up can be off by an entire inch of water gauge.

    Fan total pressure (FTP) is the rise in total pressure across the fan: the total pressure at the fan outlet minus the total pressure at the fan inlet. Recall from Chapter 2 that total pressure is the sum of static pressure and velocity pressure at a point. Fan total pressure therefore counts all of the energy the fan adds to the air, both the "push" (static) part and the "motion" (velocity) part.

    Fan static pressure (FSP) is fan total pressure minus the fan outlet velocity pressure. In other words, it is the energy the fan adds except the kinetic energy of the air leaving the outlet. This is the AMCA definition used in North American fan catalogs, and it is the pressure most performance tables are organized around, because system designers usually calculate duct losses as static pressure.

    Fan Total and Fan Static Pressure¶

    \[ P_{tf} = P_{t,o} - P_{t,i} \]
    \[ P_{sf} = P_{tf} - P_{v,o} \]

    where:

    • \( P_{tf} \) is the fan total pressure in in. wg
    • \( P_{t,o} \) and \( P_{t,i} \) are the total pressures at the fan outlet and the fan inlet, in in. wg
    • \( P_{sf} \) is the fan static pressure in in. wg
    • \( P_{v,o} \) is the outlet velocity pressure in in. wg

    A common shortcut is to read "static pressure" on a job as "the reading on a gauge across the fan." That is not the same as fan static pressure: a gauge reading at the outlet includes whatever static pressure the inlet ductwork has already removed, and the definition above is built from total pressures, not gauge readings. Measuring installed airflow in the field is covered in Chapter 23.

    Worked Example: Converting Between the Two¶

    A fan delivers 10,000 CFM through the 4.0 ft² outlet from the previous example (outlet velocity pressure 0.39 in. wg). The system designer has calculated that the duct system needs 4.0 in. wg of fan static pressure. The fan total pressure is:

    \[ P_{tf} = P_{sf} + P_{v,o} = 4.0 + 0.39 = 4.39 \text{ in. wg} \]

    If the same job used the smaller fan with a 2.5 ft² outlet, the fan total pressure at the same 4.0 in. wg static pressure would be 5.0 in. wg. The two fans do the same useful static work, but the smaller one must add an extra 0.61 in. wg of velocity energy to do it. The comparison is summarized below.

    Fan Outlet area Outlet velocity Outlet velocity pressure Fan static pressure Fan total pressure
    Larger fan 4.0 ft² 2,500 FPM 0.39 in. wg 4.0 in. wg 4.39 in. wg
    Smaller fan 2.5 ft² 4,000 FPM 1.00 in. wg 4.0 in. wg 5.00 in. wg

    Static or Total? Ask Before You Select

    A common trap is selecting a fan at the specification's pressure without checking whether the engineer meant static or total. On high-velocity fans the difference can be a full inch of water gauge, enough to land on the wrong fan size. Confirm the pressure type in writing, and if the spec gives total pressure, subtract the outlet velocity pressure before entering a static pressure table.

    Brake Horsepower and Fan Efficiency¶

    Brake horsepower (BHP) is the power actually delivered to the fan shaft to produce a given airflow and pressure. The name comes from the brake dynamometer once used to measure shaft power. BHP is always larger than the power that ends up in the air, because the impeller and housing lose some energy to friction, turbulence, recirculation and leakage. It does not include losses in the belts, sheaves or motor; those are added separately when the motor is selected.

    The power that ends up in the air is the air power from Chapter 2. In US units, air power in horsepower is the airflow times the pressure divided by the constant 6,356, which converts CFM × in. wg into horsepower:

    Air Power¶

    \[ P_{air} = \frac{Q \times P}{6356} \]

    where:

    • \( P_{air} \) is the air power in horsepower
    • \( Q \) is the airflow volume in CFM
    • \( P \) is the fan pressure in in. wg (static or total, see below)
    • 6,356 is the unit conversion constant for CFM, in. wg and horsepower

    Fan efficiency is the ratio of air power to brake horsepower. Because there are two fan pressures, there are two efficiencies. Static efficiency uses fan static pressure; total efficiency (sometimes called mechanical efficiency) uses fan total pressure. Total efficiency is always the larger of the two at a given point, because fan total pressure is always at least as large as fan static pressure. Efficiency changes along the fan curve: it is low near zero flow, rises to a peak somewhere in the middle of the curve, and falls again toward the right end. Rearranging the definition gives the equation application engineers use most often:

    Brake Horsepower from Efficiency¶

    \[ BHP = \frac{Q \times P_{sf}}{6356 \times \eta_s} = \frac{Q \times P_{tf}}{6356 \times \eta_t} \]

    where:

    • \( BHP \) is the brake horsepower
    • \( Q \) is the airflow volume in CFM
    • \( P_{sf} \) and \( P_{tf} \) are the fan static and fan total pressures in in. wg
    • \( \eta_s \) and \( \eta_t \) are the static and total efficiencies, as decimals

    Worked Example: Brake Horsepower and Both Efficiencies¶

    The larger fan from the previous section delivers 10,000 CFM at 4.0 in. wg fan static pressure (4.39 in. wg fan total pressure), and its static efficiency at that point is 70% (illustrative).

    Step 1, the static air power:

    \[ P_{air,s} = \frac{10{,}000 \times 4.0}{6356} = 6.29 \text{ hp} \]

    Step 2, the brake horsepower:

    \[ BHP = \frac{6.29}{0.70} = 8.99 \text{ hp} \]

    Step 3, the total efficiency at the same point, using the total air power:

    \[ \eta_t = \frac{10{,}000 \times 4.39}{6356 \times 8.99} = \frac{43{,}900}{57{,}140} = 0.768 \]

    So the fan is 70% statically efficient and about 77% totally efficient at this point, and it absorbs about 9.0 BHP. If the customer's specification had required a minimum total efficiency of 75%, this point would pass; if it had required a static efficiency of 75%, it would fail. The table below shows how quickly BHP climbs as efficiency drops at the same duty.

    Static efficiency BHP at 10,000 CFM and 4.0 in. wg FSP Likely motor (before drive losses)
    80% 7.87 10 hp
    70% 8.99 10 hp
    60% 10.49 15 hp
    50% 12.59 15 hp

    The last two rows explain why selecting a fan well away from its peak-efficiency region costs money twice: once in a larger motor and again in the electric bill for the life of the fan. The power and efficiency terms are summarized here:

    Term Pressure it uses What it tells you
    Air power Static or total The useful energy delivered to the air
    Brake horsepower Not applicable The power the fan shaft absorbs
    Static efficiency Fan static pressure Air power (static) ÷ BHP
    Total efficiency Fan total pressure Air power (total) ÷ BHP

    Tip Speed, Maximum Safe Speed and Fan Class¶

    The faster an impeller turns, the more pressure it can develop and the harder its material is pulled apart by centrifugal force. Three ideas govern that trade-off: tip speed, maximum safe speed and fan class.

    Tip Speed¶

    Tip speed is the linear speed of the outermost point of the impeller blades, in feet per minute. It depends on both the impeller diameter and the rotating speed:

    Tip Speed¶

    \[ TS = \frac{\pi \times D \times N}{12} \]

    where:

    • \( TS \) is the tip speed in FPM
    • \( D \) is the impeller diameter in inches
    • \( N \) is the fan speed in RPM
    • 12 converts inches to feet

    Tip speed matters because, for a given impeller design, the pressure a fan develops rises roughly with the square of tip speed, and so does the centrifugal stress in the blades and back plate. Two fans of very different sizes that run at the same tip speed load their impellers similarly and develop similar pressures. This is why a large fan can make high pressure at low RPM, and a small fan has to spin fast to make the same pressure.

    Worked Example: Same Tip Speed, Different Fans¶

    An illustrative 30-inch impeller turns at 1,780 RPM:

    \[ TS = \frac{\pi \times 30 \times 1{,}780}{12} = 13{,}980 \text{ FPM} \]

    What speed would an illustrative 40-inch impeller of the same design need for the same tip speed? Solve the equation for speed:

    \[ N = \frac{12 \times TS}{\pi \times D} = \frac{12 \times 13{,}980}{\pi \times 40} = 1{,}335 \text{ RPM} \]

    The larger impeller reaches the same tip speed, and roughly the same pressure, at three-quarters of the speed. That lower speed is one reason larger, slower fans are often quieter and longer-lived, and it is why speed alone is never enough to judge how hard a fan is working.

    Maximum Safe Speed¶

    Maximum safe speed is the highest RPM at which the manufacturer allows a particular fan size and construction to operate. It is set by the strength of the impeller and the other rotating parts, the shaft's critical speed, and the bearing limits. Because centrifugal stress rises with the square of tip speed, a modest increase in RPM above the limit can consume the impeller's safety margin quickly. Maximum safe speed is published in the catalog for each fan size and class, and for high-temperature service it is reduced, because steel loses strength as it gets hot. High-temperature construction is covered in Chapter 21.

    Worked Example: Checking a Selection Against Maximum Safe Speed¶

    A selection calls for 1,380 RPM. The catalog lists a maximum safe speed of 1,450 RPM for this fan at room temperature, and the job air is hot enough that the catalog's temperature derate factor is 0.93. All three numbers are illustrative.

    \[ N_{max,hot} = 1{,}450 \times 0.93 = 1{,}349 \text{ RPM} \]

    At room temperature the selection passes (1,380 is below 1,450). At the job temperature it fails (1,380 is above 1,349). The application engineer must choose a heavier class, a larger fan size that meets the duty at a lower speed, or a construction rated for the temperature. A selection that only works at room temperature is not a selection.

    Derate Before You Compare

    A common trap is comparing the selection speed to the room-temperature maximum safe speed on a hot job. The impeller is weaker at operating temperature, so apply the catalog's temperature derate first, then compare. If the derated limit is lower than the selection speed, move to a heavier class or a larger size rather than hoping the margin holds.

    Fan Class¶

    Fan class is an industry rating, defined by AMCA Standard 99-2408, that groups centrifugal fans by the pressure and outlet velocity range they are built to handle. Class I is the lightest construction; Class II, Class III and Class IV are progressively heavier. Each class has minimum performance limits stated as combinations of outlet velocity and static pressure that a fan of that class must be able to reach, and a manufacturer builds each class with the impeller, shaft, bearings and housing needed to reach those limits safely. In practice, the class decides the maximum safe speed: a Class II version of a fan size is allowed to run faster, and so make more pressure, than the Class I version of the same size.

    Twin City Fan uses the class terms in its product descriptions; for example, FanPedia notes that Arrangement 10 units of Class I and II fans in certain sizes are commonly referred to as Ventilating Sets (FanPedia p. 167). Catalog performance tables usually show class boundaries by shading, so the reader can see at a glance which class a duty point requires. Class shading is covered in Chapter 26.

    Two class systems appear in Twin City Fan catalogs, and they are not interchangeable. The Roman-numeral Classes I to IV above are the AMCA system; Catalog 300 (BC fans) lists maximum RPM for each of them. Industrial radial fans (RBO, RBR, RBA, RBW and RBP, Catalog 902) use Class 22, Class 32 and Class 45 instead. Catalog 902 states that AMCA has no standard class of operation for industrial fans, so these are Twin City Fan's own three categories, named for the static pressure each can approach: about 22, 32 and 45 in. wg. The idea is the same (a higher class is built heavier to run faster and make more pressure, so the class sets the maximum safe speed), but Class 22 is not Class II, and each catalog's own table is the only place to read what its class names allow. Do not confuse fan class with construction class, the material and spark-resistance designation in Chapter 20.

    Worked Example: Which Class Does the Duty Need?¶

    An illustrative fan size has these limits in its catalog:

    Class Maximum safe speed (illustrative)
    Class I 1,200 RPM
    Class II 1,550 RPM
    Class III 1,950 RPM

    The duty of 10,000 CFM at 4.0 in. wg requires 1,380 RPM on this size. Had the customer been offered the Class I fan to save money, it would have been a fan that could not be run at the speed the duty requires. The ordered checklist below is the habit to build:

    1. Find the selection speed in the catalog.
    2. Read the maximum safe speed for each class of that fan size.
    3. Apply any temperature derate.
    4. Choose the lightest class whose derated limit is at or above the selection speed.

    The Fan Curve¶

    A performance table lists individual points. A fan curve draws all of them for one fan at one speed and one density, so the reader can see the fan's whole behavior at once. The horizontal axis is airflow volume in CFM. The vertical axis carries two or more curves on the same airflow scale:

    • The pressure curve. Fan static pressure (or fan total pressure) against airflow. It is the curve most people mean by "the fan curve."
    • The brake horsepower curve. BHP against airflow, usually drawn on its own vertical scale.
    • The efficiency curve. Often added as a third curve, showing static or total efficiency against airflow.

    Reading the pressure curve from left to right tells a story. At the left end, with zero airflow, the fan is blocked off and develops some pressure but moves no air. As resistance is relaxed, airflow increases; for a backward inclined or airfoil fan the pressure first rises slightly to a peak and then falls steadily. At the right end, the fan is discharging into almost no resistance, so it moves its maximum airflow at nearly zero static pressure. Every point on the curve is a possible operating point for that fan at that speed. Which point actually happens depends on the system, which is the subject of the next section.

    A customer needs 10,000 CFM at 4.0 in. wg from this fan at this speed.     Now suppose the customer asks for 13,000 CFM at the same speed. Halfway between the 12,000 and 14,000 CFM rows, the pressure is about 2.6 in. wg and the BHP about 9.45. The fan can move 13,000 CFM, but only against 2.6 in. wg of resistance, not 4.0. To deliver 13,000 CFM at 4.0 in. wg, the fan must run faster (the fan laws in Chapter 25) or a larger size must be chosen. A fan curve answers "what happens at this speed" for every flow at once, which is why it is the most useful single picture in fan selection.

    One Speed, One Curve

    Think of a fan curve as a photograph of the fan at one speed and one air density. Change the speed or the density and you get a different photograph, not a different point on the same one. That is why every curve and every catalog table states both.

    The Stall Region¶

    To the left of the peak of the pressure curve, the fan enters the stall region. There, the airflow through the impeller is too low for the blade angles: the air separates from the blades instead of following them, pockets of air recirculate in and out of the impeller, and the pressure becomes unsteady. A fan operating in the stall region may pulse, surge, vibrate and make a characteristic rumbling noise, and its pressure and airflow wander instead of settling at one point. For a backward inclined or airfoil fan, the stall region begins at the airflow of peak pressure and extends to the left, toward zero flow. Forward curved fans have a dip in the middle of their pressure curve, and the region to the left of the right-hand pressure peak is unstable. Catalog performance tables usually stop short of the stall region so that a reader cannot pick a point inside it, which is one reason a table may have blank cells at low airflow and high pressure. Surge, the related system-level instability, is covered in Chapter 25.

    Worked Example: Is This Point Stable?¶

    Using the illustrative fan curve above, the peak pressure of 4.9 in. wg occurs at 4,000 CFM.

    • A duty of 10,000 CFM at 4.0 in. wg sits well to the right of 4,000 CFM, so it is stable.
    • A duty of 3,000 CFM at about 4.85 in. wg sits to the left of 4,000 CFM, so it is in the stall region, even though the fan can technically produce that pressure.

    A customer who wants 3,000 CFM at 4.85 in. wg should be offered a smaller fan, on which that duty falls to the right of the peak. Never select to the left of the peak, even when the numbers seem to fit; a fan in stall wears faster and will generate complaints.

    Overloading and Non-Overloading Power Curves¶

    The shape of the BHP curve decides how much risk the motor carries if the system turns out to have less resistance than designed. A non-overloading power curve is a BHP curve that rises to a maximum and then falls as airflow continues to increase. Backward inclined, backward curved and airfoil centrifugal fans have this shape. Because the BHP has a ceiling at a given speed, a motor sized for that maximum cannot be overloaded at that speed, no matter how far to the right the fan runs. In the illustrative table above, the maximum is 9.5 BHP at 12,000 CFM, so a 10 hp motor protects this fan at this speed even if the system resistance is overestimated.

    An overloading power curve is a BHP curve that keeps rising as airflow increases, all the way to the right end of the curve. Forward curved fans and radial bladed fans have this shape. A motor sized exactly for the design point will be overloaded if the fan runs at higher airflow than expected, for instance when the duct system has less resistance than the designer calculated, when an access door is left open, or when a downstream damper is removed. With an overloading curve, the motor must be sized for the highest airflow the fan could realistically see, or the system resistance must be confirmed. Impeller types and their characteristic curves are introduced in Chapter 8.

    System Resistance, the System Curve and the Operating Point¶

    System Resistance¶

    System resistance is the pressure needed to push a given airflow through everything connected to the fan: the ductwork, elbows, dampers, filters, coils, hoods, louvers and the process equipment. Every one of those items resists airflow by friction and turbulence, and nearly all of that resistance follows one rule: it rises with the square of the airflow. Double the airflow through a duct system and its resistance rises to four times its original value. The rule comes from the velocity pressure equation, since resistance in turbulent flow is proportional to velocity pressure, and velocity pressure is proportional to velocity squared.

    System Resistance at a New Airflow¶

    \[ P_2 = P_1 \left( \frac{Q_2}{Q_1} \right)^2 \]

    where:

    • \( P_1 \) is the known system resistance in in. wg at airflow \( Q_1 \) in CFM
    • \( P_2 \) is the system resistance in in. wg at the new airflow \( Q_2 \) in CFM

    Worked Example: Resistance at Other Airflows¶

    A duct system has been calculated to need 4.0 in. wg at 10,000 CFM. At 12,000 CFM it needs:

    \[ P_2 = 4.0 \times \left( \frac{12{,}000}{10{,}000} \right)^2 = 4.0 \times 1.44 = 5.76 \text{ in. wg} \]

    At 8,000 CFM it needs \( 4.0 \times 0.64 = 2.56 \) in. wg. A 20% increase in airflow costs a 44% increase in pressure, which is why "just a little more air" is rarely a small request.

    The System Curve¶

    A system curve is the graph of system resistance against airflow for one particular duct system, drawn on the same axes as the fan curve. Because resistance rises with airflow squared, the system curve is a parabola that starts at zero pressure at zero airflow and bends upward. Every system has its own curve; a system with more resistance has a steeper curve. The system curve does not depend on the fan at all, only on the ductwork and equipment, so a designer can draw it before the fan is chosen. It changes when the system changes: a damper that closes, a filter that loads with dust, or a coil that ices all make the curve steeper.

    Worked Example: Plotting a System Curve¶

    Using the system from the previous example (4.0 in. wg at 10,000 CFM), the points of its system curve are:

    Airflow (CFM) System resistance (in. wg)
    0 0.00
    4,000 0.64
    8,000 2.56
    10,000 4.00
    12,000 5.76
    14,000 7.84

    Each value is \( 4.0 \times (Q/10{,}000)^2 \). Notice that the system curve is very flat at low airflow and very steep at high airflow; that shape is the reason a fan curve crosses it at a well-defined point.

    The Operating Point¶

    The operating point is the single airflow and pressure at which a fan actually runs in a system. It is the intersection of the fan curve (for the fan's speed) and the system curve. At that point, the pressure the fan develops exactly equals the pressure the system needs at the same airflow, so the airflow settles there and stays. A fan cannot run anywhere else on its curve unless the system curve or the fan speed changes. The operating point is what the customer actually gets; the duty point on the specification is what they asked for. Duty point and the related vocabulary are developed further in Chapter 25.

    Worked Example: Finding the Operating Point¶

    Lay the system curve from the previous table over the illustrative fan curve. At 10,000 CFM the fan develops 4.0 in. wg and the system needs 4.0 in. wg, so the operating point is 10,000 CFM at 4.0 in. wg, and the fan absorbs 9.0 BHP.

    Now let the filters load with dust so the system needs 4.0 in. wg at only 8,000 CFM. The new system curve is steeper: at 8,000 CFM it needs 4.0 in. wg while the fan offers 4.5 in. wg, so the fan pushes more air; at 9,000 CFM the system needs 5.06 in. wg while the fan offers only about 4.25 in. wg. The curves cross between those flows, at about 8,390 CFM and 4.40 in. wg. The fan did not fail; the system moved the operating point to the left.

    Finally, suppose the designer overestimated resistance and the real system needs only 2.5 in. wg at 10,000 CFM. The curves now cross at about 11,600 CFM and 3.36 in. wg. The customer gets more air than designed, and the fan draws about 9.4 BHP. For this non-overloading fan the motor is still protected; for a forward curved fan with an overloading power curve, the extra airflow could trip the motor. The table summarizes the three cases.

    System Effect¶

    A catalog rating is measured with the fan connected to ideal test ductwork: long, straight, uniform runs that deliver a smooth, even airflow into the inlet and carry it away from the outlet without disturbance. Real installations seldom look like that. An elbow bolted directly to the fan inlet, a short or missing discharge duct, or an abrupt transition makes the fan perform worse than its rating. System effect is that loss of fan performance caused by the way the fan is connected to the system, expressed as an extra pressure the fan must overcome. It is not a loss in the ductwork itself; it exists only because the fan's inlet or outlet airflow is disturbed.

    System effect matters because neither the fan curve nor the designer's system curve contains it. If it is ignored, the actual operating point falls short of the design point, even though both the fan and the duct calculations are correct. FanPedia gives a practical example: inlet boxes are used when the installation does not allow a straight run of duct into the fan, and the inlet box "is designed to minimize the system effect of a 90 degree turn into the fan" (FanPedia p. 187). Values for system effect come from AMCA Publication 201, Fans and Systems, which tabulates the loss for common inlet and outlet connections. The detailed inlet and outlet cases and the system effect curve are covered in Chapter 25.

    Worked Example: Allowing for System Effect¶

    A designer calculates 4.0 in. wg of duct resistance at 10,000 CFM and selects the fan at exactly that point. The fan is installed with an elbow directly on its inlet, which carries a system effect of 0.5 in. wg at 10,000 CFM (illustrative). The fan must now overcome 4.5 in. wg to move 10,000 CFM, but the illustrative fan curve offers only 4.0 in. wg there. Because the system effect also rises with airflow squared, the effective system curve passes through 4.5 in. wg at 10,000 CFM, and it crosses the fan curve at about 9,560 CFM and 4.11 in. wg. The job is about 4% short of its air, and nothing in the duct calculation or the catalog explains why. A larger system effect, or a fan selected on a steeper part of its curve, makes the shortfall larger.

    The correct practice is to add the system effect to the design pressure before selection, so the fan is chosen at 10,000 CFM and 4.5 in. wg (a higher speed or a larger fan), or to change the connection so the system effect disappears, for instance by adding straight duct or an inlet box. The ordered steps are:

    1. Calculate the duct system resistance at the design airflow.
    2. Identify each non-ideal fan connection and look up its system effect at the design airflow.
    3. Add the system effects to the duct resistance.
    4. Select the fan at the design airflow and the sum.

    System Effect Is Not in the Fan Curve

    Watch out for field complaints that blame the fan for a shortfall caused by its connection. The catalog rating assumes ideal ductwork, so an elbow on the inlet costs pressure no table shows. Before you quote, ask what is bolted to the inlet and outlet, and add the system effect to the design pressure.

    Density Corrections¶

    Every catalog rating assumes standard air: 0.075 lb/ft³, which is dry air at 70°F at sea-level barometric pressure (29.92 in. Hg). Real jobs are hotter, colder, higher or wetter. A fan running at a fixed speed moves the same volume of air (the same CFM) whatever the density, because the impeller sweeps the same volume each revolution. But the pressure it develops and the power it absorbs are both proportional to density. Lighter air means less pressure and less horsepower; heavier air means more of both.

    The density correction factor is the ratio of the actual air density to standard density. It converts between catalog (standard-air) values and actual job values:

    Density Correction Factor¶

    \[ K_d = \frac{\rho_{actual}}{0.075} \]
    \[ P_{actual} = P_{std} \times K_d \qquad BHP_{actual} = BHP_{std} \times K_d \]

    where:

    • \( K_d \) is the density correction factor (dimensionless)
    • \( \rho_{actual} \) is the actual air density in lb/ft³
    • \( P \) is fan pressure in in. wg and \( BHP \) is brake horsepower, at standard or actual conditions as subscripted

    To select a fan from a standard-air catalog for a job at non-standard density, the procedure runs in the opposite direction: divide the actual required pressure by \( K_d \) to get the equivalent standard-air pressure, look up the fan at the actual CFM and that pressure, then multiply the catalog BHP by \( K_d \) to get the actual BHP. The two most common causes of non-standard density are temperature and altitude, and each has its own factor.

    Temperature Correction¶

    Air expands as it is heated, so hot air is less dense than standard air. A temperature correction is the density correction factor for a temperature other than 70°F. Since density is inversely proportional to absolute temperature, the factor uses absolute temperatures in degrees Rankine (°F + 460):

    Temperature Correction Factor¶

    \[ K_t = \frac{70 + 460}{T + 460} = \frac{530}{T + 460} \]

    where:

    • \( K_t \) is the temperature correction factor (dimensionless)
    • \( T \) is the actual air temperature at the fan inlet in °F

    The table lists the factor at several temperatures. All values come from the equation and are standard engineering values.

    Air temperature (°F) Temperature correction factor
    0 1.152
    70 1.000
    120 0.914
    200 0.803
    300 0.697
    400 0.616
    600 0.500
    800 0.421

    Worked Example: A Hot Exhaust Fan¶

    An oven exhaust fan must handle 8,000 CFM of 200°F air against 2.0 in. wg of actual static pressure at sea level. The temperature factor is \( 530/660 = 0.803 \). The equivalent standard-air pressure is \( 2.0 / 0.803 = 2.49 \) in. wg, so the fan is selected from the catalog at 8,000 CFM and 2.49 in. wg. If the catalog shows, illustratively, 4.6 BHP at that point, the fan absorbs \( 4.6 \times 0.803 = 3.7 \) BHP when running hot. When the same fan starts up cold, the air is denser and the BHP is higher, which is why hot fans need a cold-start motor check (cold start horsepower is covered in Chapter 25).

    Altitude Correction¶

    Barometric pressure falls with elevation, and air density falls with it. An altitude correction is the density correction factor for a site above sea level, equal to the ratio of the local standard barometric pressure to the sea-level value. It can be computed from the standard atmosphere:

    Altitude Correction Factor¶

    \[ K_a = \left( 1 - 6.875 \times 10^{-6} \times h \right)^{5.2559} \]

    where:

    • \( K_a \) is the altitude correction factor (dimensionless)
    • \( h \) is the site elevation in feet above sea level

    The table gives the factor at 1,000-foot steps. Combined temperature and altitude effects multiply: \( K_d = K_t \times K_a \).

    Elevation (ft) Altitude correction factor
    0 1.000
    1,000 0.964
    2,000 0.930
    3,000 0.896
    4,000 0.864
    5,000 0.832
    6,000 0.801
    7,000 0.772
    8,000 0.743

    Worked Example: A Hot Fan at Altitude¶

    A dryer exhaust fan in a plant at 5,000 ft must move 10,000 CFM of 200°F air against 3.0 in. wg actual static pressure.

    1. Temperature factor: \( K_t = 530/660 = 0.803 \).
    2. Altitude factor: \( K_a = 0.832 \) from the table.
    3. Combined factor: \( K_d = 0.803 \times 0.832 = 0.668 \).
    4. Equivalent standard-air pressure: \( 3.0 / 0.668 = 4.49 \) in. wg.
    5. Select the fan from the catalog at 10,000 CFM and 4.49 in. wg. Suppose the catalog shows 11.0 BHP at that point (illustrative).
    6. Actual BHP when running hot at altitude: \( 11.0 \times 0.668 = 7.35 \) BHP.

    A salesperson who skips steps 1 through 4 and selects at 3.0 in. wg will deliver a fan that falls about a third short on pressure at the job.

    Two Directions, One Factor

    If density corrections feel slippery, that's normal; most people mix up the dividing and multiplying direction at first. You already handle the factor itself, so anchor on one sentence: pressure goes into the catalog divided by the factor, and horsepower comes out of the catalog multiplied by it. Work the dryer example once more with your own numbers and it will stick.

    How Ratings Are Measured: the AMCA 210 Rating Standard¶

    Catalog ratings are only comparable if every manufacturer measures fans the same way. The AMCA 210 rating standard is ANSI/AMCA Standard 210 (also published as ASHRAE Standard 51), Laboratory Methods of Testing Fans for Certified Aerodynamic Performance Rating. It defines how a fan is mounted on a test chamber or test duct, where pressures are measured, how airflow is measured (with calibrated nozzles or a Pitot traverse), how speed and power are measured, and how the results are converted to standard air density and a reference speed. A fan is tested at many points from no flow to full open flow, so the test traces the whole fan curve, not one point.

    AMCA 210 also defines four installation types, the combinations of a free or ducted inlet with a free or ducted outlet, because the same fan performs differently in each. A certified rating states which installation type it applies to. Manufacturers who participate in the AMCA Certified Ratings Program may place the AMCA seal on the catalog; the program checks that published ratings match independent test results. Certified sound ratings are tested under companion standards (AMCA 300 for the test and AMCA 311 for certification). Installation types are covered in Chapter 25, and how to read the certified-ratings statement in a catalog in Chapter 26.

    Worked Example: Converting a Test Point to Standard Air¶

    In an illustrative AMCA 210 test, a fan running at the reference speed is measured at 9,800 CFM, 3.85 in. wg fan static pressure and 8.6 BHP, on a day when the test air density is 0.073 lb/ft³. To publish the point at standard air, the lab applies the density correction from the previous section in the reverse direction:

    \[ K_d = \frac{0.075}{0.073} = 1.027 \]
    Quantity Measured at 0.073 lb/ft³ Published at 0.075 lb/ft³ Rule
    Airflow 9,800 CFM 9,800 CFM Unchanged at fixed speed
    Fan static pressure 3.85 in. wg 3.96 in. wg Multiply by 1.027
    BHP 8.6 8.84 Multiply by 1.027

    The published table is therefore a set of standard-air values that no customer's job may exactly match. That is the reason the density correction, system effect and installation type all exist: they translate a laboratory rating back into a real installation.

    Check the Seal and the Footnote

    What does the current catalog say? Before quoting a rating, read the certified-ratings footnote under the table: it names the installation type and whether the BHP includes drive losses. Thirty seconds there prevents most "the fan doesn't match the catalog" calls.

    Fan Sound¶

    Every fan makes noise, and many specifications limit it. Fan sound is the airborne noise a fan generates, produced mainly by the impeller blades passing the housing cutoff, by turbulence in the airflow, and to a lesser degree by bearings, belts and motors. Fan sound is described with a small set of terms, and two of them, sound power and sound pressure, are easily confused.

    Decibel¶

    The ear responds to an enormous range of acoustic energy, from a whisper to a jet engine, so sound is measured on a logarithmic scale. The decibel (dB) is a unit equal to ten times the base-10 logarithm of the ratio of a quantity to a fixed reference quantity. Because it is a ratio, a decibel value always implies a reference. A 3 dB increase means the acoustic energy has doubled, and a 10 dB increase means it has risen tenfold. Decibels do not add arithmetically: two identical fans, each 90 dB, together produce 93 dB, not 180 dB.

    Sound Power Level¶

    Sound power level (Lw) is the total acoustic power a fan radiates, expressed in decibels relative to a reference of 10⁻¹² watts. It is a property of the fan at its operating point, like BHP, and it does not depend on the room or the distance from the fan. That is why fan manufacturers publish sound power, usually in eight octave bands (octave bands are covered in Chapter 25).

    Sound Power Level¶

    \[ L_w = 10 \log_{10} \left( \frac{W}{10^{-12}} \right) \]

    where:

    • \( L_w \) is the sound power level in dB
    • \( W \) is the acoustic power in watts
    • \( 10^{-12} \) W is the reference power

    Sound pressure level, by contrast, is what a microphone measures at one location; it depends on distance, room size and reflections, and it is developed in Chapter 25. A specification that asks for "85 dB" without saying whether it means sound power or sound pressure, and at what distance, cannot be checked.

    Worked Example: Adding Fans¶

    A plant installs two identical exhaust fans, each rated at a sound power level of 90 dB in one octave band. The combined level is:

    \[ L_{w,total} = 10 \log_{10} \left( 10^{90/10} + 10^{90/10} \right) = 90 + 10 \log_{10} 2 = 93 \text{ dB} \]

    Ten identical fans would produce \( 90 + 10 \log_{10} 10 = 100 \) dB. When one fan is 10 dB louder than the other, the quieter fan adds only about 0.4 dB to the total, so treating the loudest source first is almost always the right noise-control priority. The rules of thumb are:

    Change Effect on combined level
    Two equal sources +3 dB
    Ten equal sources +10 dB
    Add a source 10 dB quieter About +0.4 dB

    Sone Rating¶

    A sone rating is a measure of perceived loudness on a linear scale, where 1 sone is defined as the loudness of a 1,000 Hz tone at a sound pressure level of 40 dB, and a sound of 2 sones is judged twice as loud as one of 1 sone. Because the scale is linear, sones are easier for non-specialists to compare than decibels: a 10-sone fan sounds about twice as loud as a 5-sone fan. Sone ratings are common on commercial products such as roof and wall exhausters, ceiling and cabinet ventilators, and are calculated from measured sound data under AMCA procedures for a stated listening distance.

    Blade Pass Frequency¶

    The blade pass frequency (BPF) is the rate at which impeller blades pass a fixed point such as the housing cutoff, equal to the fan speed in revolutions per second times the number of blades. It often appears as a distinct tone in a fan's sound spectrum. For example, a 10-blade impeller at 1,770 RPM has a blade pass frequency of \( 1{,}770 \times 10 / 60 = 295 \) Hz. Knowing it helps an engineer recognize whether a tonal complaint comes from the fan or from something else in the system.

    Blade Pass Frequency¶

    \[ BPF = \frac{N \times B}{60} \]

    where:

    • \( BPF \) is the blade pass frequency in hertz (Hz)
    • \( N \) is the fan speed in RPM
    • \( B \) is the number of impeller blades

    You Can Read a Fan Curve

    You just learned to find a fan's operating point from its fan curve and a system curve, and to explain why it moves when filters load or a damper closes. Add density correction and system effect, and you can turn a catalog rating into what the fan will really do on the job.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • A performance rating is five numbers, not one: airflow volume, pressure, speed, brake horsepower and the density they assume. A CFM without a pressure cannot be checked or ordered.
    • Fan static pressure equals fan total pressure minus outlet velocity pressure. Outlet velocity pressure comes from outlet velocity, which is airflow divided by fan outlet area; confirm which pressure a specification means.
    • BHP equals air power divided by efficiency: \( BHP = Q \times P / (6356 \times \eta) \), using static pressure with static efficiency or total pressure with total efficiency.
    • Tip speed (\( \pi D N / 12 \)) governs pressure and impeller stress. Every selection speed must be below the maximum safe speed for the fan's AMCA class, after any temperature derate.
    • A fan curve shows every possible operating point at one speed and density. Never select left of the pressure peak, in the stall region.
    • Non-overloading BHP curves (backward inclined, airfoil) have a ceiling; overloading curves (forward curved, radial) do not, so their motors must be sized for the highest airflow the fan may see.
    • System resistance rises with airflow squared, and the system curve is a parabola through the design point. The operating point is where the fan curve and system curve cross, and it moves when either one changes.
    • System effect is lost performance caused by poor fan connections. It is not in the fan curve; add it to the design pressure before selecting.
    • Correct for density: divide actual pressure by \( K_d = K_t \times K_a \) to enter a standard-air catalog, and multiply the catalog BHP by \( K_d \) to get actual BHP. CFM does not change.
    • AMCA 210 defines how fans are tested and rated, so certified catalog ratings from different manufacturers can be compared.
    • Fan sound is published as sound power level in decibels; sones express loudness on a linear scale, and the blade pass frequency (\( N \times B / 60 \)) identifies the fan's characteristic tone.
