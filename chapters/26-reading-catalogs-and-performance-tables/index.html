    Reading Catalogs and Performance Tables¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter teaches how to read a current Twin City Fan catalog. It covers catalog structure, fan size and model codes, performance tables and their headers, class shading, interpolation, rating conditions and AMCA certified ratings, transmission losses and appurtenance effects, actual operating conditions, catalog operating limits, first critical speed, balance grade, material specification tables, and the fan selector software. It explains why current catalogs take precedence over FanPedia where they disagree, and introduces fan family and motor selection. After completing it, readers can find a fan's speed and brake horsepower for a duty point in a catalog.

    Why the Catalog Is the Last Word¶

    Every earlier chapter described fans in general terms: what an arrangement is, why a class limits speed, how density changes pressure. A customer does not order a general fan. They order a specific model, in a specific size and class, at a speed and horsepower that someone read out of a Twin City Fan catalog or the Fan Selector software. This chapter teaches you to do that reading accurately, and to know which numbers the catalog gives you, which it leaves to you, and which it deliberately leaves out.

    From Duty Point to Order Line

    A customer gives you an airflow and a pressure; by the end of this chapter you will turn that into a fan size, a class, a speed and a brake horsepower you can defend line by line. That is the moment a conversation becomes an order. Let's follow the airflow, from the catalog cover to the last footnote under the performance table.

    The examples in this chapter use three current Twin City Fan publications posted on tcf.com: Catalog 300 for the BC backward inclined fan (dated April 2024), Catalog 902 for the RBA, RBO, RBR, RBW and RBP radial bladed industrial fans (dated November 2017), and the Engineering Resource Guide ERG-100 (dated May 2024). Numbers quoted from them are real catalog values and are labeled with their source. Numbers that are not from a catalog are labeled illustrative. Before you quote any of them to a customer, check the catalog on tcf.com for a newer edition; that habit is the first lesson of the chapter.

    The Product Catalog¶

    A product catalog is the manufacturer's published document for one product line: the models in the line, the sizes and classes built, the arrangements and options offered, the construction details, the engineering limits and, for many lines, the rated performance. Twin City Fan publishes one numbered catalog per line and lists them on the tcf.com Product Catalogs page. Catalog 300 covers the BC-SW and BC-DW backward inclined fans, Catalog 600 covers the BCV, BAV and FCV utility sets, Catalog 902 covers the radial bladed industrial fans, and so on through the axial, plenum, mixed flow and roof lines. Each catalog's cover carries its number and its edition date.

    A catalog answers three kinds of question, and it helps to know which kind you are asking before you start turning pages:

    • Can it be built? Which sizes, classes, arrangements, materials and temperatures are offered. These answers are yes or no, and a "no" ends the discussion.
    • How will it perform? Speed and brake horsepower for a stated airflow and pressure, either in printed performance tables or, for newer lines, in the Fan Selector software the catalog points to.
    • What will it look like on the job? Dimensions, weights, shaft and bearing sizes, and the paragraphs an engineer copies into a specification.

    The catalog is also a legal document of sorts. Its performance is certified under stated conditions, its dimension pages carry the warning "Dimensions not to be used for construction. Certified drawings available upon request," and its statements about what is standard and what is optional decide what a customer receives for the quoted price. Reading a catalog carelessly is how quotes go wrong.

    Worked Example: Answering a Customer Question from the Catalog¶

    A plant engineer asks whether a BC-SW fan in Arrangement 9 can exhaust air at 600°F. Here is how the question is answered from Catalog 300 alone.

    1. Find the product. The cover and the overview (pages 2–3) confirm that the BC-SW is the single width, single inlet backward inclined fan and that Arrangement 9 is offered for it.
    2. Find the limit. The high temperature section (page 5) contains Table 1, High Temperature Construction Requirements. It has three temperature bands: –20°F to +300°F, 300°F to 500°F, and 500°F to 800°F.
    3. Read the row. In the 500°F to 800°F row, the bearings are ball or roller with one expansion bearing, lubricated with high temperature grease, and the fan needs high temperature aluminum paint, a shaft cooler and a shaft seal, with a motor heat shield included for Arrangement 9 and 10 fans. The available arrangements are 1, 8, 9, 9F and 10, with the note that Arrangements 9 and 10 are limited to 600°F.
    4. Read the footnote that follows. The same page says that high temperature reduces impeller strength, so maximum safe speed must be derated, and directs you to the Fan Selector (FS10) software for the derated speeds.

    The answer is "yes, at exactly the 600°F limit for Arrangement 9, with high temperature construction, and only if the selection speed is below the derated maximum speed from Fan Selector." A good answer names the construction it requires and the check that is still open.

    Read the Edition Date First

    Before you quote anything from a catalog PDF saved on your laptop, compare its cover date with the copy on tcf.com. Thirty seconds spent checking the date saves you from quoting an arrangement, option or limit that the current edition has changed.

    Catalog Structure¶

    Catalog structure is the standard order of sections inside a Twin City Fan product catalog. The order is close to the same from line to line, so once you know it in one catalog you can find your way in any other. Catalog 300 runs in this sequence:

    1. Overview (pages 2–3): models, impeller diameters, airflow and pressure range, arrangements, standard and optional construction, certifications and typical applications.
    2. Construction features (page 4): how the impeller, housing, shaft and bearings are built, including balance grade, shaft critical speed and bearing life.
    3. High temperature modifications and energy regulations (page 5): Table 1 and the derating note, plus a statement on DOE and state efficiency rules.
    4. Arrangements (pages 6–9): a picture and paragraph for every arrangement offered.
    5. Options and accessories (pages 10–12): numbered callouts for guards, bases, dampers, inlet vanes, flanges, seals, doors and drives.
    6. Motor positions and standard configurations (page 13), inlet boxes (page 14) and vibration isolation (page 15).
    7. Engineering data (page 16): maximum RPM, impeller weight and WR² (the moment of inertia of the rotating impeller, in lb-ft², used to check that a motor can start it) for every size and class.
    8. Material specifications (page 17 onward): housing gauges, shaft diameters, bearing types and bare fan weights.
    9. Dimensions: one drawing and table per arrangement and size range.
    10. Typical specifications (pages 50–51): ready-to-copy specification paragraphs.

    Catalog 902 follows the same pattern but adds a section that Catalog 300 does not have: performance data, printed tables of RPM and brake horsepower for each model and size, starting on page 20. Catalog 300 instead says on its overview page that "certified performance data may be found in Twin City Fan & Blower's Fan Selector software." Whether performance is printed or lives in software is the single biggest structural difference between catalogs, and you should check which kind you are holding before you look for a table.

    Worked Example: Three Questions, Three Sections¶

    A representative is preparing a quote for a BC-SW and has three questions. Each one maps to a section by the kind of answer it needs:

    Question Kind of answer Section What the catalog says
    What is the highest speed a size 365 Class II can run? Engineering limit Engineering data, page 16 1,283 RPM
    What shaft diameter and bearing does a size 365 Class I use in Arrangement 1? Construction detail Material specifications, page 17 1-15/16 in. shaft, ball bearings
    Can the outlet damper modulate airflow over the whole range? Option choice Options and accessories, page 12 Opposed blade dampers are recommended for modulation over the entire range

    None of the three questions needed a performance table. Most of the questions a sales or application engineer fields in a day are "can it be built" and "what does it look like" questions, and they are answered in the front and back of the catalog, not in the middle.

    Technical Descriptions and the Typical Specification¶

    A catalog explains in words as well as in tables. A technical description is a short written paragraph that defines one component, arrangement or option and states its restrictions. FanPedia collects these in its Technical Descriptions section (pages 161–197), alphabetically from Anti-Backspin Devices onward, and the product catalogs carry similar paragraphs beside each arrangement and accessory. Technical descriptions often hold rules that never appear in a table. FanPedia's description of Arrangement 1 SWSI (page 162), for example, says the motor can go in any of the four AMCA motor positions W, X, Y or Z, and then lists the exceptions: on a bottom horizontal discharge (BHD), motor position Z is not allowed on a CW fan and position W is not allowed on a CCW fan, because the motor would block the outlet. A table of arrangements would simply show "W, X, Y, Z"; the description is where the exception lives.

    The typical specification at the back of each catalog is the same idea written for an engineer's project manual. Catalog 300's BC-SW paragraph requires, among other things, AMCA certified ratings for sound, air and fan energy index (FEI, the AMCA 208 efficiency metric), shafts sized for a first critical speed of at least 1.43 times maximum speed, and balance to AMCA 204 grade BV-3. When a specification on a bid looks familiar, it has often been copied from one of these paragraphs.

    FanPedia Versus the Current Catalog¶

    FanPedia (©2021) is Twin City Fan's encyclopedia of terms, arrangements, components and construction across all its product lines. It explains what things are. A product catalog states what one line offers today: its sizes, limits, options and ratings. FanPedia vs current catalog is the rule for when they disagree: the current catalog wins, because it is the document that describes what the factory builds and certifies now. FanPedia carries almost no size-specific numbers at all; it does not list maximum speeds, performance or dimensions, and it points to the product catalogs for details such as bearing life by model.

    "Current" means the latest edition on tcf.com, not "newer than FanPedia." Catalog 300 (April 2024) is newer than FanPedia, but Catalog 902 (November 2017) is older, and it is still the current catalog for the radial bladed line. Use FanPedia to understand a feature, then confirm the specific offering, limit or option in the current catalog for the model you are quoting, and use Fan Selector or the factory when the catalog is silent.

    Fan Size¶

    Fan size is the number Twin City Fan uses to identify one impeller diameter within a model. The industrial fans in Catalog 902 state that their sizes and impeller diameters conform to AMCA Standard 2402 (Sizes for Industrial Centrifugal Fans), and every performance page heads its table with the size number and the impeller diameter it stands for. Fan size is not the outlet dimension, the inlet diameter or the housing width; it is a label for the impeller, and every other dimension follows from it.

    Two numbering styles appear in the catalogs used in this chapter:

    • In Catalog 300, the BC size number tracks the impeller diameter in inches with the decimal point dropped. The BC-SW line runs from 12.25-inch to 98.25-inch impeller diameters, and its sizes run from 122 to 982.
    • In Catalog 902, the radial bladed sizes are a 900-series code. Size 905 is an 8.75-inch impeller, size 913 is 22.625 inches, and size 960 is 104.25 inches. The digits after the 9 are not the diameter; you read the diameter from the performance page.

    The table below gives a few real size-to-diameter pairs from Catalog 902's performance pages, so you can see that the size code climbs with diameter but is not equal to it:

    Size (Catalog 902) Impeller diameter (in.) Tip speed factor (FPM per RPM)
    905 8.75 2.29
    913 22.625 5.92
    921 36.5 not used in this chapter
    960 104.25 not used in this chapter

    Size also decides which classes and arrangements exist. In Catalog 902's RB Series Availability table (page 13), size 905 is offered only in Class 22, while sizes 913 through 919 are offered in Classes 22, 32 and 45. In Catalog 300, Arrangement 10 units of Class I and II in sizes 122 through 365 are the fans commonly called ventilating sets. So the first question about any size is not "how big is it?" but "what does this size let me build?"

    The header of each performance page turns the size into the numbers you need for calculations. For size 913 it reads: impeller diameter 22.625 in., tip speed (FPM) 5.92 × RPM, inlet area 0.87 ft², inlet diameter 13 in. O.D., outlet area 0.93 ft², outlet dimension 10-7/8 in. × 12-3/16 in.

    Tip Speed Factor¶

    \[ TS = k \times N, \qquad k = \frac{\pi \times D}{12} \]

    where:

    • \( TS \) is the impeller tip speed in FPM
    • \( k \) is the catalog's tip speed factor in FPM per RPM
    • \( N \) is the fan speed in RPM
    • \( D \) is the impeller diameter in inches

    Worked Example: What Size 913 Tells You¶

    Check the header against itself, then use it.

    1. Tip speed factor. \( k = \pi \times 22.625 / 12 = 5.92 \) FPM per RPM, which matches the printed "5.92 × RPM."
    2. Tip speed at a selection. The RBA 913 table gives 2,167 RPM for 4,092 CFM at 12 in. wg (Catalog 902, page 24). The tip speed is \( 5.92 \times 2{,}167 = 12{,}829 \) FPM.
    3. Outlet velocity. Outlet velocity is airflow divided by outlet area: \( 4{,}092 / 0.93 = 4{,}400 \) FPM. That is the number printed in the OV column on the same row, which confirms that the OV column is computed from the outlet area in the header.

    Three numbers from the header (diameter, tip speed factor, outlet area) let you check the table, compute tip speed for comparison with other sizes (Chapter 10), and size the duct transition at the outlet. A size number with no header beside it tells you very little.

    Size Is Not Interchangeable Across Models

    A common trap is assuming that "size 365" or "size 913" means the same impeller in every model. Size numbering belongs to a model's catalog, and the same digits can mean different diameters in different lines. Always read the diameter from that model's own performance page or engineering data before comparing two fans.

    Model Code¶

    A model code is the short letter designation that identifies a Twin City Fan product line and impeller type, such as BC-SW, RBA or TCTA. On its own it names a family; combined with a size, a class and an arrangement, it names one buildable fan. Twin City Fan's catalogs are titled by model code, so the code is also how you find the right catalog:

    Model code Catalog title (tcf.com) Catalog
    BC-SW, BC-DW Backward Inclined Fans (single width, double width) 300
    BAE Airfoil Fans 370
    RBA, RBO, RBR, RBW, RBP Industrial Fans (radial bladed) 902
    BCV, BAV, FCV Utility Sets 600
    EPF, EPFN, EPQ, EPQN E-Series Plenum Fans 470
    TCTA Tubeaxial Fans AX200
    TCVA Vaneaxial Fans AX100
    QSL, QSLSH, QSLR, QFE Mixed Flow Fans 1060

    Inside a line, the letters often separate impeller types that share a housing. Catalog 902 uses one housing family with five impellers: RBA (air handling, backward inclined blades welded to a back plate and front ring), RBO (open radial paddle), RBR (paddle with blade end rings for larger sizes and higher RPM), RBW (back plate wool impeller for stringy material) and RBP (paper handling, a modified RBW with trapezoidal gussets). The performance of each impeller differs, so each has its own table on the same size page: RBO 913 and RBA 913 share a header but not their numbers.

    A complete order line adds the rest of the designation. A typical written form is model, size, class and arrangement, for example "RBA 913, Class 32, Arrangement 9." Rotation and discharge (Chapter 13), motor position and construction options are then added for a full fan description, which Chapter 27 builds in full.

    Worked Example: Is This Designation Buildable?¶

    Catalog 902's RB Series Availability table (page 13) lists the arrangements offered for each impeller type, size and class. For RBA sizes 913 through 919 it reads:

    Class Arrangements offered
    Class 22 1, 4, 8, 10, 4VI, 9A, 9B, 9F
    Class 32 1, 4, 8, 9, 4VI, 9F
    Class 45 1, 8, 9, 9F

    Now check three designations a customer might send:

    1. RBA 913, Class 22, Arrangement 10. Arrangement 10 appears in the Class 22 list. Buildable.
    2. RBA 913, Class 32, Arrangement 10. Arrangement 10 is not in the Class 32 list. Not buildable; offer Arrangement 9 or 9F, which are the belt-drive arrangements listed for Class 32.
    3. RBA 913, Class 45, Arrangement 4. Arrangement 4 (impeller on the motor shaft) is not in the Class 45 list. Not buildable; a Class 45 RBA 913 must have its own shaft and bearings, as in Arrangements 1, 8, 9 or 9F.

    The second and third designations look reasonable and would pass a casual read. They fail only when you check the model, size and class together against the availability table, which is why a model code is never checked one field at a time.

    Where the Duty Point Comes From¶

    A performance table is entered with an airflow and a static pressure, the duty point. On many inquiries the customer supplies both. On others, especially ventilation and dust collection jobs, the airflow has to be estimated first. Twin City Fan's Engineering Resource Guide ERG-100 gives two quick methods that belong in every application engineer's toolkit.

    Air Change Method¶

    The air change method estimates the ventilation airflow for a space from its volume and a recommended time to replace all of its air once. ERG-100 recommends it for standard commercial applications where environmental control of hazards, heat or odors is not required, and it lists typical minutes per air change by area type, for example 3–6 minutes for a machine shop, 1–3 for a boiler room and 4–10 for a warehouse. Local code, how the space is used, and the climate also affect the choice.

    Airflow by the Air Change Method¶

    \[ Q = \frac{V_{room}}{t_{change}} \]

    where:

    • \( Q \) is the required airflow in CFM
    • \( V_{room} \) is the room volume in ft³
    • \( t_{change} \) is the chosen time per air change in minutes

    For an illustrative machine shop 100 ft × 60 ft × 20 ft high (120,000 ft³), choosing 4 minutes per change gives \( Q = 120{,}000 / 4 = 30{,}000 \) CFM. The method gives airflow only; the static pressure still comes from the duct, louver and filter losses.

    Minimum Conveying Velocity¶

    The minimum conveying velocity is the lowest average duct velocity that keeps a given material suspended in the airstream so it does not settle and clog the duct. ERG-100 reprints typical values from the ACGIH Industrial Ventilation manual: 2,500–3,000 FPM for very fine light dust such as cotton lint, 3,500–4,000 FPM for average industrial dust such as grinding dust, 4,000–4,500 FPM for heavy dusts such as metal turnings, and 4,500 FPM and up for heavy or moist material. Dividing the airflow by the conveying velocity gives the largest duct area that will still convey: 4,092 CFM at 4,000 FPM needs a duct no larger than about 1.02 ft² (roughly a 13.7-inch round duct). Conveying velocity also points toward the fan family, because a fan that the material passes through must survive it.

    Fan Family Selection¶

    Fan family selection is choosing the product line, and within it the impeller type, before choosing a size. It comes first because every later step happens inside one catalog. Chapter 6 described the families; here the point is that each catalog's overview tells you, in a few sentences, what the family is for:

    • Catalog 300 calls the BC "a popular choice for the efficient handling of large volumes of relatively clean air at low to moderate pressures," with BC-SW static pressure to 20 in. wg and airflow to 277,500 CFM.
    • Catalog 902 describes the radial bladed fans as "designed for applications with moderate air volumes of moderate to high pressures," built for "dirty, abrasive, sticky or bulky particulate-laden airstreams," with static pressure to 46 in. wg for RBA, RBO and RBR.
    • Within Catalog 902, the RBA is "the most efficient impeller in the RB family" but is "limited to handling relatively clean air or gases," the RBO and RBR suit "coarse, sticky, heavy and/or abrasive materials," the RBW keeps "long, stringy, fibrous" material from wrapping, and the RBP handles paper trim. The RBP is not AMCA licensed, and its Class 32 construction is limited to 22 in. wg.

    Performance Tables¶

    A performance table is a grid in a product catalog that lists, for one model and size at standard air, the fan speed (RPM) and brake horsepower (BHP) required to deliver each combination of airflow and static pressure. Rows are airflows; columns are static pressures; each cell holds an RPM and a BHP. A performance table is a fan curve (Chapter 10) laid out as numbers: each column is one pressure, and reading down the column follows the fan along a family of curves at increasing speed.

    The Performance Table Header¶

    The performance table header is the block above and around the grid that tells you what the grid means. On a Catalog 902 page it has four parts:

    • The size block: size number, impeller diameter, tip speed factor, inlet area and diameter, outlet area and outlet dimension, as in the Fan Size section.
    • The model line: the model and size (for example RBA 913) and its Fan Efficiency Grade (FEG), an AMCA 205 rating of the fan's peak efficiency; RBA 913 is FEG80 and RBO 913 is FEG67.
    • The column heads: CFM and OV (outlet velocity in FPM) for the rows, then one static pressure per column pair, each split into RPM and BHP.
    • The footer: maximum RPM for each class, followed by the rating notes that say what the numbers include and exclude (covered under Rating Conditions below), and the legend for shading, italics and underlining.

    Read the header and footer before the grid. The grid has no units printed in its cells, no class labels and no conditions; all of that is in the header and footer.

    Class Shading in Tables¶

    Class shading is the catalog's way of showing, inside the grid, which fan class each duty point requires. Because a class is defined by a maximum safe speed for the size (Chapter 10), the class boundary in a table is really a speed boundary. On the RBA 913 page the footer gives Class 22 = 2,812 RPM, Class 32 = 3,337 RPM and Class 45 = 4,000 RPM, and the legend says: regular face to the left of Class 32 = Class 22; regular face in light shaded area = Class 32; italic face to right of Class 32 = Class 45. Other catalogs use the same idea with their own legend, so read the legend on the page you are using.

    Class from Selection Speed¶

    \[ \text{Class} = \begin{cases} 22 & N \le 2{,}812 \\ 32 & 2{,}812 < N \le 3{,}337 \\ 45 & 3{,}337 < N \le 4{,}000 \end{cases} \]

    where:

    • \( N \) is the selection speed in RPM read from the RBA 913 table
    • the limits are the RBA 913 maximum RPM values for each class from Catalog 902, page 24

    Worked Example: Reading the Class from the Grid¶

    Take three cells from the 4,836 CFM row of the excerpt:

    • 20 in. SP: 2,740 RPM, regular face. \( 2{,}740 \le 2{,}812 \), so Class 22.
    • 28 in. SP: 3,152 RPM, shaded. \( 2{,}812 < 3{,}152 \le 3{,}337 \), so Class 32.
    • 32 in. SP: 3,340 RPM, italic. \( 3{,}337 < 3{,}340 \le 4{,}000 \), so Class 45.

    The last cell is the instructive one. At 4,464 CFM the same 32 in. column reads 3,321 RPM and is Class 32; one row lower it crosses the Class 32 limit by 3 RPM and becomes Class 45. Class boundaries do not follow straight columns or rows, which is why the catalog shades cell by cell. When a selection lands within a few RPM of a boundary, consider the heavier class or a different size, because any later correction for temperature, drive slip or system effect will push the speed up, not down.

    The Shading Is a Speed Map

    Notice that class shading is just the maximum safe speed for each class drawn onto the grid. Once you see it that way, you can check any cell yourself by comparing its RPM with the footer, and you can predict where the shading starts on a table you have never seen.

    Interpolation¶

    Duty points rarely land exactly on a printed row and column. Interpolation is estimating the RPM and BHP between printed values by assuming they change in a straight line between neighboring rows or columns. Catalog tables are spaced closely enough that straight-line interpolation is the accepted practice; ERG-100 works its density examples this way.

    Linear Interpolation¶

    \[ y = y_1 + \frac{x - x_1}{x_2 - x_1}\,(y_2 - y_1) \]

    where:

    • \( x \) is the required airflow (or static pressure)
    • \( x_1 \) and \( x_2 \) are the printed airflows (or pressures) on either side of \( x \)
    • \( y_1 \) and \( y_2 \) are the printed RPM (or BHP) values at \( x_1 \) and \( x_2 \)
    • \( y \) is the interpolated RPM (or BHP)

    Two Directions, One Step at a Time

    Double interpolation looks like a lot of arithmetic the first time, and most people lose track of which number goes where. Do it as three single interpolations: across the airflow rows in each of two pressure columns, then once between those two results. Write each intermediate RPM and BHP down before moving on.

    Worked Example: Single and Double Interpolation on RBA 913¶

    Single interpolation. Find RPM and BHP for 4,000 CFM at 12 in. SP. The airflow lies between the 3,720 and 4,092 CFM rows:

    \[ f = \frac{4{,}000 - 3{,}720}{4{,}092 - 3{,}720} = \frac{280}{372} = 0.753 \]
    \[ N = 2{,}128 + 0.753 \times (2{,}167 - 2{,}128) = 2{,}157 \text{ RPM} \]
    \[ BHP = 10.51 + 0.753 \times (11.72 - 10.51) = 11.42 \]

    Double interpolation. Now find 4,000 CFM at 14 in. SP. The pressure lies between the 12 and 16 in. columns, so first interpolate the 16 in. column at the same fraction \( f = 0.753 \):

    \[ N_{16} = 2{,}401 + 0.753 \times (2{,}433 - 2{,}401) = 2{,}425 \text{ RPM}, \quad BHP_{16} = 13.87 + 0.753 \times (15.30 - 13.87) = 14.95 \]

    Then interpolate between the two columns. 14 in. is halfway between 12 and 16, so the fraction is 0.5:

    \[ N = 2{,}157 + 0.5 \times (2{,}425 - 2{,}157) = 2{,}291 \text{ RPM}, \quad BHP = 11.42 + 0.5 \times (14.95 - 11.42) = 13.18 \]

    The result, 2,291 RPM and 13.18 BHP, is Class 22 (\( 2{,}291 \le 2{,}812 \)). Because the pressure columns are 4 in. wg apart at this point in the table, a straight line between them is an approximation; for a quotation, confirm the final speed in Fan Selector, which computes the point directly.

    Fan Selection Range¶

    A size can deliver a duty point and still be the wrong size. The fan selection range is the part of a fan's performance table, or curve, where it runs stably and near its best efficiency: to the right of the stall region (Chapter 10) and not so far right that efficiency falls away. Catalog 902's overview puts it directly: "it is important that the fan size is selected so that it will operate at or near its maximum efficiency point." Its tables mark the maximum static efficiency by underlining those figures. Catalog 300's typical specification says the BC's horsepower curve "shall reach a peak in the normal selection area," which is the same idea expressed through the power curve.

    Static efficiency for any cell can be computed from the table with the equation from Chapter 10:

    Static Efficiency from a Table Cell¶

    \[ \eta_s = \frac{Q \times P_s}{6356 \times BHP} \]

    where:

    • \( \eta_s \) is the fan static efficiency (as a decimal)
    • \( Q \) is the airflow in CFM
    • \( P_s \) is the fan static pressure in in. wg
    • \( BHP \) is the brake horsepower from the table
    • 6,356 converts CFM × in. wg to horsepower

    Worked Example: Same Airflow, Three Pressures¶

    All three cells are on the 4,836 CFM row of RBA 913:

    SP (in. wg) RPM / BHP Static efficiency Position
    28 3152 / 31.23 \( 4{,}836 \times 28 / (6356 \times 31.23) = 0.68 \) Underlined: maximum static efficiency
    8 1967 / 10.41 \( 4{,}836 \times 8 / (6356 \times 10.41) = 0.58 \) Right of peak
    4 1612 / 6.38 \( 4{,}836 \times 4 / (6356 \times 6.38) = 0.48 \) Far right of peak

    At 28 in. wg the RBA 913 is working at its best. At 4 in. wg it delivers the airflow, but at less than half the static efficiency, because a fan this size is built for higher pressure than 4 in. wg needs. A low-pressure duty at this airflow belongs on a different size or a different family, not on the RBA 913, and comparing several sizes at the same duty point, in the tables or in Fan Selector, is how you find it.

    Check the Neighbors

    Before accepting a size, look one size smaller and one size larger at the same duty point. If a neighbor needs noticeably less BHP, your first choice is outside its selection range.

    Rating Conditions¶

    Every number in a performance table is true only under the conditions the catalog states. Rating conditions are those stated conditions: the air density, the installation type, and what the BHP does and does not include. The Catalog 902 footer states all of them in three lines:

    • "Performance certified is for installation type B & D: Free or ducted inlet, ducted outlet."
    • "Power ratings (BHP) do not include transmission losses."
    • "Performance ratings do not include the effects of appurtenances (accessories)."

    The density is stated elsewhere: ERG-100 says that in each fan catalog the performance tables are based on standard air density, dry air at 70°F at sea level (29.92 in. Hg), equal to 0.075 lb/ft³. The installation types are the four AMCA test setups introduced in Chapter 10: A (free inlet, free outlet), B (free inlet, ducted outlet), C (ducted inlet, free outlet) and D (ducted inlet, ducted outlet). A rating certified for B and D therefore assumes there is a duct on the fan outlet.

    Worked Example: When the Job Is Not the Rating¶

    An RBA 913 will move 4,464 CFM at 16 in. wg on a job where it discharges directly into a plenum room with no outlet duct.

    1. Read the table: 2,470 RPM, 16.85 BHP, Class 22.
    2. Compare the installation: no outlet duct means installation type A or C. The rating is certified for B and D, so the table does not describe this installation.
    3. Size the energy at stake: the outlet velocity on this row is 4,800 FPM, so the outlet velocity pressure at standard air is \( (4{,}800 / 4{,}005)^2 = 1.44 \) in. wg. That is the kinetic energy leaving the fan; with a ducted outlet some of it is recovered as static pressure, and with a free outlet it is not, so the fan will not deliver the tabulated static pressure at the tabulated speed.
    4. Act: confirm the selection for the actual installation with Fan Selector or the factory, or add an outlet duct, and account for the outlet condition as a system effect (Chapter 25).

    The table value was correct and the selection was still wrong, because the job broke a rating condition. Read the conditions every time.

    AMCA Certified Ratings¶

    AMCA certified ratings are published performance figures that the Air Movement and Control Association has licensed the manufacturer to mark with the AMCA seal, after testing in an AMCA-accredited laboratory and check tests under the AMCA Certified Ratings Program (AMCA 211 for air, AMCA 311 for sound). The seal applies only to the models, the catalog and the performance types the certification statement names, so the statement itself is the thing to read.

    The two catalogs used here word it differently:

    • Catalog 300: "Twin City Fan & Blower certifies that the Model BC-SW fans shown herein are licensed to bear the AMCA Seal. Certified performance data may be found in Twin City Fan & Blower's Fan Selector software." The overview lists certifications as AMCA Sound/Air, AMCA Air, FEI and UL 705.
    • Catalog 902: the RBO, RBR, RBA and RBW are licensed to bear the AMCA Seal, and "the ratings shown are based on tests and procedures performed in accordance with AMCA Publication 211." The overview lists certifications as AMCA Air and FEG. The RBP "is not AMCA licensed."

    RBA AMCA Air (and FEG) listed; no sound certification listed No, for the sound part; raise it with the engineer before bidding
    RBP Not AMCA licensed No

    The RBA might be the better fan for the airstream, and it may still be the right recommendation. The point is to find the gap from the certification statement before the bid, not from a submittal rejection after it.

    Transmission Losses¶

    Transmission losses are the power lost between the motor shaft and the fan shaft in the drive: belt flexing and slip, and bearing friction in the drive components. Catalog BHP is the power at the fan shaft, and the Catalog 902 footer says plainly that it does not include them. On a belt-driven fan the motor must supply the fan BHP plus the drive loss. On an Arrangement 4 or 8 fan there are no belts, and coupling losses are small enough to be ignored in a catalog selection.

    AMCA Publication 203 gives estimated belt drive losses as a percentage of motor power, larger for small drives and smaller for large ones. The 4 percent used below is illustrative.

    Motor Power Including Drive Loss¶

    \[ HP_{motor} = \frac{BHP}{1 - L_d} \]

    where:

    • \( HP_{motor} \) is the power the motor must deliver, in horsepower
    • \( BHP \) is the fan brake horsepower from the performance table
    • \( L_d \) is the drive loss as a decimal fraction of motor power

    Worked Example: Adding the Drive Loss¶

    The RBA 913 at 4,092 CFM and 12 in. wg needs 11.72 BHP at the fan shaft. On a belt-driven Arrangement 9 with an illustrative 4 percent drive loss:

    \[ HP_{motor} = \frac{11.72}{1 - 0.04} = 12.21 \text{ hp} \]

    The difference is about half a horsepower. It decides nothing here, but it is the half horsepower that pushes a selection over a motor size when the fan BHP sits just under a standard rating, which is exactly the case where people forget it.

    Appurtenance Effects¶

    Appurtenance effects are the changes in fan performance caused by accessories in or at the airstream, such as inlet boxes, variable inlet vanes, outlet dampers, inlet and outlet screens, and guards over an open inlet. Catalog ratings are for the bare fan and exclude them. Each accessory adds a pressure loss or disturbs the flow into or out of the impeller, so the fan must run faster and draw more power to deliver the same duty. The catalogs describe accessories in terms of this effect: Catalog 300 calls its inlet boxes "designed to minimize pressure drop," and its typical specification uses cantilevered inlet vane blades through size 660 "to minimize air performance insertion losses and noise." Account for appurtenances with the manufacturer's data in Fan Selector or from the factory, and treat their flow disturbance as a system effect (Chapter 25).

    Actual Operating Conditions¶

    Actual operating conditions are the temperature, altitude and density at which the fan will really run, as opposed to the standard air of the table. Because tables are at standard air, actual conditions must be converted before entering the table, and the results converted back. ERG-100 works this example: 17,000 CFM at 2.5 in. wg, 300°F and 3,000 ft altitude.

    1. The ERG-100 correction factor for 300°F at 3,000 ft is 0.624.
    2. Standard-air static pressure: \( 2.5 / 0.624 = 4.0 \) in. wg. Enter the table at 17,000 CFM and 4.0 in. wg. Airflow is not corrected.
    3. The table (by interpolation) gives 918 RPM and 14.36 BHP at standard air.
    4. BHP at operating conditions: \( 14.36 \times 0.624 = 8.96 \) BHP.
    5. Cold start BHP at the site (70°F, 3,000 ft, factor 0.896): \( 14.36 \times 0.896 = 12.87 \) BHP.

    The fan runs at 918 RPM in every case. The motor sees 8.96 BHP when the air is hot but 12.87 BHP when the fan starts in cold air, and that difference is the subject of motor selection below.

    Limits and Mechanical Data¶

    Catalog Operating Limits¶

    Catalog operating limits are the published boundaries a selection must stay inside: maximum RPM by size and class, temperature limits by construction and arrangement, and limits stated as notes. Examples from the two catalogs:

    • Maximum RPM: BC-SW size 365 runs to 995 RPM in Class I and 1,283 RPM in Class II (Catalog 300, page 16).
    • Speed notes: "Operating speeds above 4000 RPM are not recommended. Consult factory" (Catalog 300); RBO 905 has a Class 22 maximum of 5,296 RPM but "selections above 4000 RPM not recommended" (Catalog 902).
    • Temperature: standard BC construction from –20°F to +300°F, but Arrangements 3 and 3F only to 130°F and Arrangement 4 to 180°F; standard RB Arrangement 4 fans to 200°F.
    • Derating: maximum safe speed must be reduced at high temperature; Catalog 300 directs you to Fan Selector for the derated values.

    A selection that violates any one of these is not a selection, however good its efficiency.

    First Critical Speed¶

    The first critical speed is the lowest rotating speed at which the shaft and impeller assembly's natural bending frequency matches the speed of rotation, so that small unbalance forces are amplified into severe vibration. Fans are designed to run well below it. Both catalogs state the margin: shafts "are sized for a first critical speed of at least 1.43 times the maximum speed for the class." For a BC-SW size 365 Class II, the shaft's first critical speed is therefore at least \( 1.43 \times 1{,}283 = 1{,}835 \) RPM. That margin is calculated at the class maximum, which is one more reason a fan must never be sped up past its class limit in the field.

    AMCA 204 Balance Grade¶

    The AMCA 204 balance grade is the balance quality and vibration category a fan is built to under ANSI/AMCA Standard 204, Balance Quality and Vibration Levels for Fans. Each fan application category (BV-1 through BV-5) sets a balance quality grade for the impeller and vibration limits for the assembled fan. Catalog 300 states that BC impellers are statically and dynamically balanced to grade BV-3, which "corresponds to a Balance Quality Grade G6.3," that assembled fans are test run at the rated speed or maximum RPM with vibration recorded in three directions on both bearings, and that trim balancing is performed if needed to stay within BV-3 limits. When a specification asks for a stricter category, it is asking for something beyond the catalog standard and must be quoted as such.

    Material Specifications Table¶

    The material specifications table lists, for each size and class, the physical makeup of the fan: housing sheet gauges for the sides and scroll, shaft diameter and bearing type by arrangement, and bare fan weight by arrangement. Its columns use terms from Chapters 16 and 17, and its footnotes carry the bearing type codes. In Catalog 300 the bearing codes are B (ball bearing), R (unit roller bearings) and SR (spherical roller bearings with split pillow block housings); gauge numbers follow sheet steel practice, where a smaller number is thicker steel.

    Worked Example: Reading One Row¶

    Catalog 300, BC SWSI Class I, size 365 (page 17):

    Column Value What it tells you
    Housing sides 10 10 gauge steel sides
    Housing scroll 12 12 gauge steel scroll, lighter than the sides
    Arr. 1 and 9 shaft and bearing 1-15/16 in., B Ball bearings on a 1-15/16 in. shaft
    Arr. 3 shaft and bearing 1-15/16 in., B Same shaft and bearing in Arrangement 3
    Bare fan weight, Arr. 1 / 3 / 9 1,009 / 948 / 1,059 lb Weight without motor and drive, for rigging and isolator selection

    Two uses follow directly. The bare fan weight, plus motor and drive weight, sets the load for vibration isolators (Chapter 22). The bearing type tells the maintenance department which bearings to stock. Moving up the same table, size 490 switches from ball to unit roller bearings in Arrangement 3 (R) while Arrangements 1 and 9 stay on ball bearings, a difference a reader only finds by reading the row.

    Product Options¶

    Product options are the construction choices and accessories a catalog offers beyond the standard fan: optional materials and construction (aluminum, stainless steel, high temperature, spark resistant), and accessories such as belt guards, shaft and bearing guards, vibration isolation and inertia bases, drains, access doors, flanged inlets and outlets, companion flanges, shaft seals, inlet boxes, coupling guards, inlet and outlet screens, outlet dampers, variable inlet vanes and drives. The catalog states what each one does and when to use it, and those statements are the basis of a recommendation.

    Worked Example: Choosing a Capacity Control¶

    A BC-SW on clean supply air must reduce airflow, and the customer expects to run at about 60 percent of full volume for most of the year. Catalog 300 describes three options:

    1. Outlet dampers add resistance and move the operating point left. They are the least expensive option, and the horsepower savings are "usually much less than other methods." Parallel blade dampers suit modulation from full open to about 75 percent open; opposed blade dampers cost about 10 percent more and suit modulation over the entire range.
    2. Variable inlet vanes pre-spin the air in the direction of rotation, giving a family of reduced curves. They cost about 50 to 80 percent more than outlet dampers but offer significant energy savings.
    3. A variable frequency drive changes fan speed and "can provide the greatest potential for energy savings"; the catalog says it should be considered for extended operation at part load, "especially below 70% of the full volume operation."

    Sixty percent of full volume for most of the year is below the 70 percent the catalog names, so the VFD is the recommendation. If the owner cannot accept a VFD, the next choice is variable inlet vanes; an outlet damper would have to be opposed blade to modulate that far. Any damper or vane is an appurtenance, so its effect must be included in the selection.

    Motor Selection¶

    Motor selection is choosing the motor horsepower, enclosure and type for a fan from the catalog BHP and the job conditions. The catalog gives the fan BHP at standard air; the motor must also cover drive losses, the cold start of a hot fan, and the service conditions covered in Chapters 14 and 15 (enclosure, efficiency class, inverter duty, hazardous location). Catalog 300 also sets drive rules that follow from the motor: drives and belts rated for 150 percent of the required motor horsepower, and variable pitch motor sheaves on 10 HP and smaller, fixed pitch on 15 HP and larger.

    Worked Example: Two Motors from the Catalog¶

    Room-temperature duty. The RBA 913 at 4,092 CFM and 12 in. wg needs 12.21 hp at the motor after the illustrative 4 percent drive loss. The next standard NEMA motor rating at or above 12.21 hp is 15 HP. A 15 HP TEFC motor is a sound choice for a dust collector exhauster; the motor is not running into its service factor, and Catalog 300's drive rule would call for a fixed pitch sheave at 15 HP.

    Hot duty. For the ERG-100 fan at 300°F and 3,000 ft, the hot BHP is 8.96, but the fan starts in 70°F air at 3,000 ft and draws 12.87 BHP until the air heats up. With the same illustrative 4 percent drive loss the cold requirement is \( 12.87 / 0.96 = 13.41 \) hp, so the motor is 15 HP, not the 10 HP that the hot BHP alone would suggest. If the fan could be started with dampers closed or on a VFD, a smaller motor might be justified, but that is a deliberate design choice covered in Chapter 27, not a default.

    Size the Motor for the Cold Start

    A common trap on hot jobs is sizing the motor from the operating BHP, because that is the number at the design condition. Cold air is denser, so the same fan at the same speed draws more power at start-up and until the system warms. Always take the cold BHP at the site altitude, add the drive loss, and size the motor from that.

    Fan Selector Software¶

    Fan Selector software is Twin City Fan's selection program, downloaded from tcf.com, that computes certified performance for a duty point and supplies the performance, drawings and accessory data the printed catalog leaves out, including maximum safe speeds derated for elevated temperature. Catalog 300 states that BC certified performance data "may be found in Twin City Fan & Blower's Fan Selector software," and Catalog 902 says that "for complete product performance, drawings and available accessories," you should download the Fan Selector program. Fan Selector does what tables cannot: it computes the exact point instead of interpolating, and it holds certified data for lines, such as the BC, whose catalogs print no tables at all.

    The catalog still matters when you use the software, because the software answers "what does it take?" and the catalog answers "is that allowed, and what does it include?"

    Worked Example: Checking a Software Selection Against the Catalog¶

    Fan Selector returns a BC-SW size 365 at 1,150 RPM for a customer's duty at standard air (the selection speed is illustrative). Check it against Catalog 300:

    1. Class. Engineering data (page 16) gives size 365 maximum RPM of 995 in Class I and 1,283 in Class II. Since \( 995 < 1{,}150 \le 1{,}283 \), the fan must be ordered Class II.
    2. Critical speed margin. The shaft is sized for a first critical speed of at least \( 1.43 \times 1{,}283 = 1{,}835 \) RPM, well above 1,150 RPM.
    3. Arrangement and temperature. If the customer wants Arrangement 4 on 160°F air, Table 1 limits Arrangement 4 to 180°F, so it is allowed; at 200°F it would not be.
    4. Certification. The BC-SW is listed for AMCA Sound/Air, so certified sound and air data can be taken from the same selection.
    5. What is not included. Add drive losses for a belt-driven arrangement and any appurtenance effects before choosing the motor.

    The software produced a number; the catalog turned it into an order line: BC-SW 365, Class II, at 1,150 RPM, in an arrangement and temperature the catalog permits.

    Software Computes, the Catalog Permits

    Think of Fan Selector and the catalog as two halves of one answer. The software tells you what speed and power a duty requires; the catalog tells you whether that speed, class, arrangement and temperature are allowed and what the numbers leave out. A selection is finished only when both halves agree.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • A Twin City Fan product catalog answers three kinds of question: can it be built, how will it perform, and what will it look like. Know which one you are asking.
    • Catalog structure is consistent: overview, construction, high temperature, arrangements, options, engineering data, material specifications, dimensions, typical specifications, and in some catalogs performance data. Catalog 300 puts BC performance in Fan Selector instead of tables.
    • Technical descriptions hold restrictions that tables omit. When FanPedia and the current catalog disagree, the current catalog wins; check the edition date on tcf.com.
    • Fan size labels one impeller diameter within a model; the performance page header gives the diameter, tip speed factor and outlet area. A model code names the line and impeller; model, size, class and arrangement must be checked together against the availability table.
    • The air change method and minimum conveying velocity supply airflow and duct velocity; fan family selection comes before size and depends on the airstream.
    • A performance table lists RPM and BHP by airflow and static pressure at standard air. Read the header and footer first, read the cell, use interpolation between rows and columns, and read the class from the class shading, which is a map of the class maximum speeds.
    • Stay in the fan selection range near the underlined maximum efficiency; compute static efficiency to compare cells and sizes.
    • Rating conditions are standard air, a stated installation type (B and D in Catalog 902), BHP without transmission losses, and no appurtenance effects. AMCA certified ratings apply only to what the certification statement names.
    • Convert actual operating conditions to standard air before entering a table, and size the motor for the cold start. Motor selection adds drive loss to BHP and goes to the next standard motor rating.
    • Catalog operating limits, first critical speed (at least 1.43 times class maximum speed), the AMCA 204 balance grade (BV-3 for BC) and the material specifications table describe what the fan is built to tolerate.
    • Product options carry catalog guidance on when to use them; Fan Selector software computes the point, and the catalog decides whether it is allowed.
