    Motor Ratings, Speed Control and Positioners¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter covers motor ratings and features beyond the basics: intended duty, premium efficiency, voltage and phase, synchronous speed, temperature rise, thermal protection, and winding RTDs; severe duty, hazardous location, and inverter-duty motors; and variable frequency drives for speed control. It then covers how motors are mounted and adjusted: slide bases, pivot bases, post mount bases, and bi-directional, tri-directional, and jack-screw motor positioners. After completing it, readers can specify a motor's ratings and mounting for a belt-driven fan.

    The Rest of the Motor Line

    A motor line on an order is short, but every field on it decides whether the fan starts, survives its environment and stays aligned. By the end of this chapter you will be able to write that line for a belt-driven fan, choose how the motor is mounted and adjusted, and explain each choice to a customer or a plant electrician. Let's follow the airflow.

    Chapter 14 introduced the motor itself: induction and EC technology, horsepower, enclosure, frame size, service factor, insulation class and efficiency class. This chapter finishes the job. FanPedia gives a typical motor specification as a single line (FanPedia p. 71):

    50 HP, 1800 RPM, IN, P, 3 Phase, 60 Frequency, 460 Volt, SD, TEFC, 326T

    You already know how to read the horsepower, the technology code (IN for induction), the enclosure (TEFC) and the frame (326T). The remaining fields are the subject of the first half of this chapter: the speed (1800 RPM), the efficiency designation (P for premium), the electric power (phase, frequency and voltage), and the intended duty (SD for severe duty). Along the way we add the ratings and protective devices that keep a motor from overheating, and the variable frequency drive that lets a fixed-speed motor run at other speeds. The second half turns to the hardware that holds the motor on a belt-driven fan: slide bases, pivot bases, post mounts and positioners.

    Synchronous Motor Speed¶

    An induction motor's stator windings create a magnetic field that rotates around the motor. The speed of that rotating field is the synchronous motor speed, and it depends on only two things: the frequency of the power supply in hertz (Hz, cycles per second) and the number of magnetic poles wound into the stator. Poles always come in pairs, so a motor has 2, 4, 6 or 8 poles, and so on.

    \[ n_s = \frac{120 \times f}{p} \]

    where:

    • \( n_s \) is the synchronous speed in revolutions per minute (RPM)
    • \( f \) is the supply frequency in Hz
    • \( p \) is the number of poles

    A 4-pole motor on 60 Hz power runs its field at \( 120 \times 60 / 4 = 1800 \) RPM, which is the "1800 RPM" in the FanPedia example. The same motor on 50 Hz power would run at 1500 RPM. FanPedia lists the typical synchronous speeds by frequency (FanPedia p. 71):

    Poles 60 Hz 50 Hz
    2 3600 RPM 3000 RPM
    4 1800 RPM 1500 RPM
    6 1200 RPM 1000 RPM
    8 900 RPM 750 RPM

    The rotor of an induction motor always turns a little slower than its field. That small difference, called slip, is what induces current in the rotor and produces torque. This is why a motor ordered as "1800 RPM" shows a somewhat lower full-load speed on its nameplate, and FanPedia notes that actual nominal motor speeds vary by motor horsepower and manufacturer. On an order, the motor speed is stated as the synchronous speed; the fan selection uses the actual operating speed.

    Motor Voltage and Phase¶

    The electric power line of a motor specification has three parts. Phase is single phase (one alternating voltage, common in small commercial equipment) or three phase (three alternating voltages offset in time, the standard for industrial motors). Frequency is 60 Hz in North America and 50 Hz in much of the rest of the world. Voltage is the nominal supply voltage at the motor terminals. The fan industry generally uses AC power for induction motors, and DC power for EC motors and some light commercial accessories and damper motors (FanPedia p. 72).

    FanPedia lists the common combinations (FanPedia p. 72):

    Power Phase Frequency Voltages
    AC Single 60 Hz 115, 230 or 277
    AC Single 50 Hz 220 or 240
    AC Three 60 Hz 200, 208, 230, 400, 440, 460, 575, 2300, 4160 or 4300
    AC Three 50 Hz 190, 380, 415 or 4000
    DC – – 12, 24, 28, 36, 48, 72, 90, 150, 180, 240, 300 or 500

    Many motors are dual voltage, such as 115/230, 208/230 or 230/460, and can be wired for either. That flexibility creates a trap on orders with electrical add-ons, which the next note explains.

    A Dual-Voltage Motor Still Needs One Voltage

    It is tempting to write "230/460 V" on an order and let the field decide, but a disconnect switch or speed controller supplied with the fan is built for one voltage. FanPedia says the customer needs to state the actual voltage the fan will use whenever the motor has add-ons (FanPedia p. 72). Ask for the site voltage before you release the order, not after the equipment arrives.

    Premium Efficiency Motor¶

    Motor efficiency is the ratio of the useful shaft power a motor delivers to the electrical power it draws; the difference leaves the motor as heat. Chapter 14 introduced efficiency classes in general. Twin City Fan uses two designations based on the NEMA classifications (FanPedia p. 72):

    • Standard Efficiency, which compares to NEMA High Efficiency (and to IEC IE2)
    • Premium Efficiency, which compares to NEMA Premium Efficiency (and to IEC IE3)

    A premium efficiency motor is therefore the "P" in the example line: a motor built with more and better active material (copper, electrical steel) so that less of its input becomes heat. FanPedia also notes that IE1 is the IEC standard-efficiency level and that IE4 (super premium) is a future designation not currently used.

    Efficiency also depends on how heavily the motor is loaded. FanPedia states that most motors are designed to run at 50% to 100% of rated load, that maximum efficiency is usually near 75% of rated load, and that efficiency drops sharply below about 50% load. A 10 HP motor therefore has an acceptable load range of 5 to 10 HP, with peak efficiency near 7.5 HP (FanPedia p. 72). An oversized premium motor running at 30% load can waste more energy than a correctly sized standard motor.

    Worked example: what does "P" buy? A belt-driven fan needs 20 brake horsepower (bhp) at its duty point and runs 6,000 hours a year. The selection uses a 25 HP motor, so the motor runs at \( 20 / 25 = 80\% \) load, inside the 50% to 100% band and close to the peak-efficiency point. For this illustration, assume the standard efficiency motor is 92.4% efficient at this load and the premium motor is 93.6% (illustrative values; real values come from the motor data sheet).

    1. Convert the shaft power to kilowatts: \( 20 \times 0.7457 = 14.91 \) kW.
    2. Electrical input, standard motor: \( 14.91 / 0.924 = 16.14 \) kW.
    3. Electrical input, premium motor: \( 14.91 / 0.936 = 15.93 \) kW.
    4. Difference: \( 16.141 - 15.934 \approx 0.207 \) kW (steps 1 to 3 are rounded for display; carry three decimals here, from 14.914 kW).
    5. Annual energy saved: \( 0.207 \times 6000 \approx 1{,}240 \) kWh per year.

    At an illustrative $0.10 per kWh, that is about $124 a year for one fan. The number is small per fan but recurs every year of the motor's life, and it scales with horsepower and run hours. That is why premium efficiency is the usual request on continuously running fans and why many specifications require it outright.

    Heat: Temperature Rise, Thermal Protection and Winding RTDs¶

    Every watt a motor loses becomes heat in its windings, and heat is what ages motor insulation. Three related ideas describe and control that heat.

    Motor Temperature Rise¶

    Motor temperature rise is the change in temperature from the ambient air around the motor to the motor's steady-state operating temperature (FanPedia p. 69). The rise is a property of the motor design and its load; the ambient is a property of the site. Add them and you get the winding temperature, which must stay within the limit of the insulation class from Chapter 14. FanPedia notes that the typical fan motor is built with Class F insulation but rated for a Class B temperature rise. The motor therefore runs cooler than its insulation could tolerate, and that margin is what lets it survive a hot day, a dirty cooling fan, or a period on the service factor. When a motor sits in a hot location, the ambient temperature rating (the maximum allowable temperature around the motor) has to be checked along with the rise.

    Motor Thermal Protection¶

    Motor thermal protection is a device built into the motor that responds to winding temperature. FanPedia lists thermistors and thermostats as the common choices and says they protect the motor against locked-rotor conditions, continuous overload and high ambient temperature (FanPedia p. 75). A thermostat is a switch that opens at a set temperature; a thermistor is a resistor whose resistance changes sharply at a set temperature and is read by a relay. Either one gives the control circuit a signal to shut the motor down before the insulation is damaged. Do not confuse thermal protection with a space heater (also called a strip heater), which warms an idle motor to prevent condensation.

    Motor Winding RTD¶

    A motor winding RTD is a resistance temperature detector embedded in the motor windings to sense their heat rise (FanPedia p. 75). Where a thermostat or thermistor answers a yes-or-no question (too hot or not), an RTD reports a continuous temperature, because its electrical resistance changes in a known way with temperature. Plants with monitoring systems use winding RTDs to trend motor temperature, alarm at one value and trip at a higher one. RTDs are also available for motor bearings, and Chapter 16 covers bearing RTDs on the fan itself. Winding RTDs are a motor modification, so they must be called out on the order; they are more common on large induction motors in process plants.

    Match the Device to the Plant's Control System

    Before choosing between thermostats, thermistors and winding RTDs, ask one question: "What will this signal be wired to?" A plant with a monitoring system wants RTDs it can trend; a simple starter circuit only needs a switch that opens.

    Motor Intended Duty¶

    Motor intended duty describes the operating conditions a motor is built for, based on its surrounding environment (FanPedia p. 71). It is the "SD" in the example line, and it is separate from the enclosure: the enclosure says how the motor is sealed and cooled, while the intended duty says how well the whole motor (paint, seals, hardware, bearings, conduit box) stands up to its surroundings. FanPedia lists these duty designations (FanPedia p. 73):

    • GD (General Duty): general purpose motors used where the air around the motor is normal, clean and cool.
    • SD (Severe Duty): used where a severe duty motor is needed but IEEE 841 motors are not specified. Also called Mil/Chem duty or chemical duty.
    • IEEE 841: an industry standard for severe duty motors begun in 1986, used in process industries.
    • DFG: explosion proof enclosure motors used in hazardous locations.
    • Special basis: IEEE 45 motors for marine applications and washdown duty motors for food applications. IEEE 45 is not a motor-manufacturing standard: it is the IEEE series Recommended Practice for Electrical Installations on Shipboard, and Twin City Fan describes the resulting option as "IEEE 45 marine-duty motors for both above deck and below deck installations" (tcf.com marine page). Motor makers sell "IEEE 45 marine duty" motors as TEFC, usually IP56, with severe-duty construction. Because the ship's design practice itself recommends IEEE 841 motors for 10 to 500 hp (IEEE Std 45.1-2017, 13.2), a marine motor often carries IEEE 841 features too. Confirm the exact motor build and enclosure with Twin City Fan when a marine specification is involved.

    Enclosure and duty have to agree. The motor specifications page pairs them (FanPedia p. 73); the NEMA side of that table is summarized below.

    Enclosure Intended duties offered
    ODP GD
    TEFC GD or SD; IEEE 841 and IEEE 45 (marine, special basis) in the IP56 version
    EXPL (explosion proof) DFG
    TENV GD or SD

    Worked example: choosing the duty. Three inquiries arrive in one week.

    1. A school's air handler fan; the motor sits in a clean, cool mechanical room. Normal, clean, cool air is the definition of general duty, so the motor is GD, and an ODP or TEFC enclosure both fit.
    2. A chemical plant asks for a "chem duty" motor on a scrubber exhaust fan; its specification does not mention IEEE 841. Chemical duty is one of the names FanPedia gives for SD, so the motor is TEFC, SD.
    3. A refinery specification calls for "IEEE 841 motors on all rotating equipment." The duty is IEEE 841, which forces a TEFC (IP56) enclosure. Quoting a plain SD motor here would not meet the specification, even though both are severe duty.

    The next two sections look more closely at the two duties in this list that carry the most weight on industrial orders.

    Severe Duty Motor¶

    A severe duty motor is built to keep running where a general duty motor would corrode, wear or overheat early: around moisture, chemical fumes, dust, washdown or outdoor exposure. FanPedia uses "SD" for the general severe duty motor and lists Mil/Chem duty and chemical duty as names you will see for it (FanPedia p. 73). IEEE 45 is not another name for SD: the same page files it under the special-basis duties for marine applications, and pairs it with SD only in the TEFC IP56 row of its enclosure table. IEEE 841 is a stricter, published standard for severe duty motors in process industries; FanPedia names automotive, mining, paper and wood mills, and refining as typical users.

    The rule for choosing between them comes straight from FanPedia's definition: SD is used where a severe duty motor is required but IEEE 841 is not specified. That makes the decision a reading exercise, not an engineering judgment.

    Worked example: SD or IEEE 841? An automotive paint shop needs an exhaust fan. The motor will sit near the fan in air that carries paint mist and dirt, so general duty is out. Read the customer's specification:

    1. If it says "IEEE 841" anywhere in the motor section, quote an IEEE 841 motor (TEFC, IP56).
    2. If it says "severe duty," "mill and chemical duty" or describes the environment without naming IEEE 841, quote an SD motor (TEFC).
    3. If the specification is silent and the motor is in the dirty air, recommend SD and say why on the quotation.

    In this case the specification reads "TEFC, severe duty, premium efficiency," so the motor line is: TEFC, SD, P. Had the same plant's corporate standard required IEEE 841, the answer would change even though the air did not.

    Hazardous Location Motor¶

    A hazardous location is an area where flammable gases or vapors, or combustible dusts, may be present in amounts that can ignite. The U.S. National Electrical Code (NEC) classifies these areas by class (Class I for gases and vapors, Class II for dusts), by division (whether the hazard is present in normal operation or only under abnormal conditions) and by group (the specific family of gas or dust). The plant owner, not the fan supplier, determines the classification of each area.

    A hazardous location motor is a motor built and labeled for one of those classifications. In FanPedia's terms it is a DFG motor with an explosion proof (EXPL) enclosure: the internal portion of the motor is engineered to keep a motor arc or explosion from igniting a surrounding atmosphere that contains flammable dust or gas, while an external fan cools it (FanPedia p. 73). FanPedia names Groups D (gases), F and G (dusts) as the groups commonly used at Twin City Fan, and directs readers to the NEC for the full list of gases and dusts and their class and division ratings. Applications under the European ATEX directive require a special motor.

    Worked example: reading the classification onto the order. A food plant requests a fan for a room the owner's drawings mark as Class II, Division 1, Group G (combustible food dust).

    1. The area is hazardous, so the motor must be a hazardous location motor: enclosure EXPL, duty DFG.
    2. The motor nameplate must carry the same class, division and group the owner gave: Class II, Division 1, Group G. Copy those values from the owner's documents onto the order; do not choose them yourself.
    3. The motor is only part of the answer. The fan also needs a review for spark-resistant construction, covered in Chapter 20.

    An Explosion Proof Motor Does Not Make a Spark-Proof Fan

    A common mistake is to order a DFG motor and assume the fan is now safe for the hazardous area. The motor rating covers the motor's own arcs and heat; a rubbing impeller or a dragging belt can still strike a spark. Treat the motor classification and the fan's spark-resistant construction as two separate lines that both need an answer.

    Variable Frequency Drive¶

    A variable frequency drive (VFD) is an electronic controller placed between the power supply and an induction motor. It converts fixed-frequency AC power into AC power at a frequency it can adjust, typically from a small fraction of line frequency up to line frequency. Because synchronous speed is set by frequency, changing the frequency changes the motor speed in direct proportion, and with it the fan speed. On a belt-driven fan the sheaves still set the ratio between motor and fan speed; the VFD changes both together.

    FanPedia treats the VFD as one of the two usual ways to change fan performance in the field, alongside variable inlet vanes (FanPedia p. 170). Chapter 18 covers the mechanical flow-control devices; this section covers what the VFD does to the fan.

    Frequency Is the Speed Knob

    Notice that nothing about the motor's poles or the fan's sheaves changes on a VFD. The drive moves the one variable in \( n_s = 120 f / p \) that the plant normally treats as fixed, and the fan laws do the rest.

    The fan laws (developed fully in Chapter 25) say how a fan's performance changes with speed on a fixed system. With \( N \) the fan speed and the subscripts 1 and 2 for the old and new conditions:

    \[ \frac{Q_2}{Q_1} = \frac{N_2}{N_1} \qquad \frac{P_2}{P_1} = \left(\frac{N_2}{N_1}\right)^2 \qquad \frac{H_2}{H_1} = \left(\frac{N_2}{N_1}\right)^3 \]

    where \( Q \) is airflow (CFM), \( P \) is pressure (in. wg) and \( H \) is brake horsepower. On a VFD, the speed ratio equals the frequency ratio, \( N_2 / N_1 = f_2 / f_1 \).

    Worked example: turning a fan down to 45 Hz. A belt-driven fan is selected for 10,000 CFM at 2.00 in. wg static pressure, needing 8.0 bhp at full speed on 60 Hz power (illustrative values). The owner wants to run it at reduced airflow overnight, and the VFD is set to 45 Hz.

    1. Speed ratio: \( 45 / 60 = 0.75 \). A 4-pole motor's synchronous speed drops from 1800 to 1350 RPM, and the fan speed drops by the same 25%.
    2. Airflow: \( 10{,}000 \times 0.75 = 7{,}500 \) CFM.
    3. Static pressure: \( 2.00 \times 0.75^2 = 2.00 \times 0.5625 = 1.13 \) in. wg.
    4. Brake horsepower: \( 8.0 \times 0.75^3 = 8.0 \times 0.4219 = 3.38 \) bhp.

    A 25% cut in airflow cuts the fan's power by more than half. That cube relationship is the reason VFDs are so common on fans that spend long hours below full flow. Two limits keep the example honest. The fan laws assume the system resistance follows the fan along a system curve that passes through zero, so a system with a fixed pressure requirement will not fall off this steeply. And a VFD can drive a motor above 60 Hz, but the fan must never be run above the maximum safe speed listed in the current catalog; any speed increase starts with that check.

    Inverter-Duty Motor¶

    A VFD does not produce a smooth sine wave. It builds its output from rapid switched voltage pulses, and those pulses can stress motor insulation with voltage spikes and can induce currents in the motor shaft that discharge through the bearings. An inverter-duty motor is an induction motor designed and rated for operation on a VFD (the drive's output stage is called an inverter), with insulation and construction meant to withstand that power. The governing reference is NEMA MG-1 Part 31, which FanPedia cites when it notes that precautions may be needed to eliminate or reduce shaft currents imposed on a motor by a VFD (FanPedia p. 72).

    FanPedia lists the motor modifications that address shaft currents: insulated bearings, whose outer race is coated with insulating material, and a shaft grounding ring, internal or external, that gives the induced charge a path to ground around the bearings (FanPedia p. 75; see also Chapter 14). FanPedia also notes that a common service factor for a motor operated on a VFD is 1.0 (FanPedia p. 69), so the service factor margin should not be counted on when a fan runs on a drive.

    Worked example: will a TEFC motor stay cool at low speed? A TEFC motor is cooled by an external fan on its own shaft, so when the VFD slows the motor, the cooling air slows too. Take the fan from the previous example and turn the drive down to 30 Hz.

    1. Speed ratio: \( 30 / 60 = 0.5 \). The motor's cooling fan moves roughly half its normal air.
    2. Fan load: \( 0.5^3 = 0.125 \), so the motor delivers about 12.5% of its full-speed power.
    3. Because fan load falls with the cube of speed while cooling falls roughly in proportion to speed, a centrifugal fan's motor generally runs with less heat to remove than it has cooling for, even at low speed.

    The conclusion is not that any motor will do. It is that, for a fan, the main reasons to order an inverter-duty motor are the drive's voltage spikes and shaft currents, not low-speed heating; the heating case is more serious for loads that keep their torque at low speed. On the order, state that the motor will run on a VFD, request an inverter-duty rating, and decide with the customer whether insulated bearings or a shaft grounding ring are needed.

    Mounting the Motor on a Belt-Driven Fan¶

    A motor base is the structure that mounts the motor to the fan or to the fan base on a belt-driven fan. FanPedia describes it as the means for positioning the motor and adjusting belt tension during installation and maintenance, and lists the styles used depending on fan design and industry requirements: slide base or slide rails, post mount, bolt-on and various pivot styles (FanPedia p. 189). Belt tension is set by changing the center distance between the motor sheave and the fan sheave, so every style below is, at heart, a way to move the motor a controlled distance and then lock it in place.

    Slide Base¶

    A slide base is a motor base in which the motor bolts to a plate or frame that slides along a fixed base, with an adjusting screw that pushes the motor away from or toward the fan to set belt tension. FanPedia distinguishes two forms (FanPedia p. 76):

    • NEMA type slide base, used on small to large motors from 48 to 445 frame. It mounts on the fan base or on the fan pedestal. Typical mounting: Arrangements 1 and 3 (floor mounted or on a fan base); Arrangements 9, 9F, 9H and 9ST; plenum fans in Arrangements 1, 3, 3HS, 3VS and 3SM; and pedestal plug fans in Arrangements 1P and 9P.
    • Heavy-duty slide rails, two rails per motor, used on large to very large motors in the 440, 500 and 5000 frame series. They mount on a base, or on the pedestal for Arrangement 9H only.

    On some Arrangement 9 fans, Unistrut channels on the pedestal give the slide base a wider range of belt center adjustment (FanPedia pp. 76 and 85).

    Worked example: the motor from the FanPedia line. The example motor is a 50 HP, 326T frame. Suppose it drives an Arrangement 9 housed centrifugal fan.

    1. Check the frame against the NEMA slide base range: 326 lies between 48 and 445, so the NEMA type slide base fits.
    2. Check the arrangement: Arrangement 9 is on the slide base's typical mounting list.
    3. Result: NEMA type slide base on the pedestal.

    Now change the motor to a 5008S frame (one of FanPedia's large-horsepower special sizes, FanPedia p. 74) on an Arrangement 1 fan. The 5000 series is outside the NEMA slide base range, so the answer becomes heavy-duty slide rails on the base. A 449T motor lands in the same place: it is a 440-series frame but above the 445 limit of the NEMA slide base.

    Pivot Motor Base¶

    A pivot motor base carries the motor on a plate hinged at one edge, so the motor swings about the pivot point and belt tension is set by the swing rather than a straight slide. FanPedia's automotive pivot bases are used mostly in automotive applications, on Arrangement 9H pedestals, Arrangement 9SS swingout fans and, specially mounted on a fan base, Arrangement 3 (FanPedia p. 77). Because the pivot must sit on the correct side of the motor, motor location is restricted by rotation: for Arrangements 9H and 9SS, clockwise rotation goes with motor position L and counterclockwise with R; for Arrangement 3, clockwise goes with motor position Z and counterclockwise with W. Motor positions and rotation are covered in Chapter 12. A separate pivot/bolted adjustable base is used on larger motors, 254 to 445 frame, on tubular centrifugal and axial Arrangement 9 fans, plenum Arrangements 3HA and 3VA, and plug fan Arrangement 9 (FanPedia p. 78).

    Post Mount Motor Base¶

    A post mount motor base is an adjustable base in which the motor plate is carried on posts, typically threaded, so the motor can be moved along the posts to tension the belts. FanPedia's standard post mount is used on small motors, 48 to 215 frame, in a saddle mount or flat mount version, on the same fan types as the pivot/bolted design: tubular centrifugal and axial Arrangement 9, plenum Arrangements 3HA and 3VA, and plug fan Arrangement 9 (FanPedia p. 78). The two are a matched pair split by frame size: post mount up to 215 frame, pivot/bolted from 254 to 445. For tubeaxial, vaneaxial and centaxial Arrangement 9 fans, FanPedia also shows a bolt-on mount for 48 to 286 frame motors in a saddle mount.

    Motor Positioners¶

    A motor base sets belt tension, but the motor also has to be aligned: its shaft square to the drive and, on a coupled or sheave drive, in line with the mating shaft. A motor positioner is a threaded jacking screw mounted next to the motor that pushes the motor a controlled distance so it can be aligned. FanPedia also calls it a motor alignment jacking screw, defines the basic motor positioner as providing horizontal adjustment of the motor position in one direction (FanPedia p. 190), and lists positioners among the pedestal accessories used next to bearings or motors to aid alignment during assembly and testing (FanPedia p. 130). The bearing positioner in Chapter 16 is the same idea applied to fan bearings.

    FanPedia defines three more variants, each adding one direction of adjustment (FanPedia pp. 148 and 190):

    • A bi-directional positioner provides horizontal adjustment of the motor position in both directions of the horizontal plane, so the motor can be moved along and across the base.
    • Vertical jack screws thread through the motor feet, which the motor vendor drills and taps. Turning them lifts the motor so shims can be set under its feet to correct height and angle. The vertical jack screws are removed after the motor is shimmed.
    • A tri-directional positioner combines horizontal positioners with vertical jack screws, giving adjustment in both horizontal directions and vertically. Because it uses vertical jack screws, its motor feet are also drilled and tapped by the vendor, and those screws are likewise removed after shimming.

    The comparison below restates the four from the list above side by side.

    Positioner Directions of adjustment Motor feet drilled and tapped by vendor Removed after shimming
    Motor positioner Horizontal, one direction Not noted Not noted
    Bi-directional positioner Horizontal, both directions Not noted Not noted
    Vertical jack screws Vertical Yes Yes
    Tri-directional positioner Horizontal, both directions, plus vertical Yes Yes (the vertical jack screws)

    Tapped Motor Feet Start at the Motor Vendor

    Tri-directional positioners and vertical jack screws need motor feet that the motor vendor drills and taps, so they have to be on the motor order from the start. Adding them after the motor has shipped means reworking the motor. Put the positioner on the same line as the motor, not on a later accessory list.

    Putting the Motor Line Together¶

    Every section of this chapter adds one field to the motor line or one item to the mounting. A single example shows how they combine.

    Worked example: a complete motor description. A paper mill needs an Arrangement 9 housed centrifugal fan for a process exhaust. The selection calls for 21 bhp at the fan, and the fan will run on a VFD supplied by the mill. The mill's standard requires IEEE 841 motors, premium efficiency and winding RTDs, on 460 V, three-phase, 60 Hz power. The area is not classified hazardous.

    1. Power: 21 bhp needs a 25 HP motor; the motor runs near 84% load, inside FanPedia's 50% to 100% band. Because the motor is on a VFD, do not count on a service factor above 1.0.
    2. Speed: a 4-pole motor, 1800 RPM synchronous on 60 Hz; the belt drive sets the fan speed.
    3. Technology and efficiency: induction, premium efficiency (IN, P).
    4. Electric power: 3 phase, 60 Hz, 460 V, a single stated voltage.
    5. Duty and enclosure: IEEE 841, which requires TEFC (IP56).
    6. Frame: 284T, the NEMA frame for this horsepower and speed in a T-frame TEFC motor (confirm against the motor data sheet).
    7. Modifications: inverter-duty rating for VFD operation, winding RTDs, and a shaft grounding ring or insulated bearings as agreed with the mill.
    8. Mounting: 284 is within 48 to 445 and Arrangement 9 is on the list, so a NEMA type slide base on the pedestal.

    The motor line reads: 25 HP, 1800 RPM, IN, P, 3 Phase, 60 Frequency, 460 Volt, IEEE 841, TEFC, 284T, inverter duty, winding RTDs, shaft grounding ring; NEMA slide base.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Synchronous speed is \( n_s = 120 f / p \): 1800 RPM for a 4-pole motor on 60 Hz, 1500 RPM on 50 Hz; slip makes actual speed slightly lower.
    • State phase, frequency and one site voltage, even for a dual-voltage motor, whenever the fan ships with electrical add-ons.
    • Premium efficiency compares to NEMA Premium (IEC IE3); efficiency peaks near 75% load and falls off below 50%.
    • Fan motors typically have Class F insulation with a Class B temperature rise. Thermistors and thermostats trip on overload, locked rotor and high ambient; winding RTDs report a continuous temperature.
    • Intended duty: GD for clean, cool air; SD where severe duty is needed and IEEE 841 is not specified; IEEE 841 when specified; DFG explosion proof for the owner's NEC hazardous classification, which does not replace spark-resistant fan construction.
    • A VFD changes frequency and so speed: airflow follows the speed ratio, pressure its square, power its cube. Never exceed the catalog maximum safe speed.
    • Inverter-duty motors handle VFD voltage spikes and shaft currents (NEMA MG-1 Part 31); service factor on a VFD is commonly 1.0.
    • Bases: NEMA slide base 48 to 445 frame, heavy-duty slide rails for 440, 500 and 5000 series, standard post mount 48 to 215, pivot/bolted 254 to 445, automotive pivot bases with rotation-restricted motor positions.
    • Positioners align the motor in one direction, both horizontal directions, vertically (jack screws) or all three; vertical jack screws need vendor-tapped feet and come out after shimming.

    You Can Write the Whole Motor Line

    You can now take a duty point, a plant's power and its environment, and turn them into a complete motor description with its base and positioners. That line is one of the most frequently corrected parts of a fan order, and you know where every field comes from.
